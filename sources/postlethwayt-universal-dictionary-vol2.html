Postlethwayt — Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce, Vol. II (L–Z)

Author: Malachy Postlethwayt (1707–1767) Title: The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce, Vol. II Edition: Fourth edition, London: W. Strahan, J. and F. Rivington, J. Hinton, et al., 1774 Format: Folio, 236,020 lines (~10.7 MB OCR text) Raw text: Vol. II Part of: postlethwayt-universal-dictionary (combined entry covering both volumes)

Contents

Volume II contains the alphabetical entries from L through Z, preceded by a dedication to Sir Stephen Theodore Janssen, Baronet, Chamberlain of the City of London, and followed by a comprehensive “Material Articles” index covering both volumes. The alphabetical entries from A through K are in postlethwayt-universal-dictionary-vol1.

Dedication to Sir Stephen Theodore Janssen

Volume II is dedicated to Sir Stephen Theodore Janssen, Baronet, Chamberlain of the City of London, in a substantial essay — approximately 250 lines — that praises Janssen’s public services to trade and the revenue.

The prohibition of French cambrics: Janssen is credited with procuring an Act (around 1744–45) prohibiting the wearing of French cambrics. Before this, Britain paid at least £250,000 a year to France for cambrics; by prohibiting them and replacing them with English-made gauzes, blonds, and other home-made wares, the nation saved and gained a difference of half a million pounds per annum — which, over the twenty years from 1745 to 1765, amounts to ten millions of principal money. The prohibition met with ministerial opposition (the duty on French cambrics had been appropriated) but Janssen’s arguments prevailed. Postlethwayt urges that a similar prohibition on all foreign wrought silks — for which Britain was then paying above a million pounds a year including all smuggled goods — would save the nation twenty millions in ten years and give full employment to the Spitalfields manufacturers.

The reduction of tea duties: Janssen demonstrated that lowering the excise duty on tea would increase the revenue, against ministerial scepticism. Before the Act he procured, the combined excise and customs on tea had not exceeded £170,000 per annum, and the East India Company had imported not above 1,200,000 lbs weight of tea communibus annis. Since the Act, the Exchequer has received yearly from £400,000 to £700,000, and the Company has sold from three to four millions of pounds weight annually — importing and selling no less than sixty millions of pounds weight more than it would otherwise have done. The Company’s extraordinary clear profit from this, at one shilling per pound weight, amounts to three millions; the public revenue has been advantaged by Janssen above seven millions of money.

Intelligence before the Seven Years’ War: When Janssen was Lord Mayor of London in 1755, he wrote to the Duke of Newcastle (then Secretary of State) and Lord Anson (First Lord of the Admiralty) reporting that French ships bound for America were “doubly manned” — which he judged an indubitable proof that France intended war before those ships returned. He proposed seizing the homeward-bound French West India ships without formal declaration of war, “whereby we could not have at once less than eight thousand French sailors prisoners in England, which would be crippling the French navy during that whole war.” The Duke of Newcastle acknowledged in Council that he received this information and suggestion from the Lord Mayor of London; the measure was adopted, and Britain had the prophesied eight thousand French sailors prisoners before the close of the year 1755.

Alphabetical Entries: L through Z

L

LABOUR — opens Volume II immediately after the dedication. A substantial economic analysis — approximately 500 lines — drawn from French economic sources (the passage from Cantillon’s Essai is closely followed throughout):

On the natural price of labour: a labourer’s son from age 7 to 12 becomes an assistant to his father at tasks requiring no art or skill; if the father puts him to a trade, he loses his assistance during apprenticeship, and since the life of a man is commonly calculated at 10 or 12 years only, a tradesman’s wages must exceed a labourer’s in proportion to the expense and risk of his servitude. Those trades attended with danger (sailors, bell-founders, silver miners) earn proportionately more; where capacity, danger, and confidence all combine (pilots, skippers), wages are higher still.

On competition and equilibrium of labour supply: if two tailors in a village make all its clothes and one dies, the survivor may raise his price until a tradesman comes from another village; the number of tradesmen in a village “always proportions itself to the demand, or the work there is for them.” The same holds in market-towns and cities.

On the intrinsic price of commodities: the price of any thing intrinsically “seems to take in the quantity of land with regard to the goodness of the land, and the quantity of labour, with regard to the dearness of the labour.” A pound of flax wrought into Brussels lace employs the labour of one person for near 14 years; the steel spring regulating a good watch may sell at a price making the proportion of steel to workmanship as 1 to 1,000,000; the price of a bucket of water at the river is nothing, but carried into the street sells for one penny — “the measure of the labour of the water-carrier.”

On land and labour as the dual foundations of price: a sustained argument that “the price of any thing intrinsically is the measure of the land, and the labour that enters into its production.” Land, which produces all commodities, must necessarily maintain those who give those commodities their form by labour.

On property in land: it does not appear that Providence gave any man preferential right to the possession of land; “some of the most ancient titles we have any knowledge of, came by violence and conquest.” In Mexico, property in land is vested in Spaniards; in Jerusalem, in Turks. The Roman republic’s original two-acre allotment “soon after fell into few hands.” In all social arrangements, land naturally falls into the hands of a few; the role of the labourer, the slave, and the tradesman is to maintain themselves and their children from what the landlord’s estate can be made to yield.

LADING — See Bills of Lading, Charter-Parties.

LANCASHIRE — the county’s situation, its cotton and woollen manufactures, the ports of Liverpool and Lancaster, and the inland trade routes.

LANGUEDOC (France) — the province and its subdivisions, including Lauragais, Gevaudan, and Vivarais; the Canal du Midi (the Canal of Languedoc) as a commercial artery; the silk, wool, and grain trades; the commerce of Montpellier and Nîmes.

LAPLAND — divided into Danish, Swedish, and Russian Lapland; the commerce in reindeer, furs, fish, and whale oil; the routes of the Lapland trade.

LEAKAGE — an allowance of 12 per cent in the customs on the importation of wine, oil, and other liquids, to compensate for loss in transit.

LEGHORN (Livorno) — an extended commercial and accounting entry, including a specimen double-entry merchant’s ledger for a Leghorn trading account. The accounts shown include:

  • Cash account — recording receipts from Thomas Dale of Leghorn for three several draughts, received freight from the Anne Galley (Captain Barnby) made from Genoa and Leghorn, and Geminiani’s freight per manifesto
  • Provision account for Corman of Lisbon (a salt account at Lisbon)
  • Commission account for a factor in Leghorn
  • Profit and Loss accounts for the several Leghorn ventures
  • Wheat Account of Messrs. Dale and Corman

The ledger also contains specimen accounts for John Kelly of London, John King of London, Peter Winberton of Smyrna, David Burrows, and other correspondents — constituting a practical guide to merchant accounting in the Mediterranean trade.

LEVANT TRADE — the trade to Turkey, Syria, and the eastern Mediterranean; the Levant Company and its privileges; the terms on which British merchants reside and trade in the Ottoman empire (liberty of religion guaranteed); the articles of commerce: raw silk, cotton, drugs, and dyestuffs.

LINEN — a major entry — approximately 700 lines — covering the linen trade and its statutory regulation. The trade in Irish linen and its statutory framework; the rules governing imported linens from Germany, Flanders, Holland, and France; the comprehensive legislative history from William III through George II:

Statutory framework: the Anne statutes on linens, silks, and printed calicoes; § 70 of 7 Ann. c. 7 (duties on European linens, gilters, and lawns); the series of Acts from 12 Ann. stat. 2 through 4 Geo. II c. 16 regulating chequered linens, printed goods, and Irish linen importation into Great Britain. The regulations for the linen manufacture in France: all cloths manufactured for export must pass before sworn examiners; thin, ill-woven, or ill-bleached goods are condemned; candidates who wish to export must mark their goods in the weaving and produce authentic certificates of exportation; partnerships of manufacturers joined in fair partnership may export jointly.

LINCOLNSHIRE — its fens and marshes, its wool and grain trade; Lindsey, the third and largest division of the county.

M

MARITIME AFFAIRS — an extended compendium of international maritime law, drawing on the most celebrated French, Dutch, Spanish, and Hanseatic sources. Covers: the laws governing masters and pilots; French ordinances on maritime conduct; the rules of the Dutch sea-law; Spanish ordinances; the Hanseatic statutes; rights and duties of pilots; liabilities of masters in relation to owners and cargo; the law of salvage; regulations for ports and harbours.

MERCHANTS — various entries on the constitution and practice of merchant trading, including the rules for merchant factors in foreign ports and the regulation of the commodity brokers.

N

NAVIGATION — Of the practical art of navigation in relation to ship-sailing. Postlethwayt sets out what a complete treatise of the art ought to contain, according to Sir William Petty’s 28-point programme:

  1. Arithmetic in whole numbers, fractions, decimals, and logarithms
  2. Vulgar, practical, mechanical geometry performable by scale and compass
  3. Trigonometry, right-lined and spherical
  4. The stars to be known
  5. Instruments for use at sea, their construction and use
  6. The whole skill of the magnet and its directive virtues
  7. The hydrography of the globe, the perspective of coasts, the description of the under-water-bottom of the sea
  8. The knowledge of winds and meteors
  9. The history and skill of all sorts of fishings
  10. The art of medicine and surgery peculiar to the sea
  11. The common laws of the Admiralty and jurisdiction of the sea
  12. The several victuallings and clothings fit for seamen
  13. The whole science of ebbing and flowing, and of currents and eddies
  14. Dromometry, and the measures of a ship’s motions at sea
  15. The building of ships of all sorts, with the several riggings and sails
  16. Naval economy according to the several voyages and countries
  17. The art of conning, rowing, and sailing all sorts of vessels
  18. The gunnery, fireworks, and other armatures peculiar to sea and sea fights
  19. The art of loading and unloading the chief commodities to best advantage
  20. The art of weighing up sunken ships and goods, and of diving for sunken goods in deep water
  21. The general philosophy of the motion and figures of the air, the sea, and seasons; of timber, iron, hemp, brimstone, tallow, and their uses in naval affairs
  22. An account of five or six of the best navies of Europe, with their arsenals, magazines, yards, and docks 23–28. An account of all shipping, of all chief commercial parts of the world, of the chief sea-fights and naval expeditions of the last century, of the use of telescopes, the depths of the sea, and the art of making sea-water fresh and potable

Postlethwayt notes he is not acquainted with any book written upon navigation “according to this extensive plan proposed by Sir William Petty,” and recommends the work of Mr J. Robertson, F.R.S., mathematical master at Christ’s Hospital, London — from which he extracts the section on a ship’s reckoning: the account whereby, at any time, it may be known where the ship is and on what course to steer to gain her port.

NETHERLANDS (Austrian) — the Austrian Netherlands and their commerce; the port of Ostend.

O

OSTEND — a city and port town in the Austrian Netherlands in the province of Flanders, situated 12 miles from Bruges.

OSTEND EAST-INDIA COMPANY — Emperor Charles VI attempted to establish an East India company at Ostend in 1718 but was compelled to desist by the English and Dutch. Postlethwayt reproduces the full Memorial presented by the Dutch resident at Brussels (M. Peeters, on behalf of the States-General) to the Marquis de Prie, minister plenipotentiary for the Austrian Netherlands, remonstrating against the Ostend voyages and their interference with the exclusive grants of the Dutch East India and West India companies confirmed by the Treaty of Münster. The company was eventually suspended by the Emperor following the Treaty of Vienna (1731) as the price of the Pragmatic Sanction — “it was the ruin of the late Ostend East-India Company that gave birth to the establishment of the Swedish East-India Company.”

P

PILOTAGE / PILOTS — a comprehensive legal entry on the office of pilot, drawn from French ordinances and English law: a pilot coming aboard a ship where there is already a pilot must not interfere; a pilot undertaking to conduct a vessel while under a compulsory pilotage requirement is liable if he runs the ship aground through ignorance; no master or mariner shall exact greater sums from merchants than those fixed by the ordinances; if they discover any alteration in the channel they are required to give notice; it shall be free for masters of French ships to take pilots from any nation.

PORTUGAL — the kingdom of Portugal, its northern border with Galicia; its geography, products, and commerce; the trade in wine (Oporto and Lisbon), cork, salt, olive oil, and fruits; the trade with Brazil; the Methuen Treaty and its consequences for the English cloth trade; the Lisbon merchants and the English factory there; the salt trade from Setúbal and its importance to the Newfoundland fishery.

PRUSSIA — the kingdom of Prussia, divided into Royal Prussia and Ducal Prussia; the commerce of Danzig; the Baltic grain and timber trade; the export of rye, wheat, hemp, flax, and pot-ash.

R

RUSSIA / MUSCOVY — Russia bounded on the north by the Arctic Ocean; its vast extent; the commerce of Moscow, St Petersburg, Archangel, Narva, and Riga; the staple exports: hemp, flax, iron, tar, pitch, potash, tallow, furs, and leather; the White Sea route and the Baltic routes; the economic transformation under Peter the Great.

RUSSIA COMPANY — the company first projected towards the end of Edward VI’s reign, executed in the first and second years of Philip and Mary, and confirmed by Act of Parliament under Elizabeth in 1566. Its origin in three vessels sent to discover a north-east passage to China, which fell into the White Sea and made up to the port of Archangel; the warm reception by the Muscovites and the resulting petition for letters patent. The charter of 1555 (under Mary) declared the association a body politic under the name “Company of Merchant-Adventurers of England for the Discovery of Lands, Territories, Islands unknown or unfrequented”; confirmed by Elizabeth in 1566 as “the Company of English Merchants for Discovering New Trades.” Its exclusive privilege of trading to Archangel and other ports of Muscovy; the requirement to use only English ships and to export cloths only after they have been dyed and dressed. The company subsisted with reputation for almost a century until the Civil Wars, when the Czar, hearing of the execution of Charles I, expelled all English subjects from his states; the Dutch seized the opportunity and settled in their place. After the Restoration, the company partially re-established its Archangel trade but never with its former vigour.

S

SALT — a commodity of general utility in commerce; the production of salt in England (Cheshire, the sea-salt trade), France, Spain, and Portugal; its vital importance for the preservation of fish and provisions; the regulatory framework governing salt duties and the allowance of duty-free salt to fishing vessels.

SALT-PETRE — a salt, though extracted out of earth; the sources and manufacture of salt-petre; its indispensable role in the manufacture of gunpowder; the East India Company’s supply from Bengal.

SHIPPING — the dimensions, tonnage, and number of guns of men-of-war built in Spain, the Indies, France, England, and Genoa, drawn from Don Geronimo Uztáriz’s Theórica y Práctica de Comercio y de Marina (1724). The building regulations prescribed by Lieutenant-General Don Antonio Gastaneta and approved by the Spanish Crown in 1713 and 1720. The crew complements of Spanish men-of-war: 26 sailors and 26 marines per 100 tons for common ships, 28 of each for the admiral’s ship, plus 100 additional hands for the flagships. The rules of the French, English, and Dutch navies for the manning of their respective fleets are given separately and compared.

SIBERIA / ASIATIC RUSSIA — formerly denominated Scythia; the extent and commerce of Siberia; the fur trade; the overland routes to China.

SILK / SILK-WORMS — two substantial entries:

Silk manufacture of China: a survey of Chinese silk production and its extraordinary cheapness; the reasons all European nations are attracted to the Chinese trade; the methods of silk cultivation in China and how they might be imitated in Europe.

Silk-worms: a memoir upon the breeding of silk-worms in France and all other climates where mulberry trees can be cultivated, by M. de Goyon de la Plombanie. Based on observations made in Languedoc, the memoir argues that the methods commonly followed do not answer the end proposed: in the Indies and eastern climates where worms breed on trees in the open air, the silk is very fine, very strong, and in great abundance; with us, art must correspond with nature. The memo details the causes of failure in French silk breeding: cold mornings that benumb the worms, impure air from confined chambers, variety of food from different soils, leaves gathered wet or faded, the pernicious effect of “people of stinking breaths” and “women at certain times” on these delicate animals. Instructions for a correct method are provided.

SPAIN — a major geographical and commercial entry: Spain situated on the most western part of the European continent; its provinces and their products; the commerce of Cadiz, Seville, Barcelona, and Bilbao; the colonial trade and the Casa de Contratación; the annual fleet to the Indies (the Tierra Firme and New Spain fleets); Spanish wool, wine, oil, and silk; the trade with the American colonies; the Treaties of Commerce with Great Britain; the post-1763 commercial situation.

SWEDEN — the kingdom bounded by the Baltic Sea, the Sound, and the Categat on the south, Norway on the west, Russian and Norwegian Lapland on the north, and Muscovy on the east. The soil, where capable of cultivation, is tolerably fruitful; Sweden imports many sorts of grain from Livonia for want of home supply. The cattle are small in size; sheep bear a coarse wool fit only for peasants’ clothing; forests of pine, fir, beech, birch, alder, and some oak overspread great part of the country. Iron mines and forges are in great number towards the mountainous parts: iron to the value of near £300,000 is exported yearly. The iron industry’s recent difficulties are noted: as the number of forges increased to the point where each undersold the others, and as the prohibition of foreign manufactures reduced the exchange market for iron, prices fell so low that “many thousands of poor people, whose livelihood depends upon these forges and mines, will be reduced to a starving condition.” Finland (part of the Swedish crown) produces pitch, tar, wooden ware, cattle, dried fish, and train-oil.

SWEDISH EAST-INDIA COMPANY — “It was the ruin of the late Ostend East-India Company that gave birth to the establishment of the Swedish East-India Company.” A succinct history of the Swedish company, treating it as a direct consequence of the closing of the Ostend route.

T

TOBACCO — the manner of planting and ordering tobacco in Virginia: the seed is sown in beds of fine mould in the twelve days of January; when plants have grown to the breadth of a shilling they are transplanted into hills about four feet apart, “somewhat after the manner of our hop-yards”; they are kept with diligent weedings; when the plant has put out as many leaves as the ground will nourish “to a substance and largeness that will render them merchantable,” the top is taken off (at 12–16 leaves in very rich ground, 9–10 in mean ground); the plant then puts out suckers which are plucked away once a week until the plant comes to perfection in August. In dry weather with a little breeze the ripe tobacco is cut down, let lie on the ground four hours until the leaves fall to the stalk, then carried to the tobacco-houses where it is hung on tobacco-stalks — “much after the manner they hang herrings in Yarmouth” — for five or six weeks until the stem in the middle of the leaf will snap. The tobacco is then stripped, bundled, and packed in hogsheads. The 1667 storm in Virginia that destroyed above half the standing tobacco crop and blew trees up by their roots throughout the country is recounted as a notable disaster to the trade. The entry continues with the statutory framework governing the tobacco trade: the import regulations, the customs duties, the rules for damaged and unmerchantable tobacco removed by water, and the drawback system.

TREATIES OF COMMERCE AND NAVIGATION — an extensive compendium of commercial treaties, supplementing the treatment given under the article MARITIME AFFAIRS. It opens with the full text of the Treaty of Navigation and Commerce between Great Britain (Queen Anne) and France (Louis XIV), concluded at Utrecht, 31 March 1713:

  • Article I: reciprocal and entirely perfect liberty of navigation and commerce through all kingdoms, states, dominions, and provinces of both parties in Europe
  • Article II: in case of rupture, subjects of each party residing in the other’s dominions shall have six months to retire with their families, goods, and effects, and shall enjoy good and speedy justice during that period; their persons and merchandise shall not be detained or troubled by arrest or seizure
  • Article III: no subject of either party shall exercise acts of hostility and violence against the other, by sea or land or in rivers, ports, or havens, under any colour or pretence whatsoever; no privateering commissions or letters of reprisal shall be issued against the other party’s subjects

Further treaties and commercial articles follow, covering the other major commercial agreements of the 18th century.

U

UNITED PROVINCES / STATES GENERAL — the seven United Provinces of the Netherlands and their commerce; the constitution of the States General; the pre-eminence of Holland and Zeeland in the Atlantic and Baltic trades; the Dutch East India Company and the West India Company; the Bank of Amsterdam.

W

WINE — the regulation and trade in wines; the customs and import duties; the statutory table of measure (the ton must contain 252 gallons for wine, oil, and honey); the Portuguese wine trade under the Methuen Treaty.

WOOL AND WOOLLEN MANUFACTORY — a major entry tracing the progress of woollen manufactures in France and Spain and showing how both nations have made “extraordinary progress” that “must necessarily tend to the greater and greater injury of” British woollen exports. The entry surveys the state of French wool-raising and woollen manufacturing in each of the French provinces; the Spanish wool trade (merino wool and its fine quality); the Biscayan, Castilian, and Catalonian weaving industries. The entry cross-references Biscay, Castille, Catalonia, Factors, and India-House of Spain.

WORCESTERSHIRE — has Herefordshire and Shropshire to the west; its commerce in gloves and metal wares.

WRECK — where a ship is broke to pieces or lost on the sea; the law of wreck; the rules governing goods cast ashore; the statutory provisions for preventing the plundering of wrecks; the rights of the lord of the manor versus the rights of the original owner.

Y

YARN, LINEN — See the end of letter I and the end of the article LINEN; the regulations on the importation of linen yarn from Ireland and the American colonies.

YORKSHIRE — by far the largest county in England; its woollen manufactures in the West Riding (Leeds, Halifax, Bradford); the commerce of Hull; the cloth market at Leeds and the system of clothiers bringing their undressed cloth to the open market on “Cloth Hall” days.

YEAST — a method of curing it; the flowers of wine and beer and their fermentative properties; a practical note for the brewer.

Z

ZAFFRE / SAFREE — a mineral of a bluish colour; its use in painting porcelain and glass blue; where obtained and in what quantities.

ZINCK / ZINCTHUM / MARCASITA PALLIDA — a metallic, sulphurous mineral; its properties and commercial uses in the manufacture of brass and other alloys.

Material Articles Index

Volume II closes with a comprehensive “Material Articles” index covering both volumes of the dictionary, organized by letter (Letter L through Letter Z, with letters for the first volume — A through K — given at the end). Each section provides condensed summaries of the most commercially important entries, directing the reader to the relevant full articles. The index sections include:

  • Letter L: LABOUR (remarks political thereon in relation to the natural price); LEAKAGE of liquors (allowance at the customs on importation)
  • Letter M: MERCHANTS and their various employments
  • Letter N: NOVOGOROD (its commerce)
  • Letter O: OAK (its nature, growth, and commercial uses)
  • Letter P: PACKERS (the nature of their employment)
  • Letter Q: QUACK, in medicine — “a short antidote against general deception”
  • Letter R: extensive entries on Russian commerce
  • Letter S: SABLE (curious skins of an animal, where had)
  • Letter T and beyond: VENICE (its situation, produce, and trade; the sovereign fund of the state revenues of Venice)
  • Letter W, X, Y, Z: condensed cross-references to the main entries

The index also includes the sections covering the first volume (Letters A through K), appended at the end, making the index in Vol. II a complete reference tool for the entire work.
