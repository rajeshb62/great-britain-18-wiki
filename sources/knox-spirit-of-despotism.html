Knox — The Spirit of Despotism

Vicesimus Knox, D.D. (1752–1821) — The Spirit of Despotism. — Written 1794–1795; first published anonymously in 1821; this edition: London, Reprinted for William Hone, 45, Ludgate Hill, 1822.

Vicesimus Knox was a distinguished English essayist, clergyman, and headmaster of Tonbridge School. A noted liberal and opponent of the French Revolutionary Wars, he wrote this treatise at the height of the Pitt government’s repressive campaigns against domestic radicalism. The work was suppressed by Knox himself — who doubted the safety of publication — and only appeared posthumously. William Hone, the radical publisher, brought it to print after discovering a stray copy at a London bookseller. An American edition had already appeared without either Knox’s consent or his name.

The text is 11,314 lines, OCR’d from a University of California (Riverside) copy of the 1822 Hone edition (call no. JC381 K56), 352 page-files.

Content Summary

The work is a sustained attack on what Knox calls “the Spirit of Despotism” — a disposition of mind and governance that he identified not only in absolute monarchies but as a growing influence within the British constitution itself, nourished by war, corruption, court influence, and the decay of public virtue.

Section I — Introductory

Man in a state of nature is capable of moral and intellectual improvement; bad government, above all despotism, corrupts him. Despotism is the grand adversary of human virtue and happiness. Under its influence, man degenerates from his natural dignity. Turkey, Italy, and Egypt are held up as examples of once-great civilisations reduced to ruin by despotic government. Fear becomes the predominant passion under uncontrolled power.

Section II — Oriental Manners, and the Ideas Imbibed in Youth, Both in the West and East Indies, Favourable to the Spirit of Despotism

The manners of the East — servility, ostentation, arbitrary power — are contagious. Englishmen who serve in India or the West Indies bring back habits of command and submission that are incompatible with English liberty. The Nabob returns home with Asiatic notions of government, and these infect English politics.

Section III — Certain Circumstances in Education, Which Promote the Spirit of Despotism

Education in England, especially in public schools, inculcates servility toward the great and contempt for the poor. Flogging, fagging, and deference to rank train boys for a despotic society. The classics are taught as instruments of pedantry rather than as lessons in liberty.

Section IV — Corruption of Manners Has a Natural Tendency to Promote the Spirit of Despotism

Luxury, venality, and the love of pleasure weaken the spirit of liberty. A people corrupted by luxury will sell its birthright for ease. The progress of corruption in manners parallels the progress of despotic power.

Section V — An Abhorrence of Despotism and an Ardent Love of Liberty Perfectly Consistent with Order and Tranquillity

Love of liberty is not the same as love of licence. The best citizens are those who value liberty most. A free people is not a disorderly people; on the contrary, the most orderly nations are those with the most liberty.

Section VI — On the Venality of the Press Under the Influence of the Despotic Spirit

The press, which ought to be the guardian of liberty, is corrupted by government patronage and subsidies. Journalists become hirelings of the ministry. The venal newspaper is a powerful engine for spreading the spirit of despotism under the forms of liberty.

Section VII — The Fashionable Invectives Against Philosophy and Reason, a Proof of the Spirit of Despotism

The ridicule of philosophers and reasoners is a tactic of despotic power. Those who cry down “French philosophy” or “speculation” are serving the interests of tyranny. Reason is the only safeguard against arbitrary power.

Section VIII — Of Loyalty, and Certain Mistaken Ideas of It

True loyalty is loyalty to the constitution, not to the person of the king. The flatterers of monarchy are its worst enemies. Passive obedience and non-resistance are doctrines incompatible with the British constitution.

Section IX — On Taking Advantage of Popular Commotions, Accidental Excesses, and Foreign Revolutions, to Extend Prerogative and Power

The French Revolution is being used as a pretext to enlarge executive power at home. Every popular disorder serves as an excuse for new restraints on liberty. The government exploits the fear of revolution to introduce despotic measures.

Section X — When Human Life Is Held Cheap, It Is a Symptom of a Prevailing Spirit of Despotism

Despotic governments value life lightly. War, cruel punishments, impressment, and the harsh Poor Laws all evidence a disposition to sacrifice human life to the convenience or ambition of the powerful.

Section XI — Indifference of the Middle and Lower Classes of the People to Public Affairs, Highly Favourable to the Encroachments of the Tory Principle

The apathy of ordinary people about politics is a standing invitation to despotism. When the people neglect their rights, power naturally accrues to the Crown and its agents. The Tory principle thrives on public indifference.

Section XII — The Despotic Spirit Is Inclined to Discourage Commerce, as Unfavourable to Its Purposes

Commerce creates an independent middle class; independence is the enemy of despotism. Hence despotic governments everywhere discourage or cripple trade. The merchant’s wealth and spirit make him a natural opponent of arbitrary power.

Section XIII — The Spirit of Despotism Displaying Itself in Private Life, and Proceeding Thence to Avail Itself of the Church and the Military

Despotism begins in private manners — the tyranny of the master over the servant, the landlord over the tenant, the parent over the child — and extends upward into the institutions of church and army. The Church is recruited to preach submission; the military to enforce it.

Section XIV — The Despotic Spirit Inclined to Avail Itself of Spies, Informers, False Witnesses, Pretended Conspiracies, and Self-Interested Associations

The Pitt government’s use of spies and informers, the prosecution of reformers for constructive treason, the Association for Preserving Liberty and Property against Republicans and Levellers — all are instruments of the despotic spirit, which invents conspiracies where none exist to justify its repressions.

Section XV — The Manners of Tory Courtiers, and of Those Who Ape Them, as People of Fashion, Inconsistent with Manliness, Truth, and Honesty

Courtiers are debased by flattery and self-interest. Their manners — servile to superiors, insolent to inferiors — spread through society as the “fashionable” mode. This corruption of manners undermines the character necessary for free citizenship.

Section XVI — The Spirit of Truth, Liberty, and Virtue, Public as Well as Private, Chiefly to Be Found in the Middle Ranks

The middle class is the great repository of public virtue. The nobility are corrupted by court favour, the poor by dependence; but the middle ranks preserve independence of judgment and attachment to liberty. The health of the constitution depends on their vitality.

Section XVII — On Debauching the Minds of the Rising Generation by Giving Them Military Notions in a Free and Commercial Country

The cult of military glory is a mark of despotic ambition. A standing army and military display are inconsistent with free government. The admiration of soldiers and conquerors debauches the public mind and prepares it for servitude.

Section XVIII — Levity, Effeminacy, Ignorance, and Want of Principle in Private Life, Inimical to All Public Virtue

The fashionable vices — dissipation, gambling, sexual licence, neglect of domestic duties — are not merely private failings but public dangers. A nation that lives for pleasure will not fight for liberty.

Section XIX — Certain Passages in Dr. Brown’s “Estimate” Which Deserve Serious Consideration

John Brown’s Estimate of the Manners and Principles of the Times (1757) is invoked as a prophetic warning. Brown had argued that England was sinking under corruption of manners; Knox finds his analysis still more urgent forty years on.

Section XX — On Several Subjects Suggested by Lord Melcombe’s “Diary”

The diary of George Bubb Dodington (Lord Melcombe) is examined for evidence of the systematic corruption of Parliament in the mid-18th century. The bartering of parliamentary seats for places and pensions is documented from a practitioner’s own testimony.

Section XXI — On Choosing Rich Men Without Parts, Spirit, or Liberality as Representatives

The election of wealthy nonentities to Parliament serves the interests of the Crown. Rich men are easily managed through patronage; poor men of ability are excluded by the expense of elections. The result is a legislature of placement, not representatives.

Section XXII — Of the Despotic Influence of Great Merchants Over Their Subalterns, Customers Over Their Tradesmen, and Rich Trading Companies Over Their Dependents

Commercial dependence is a vehicle of political control. Great merchants, contractors, and companies compel their dependents to vote for court candidates. The independence of elections is subverted by economic power as much as by direct bribery.

Section XXIII — Of the Pageantry of Life

Pageantry — coronations, royal progresses, titles, ceremonies — originates in the spirit of despotism and reinforces it. The love of show and distinction is a mark of slavish minds. True liberty needs no external decoration.

Section XXIV — Insolence of the Higher Orders to the Middle Ranks and the Poor

The contempt of the great for ordinary people is both a symptom and a cause of despotic manners. The “condescension” of the great, when it appears, is as offensive as their insolence. True equality of manners is the foundation of free government.

Section XXV — Of a Natural Aristocracy

Knox distinguishes between a natural aristocracy of virtue and talent and an artificial aristocracy of birth and wealth. The former is beneficial to society; the latter is the engine of despotism. The “natural aristocracy” exists in all ranks and is betrayed by its nominal possessors in the peerage.

Section XXVI — The Excessive Love of Distinction and Power Deadens the Finest Feelings

The desire for distinction overpowers the natural affections. Ambition hardens the heart against pity, justice, and truth. The great man who sacrifices everything to power becomes incapable of the ordinary virtues that make life worth living.

Section XXVII — On the Opinion That the People Are Annihilated or Absorbed in Parliament

The doctrine that the people have no voice except through Parliament and that the people’s rights are exhausted by representation is a sophism of despotic lawyers. The people retain an inalienable right to watch, remonstrate, and reform their government.

Section XXVIII — The Fashionable Contempt Thrown on Mr. Locke and His Writings in Favour of Liberty

Locke’s political writings, once the foundation of the Whig constitution, have fallen into disrepute among the fashionable and the powerful. This contempt for Locke is a measure of the progress of the despotic spirit.

Section XXIX — Of the Despotism of Influence, While the Forms of a Free Constitution Are Preserved

The most dangerous despotism is that which operates through influence rather than force. When the Crown can command majorities in Parliament by patronage and corruption, the forms of liberty remain but the substance is gone. This is the state of England in 1795.

Section XXX — The Spirit of Despotism Delights in War or Systematic Murder

War is the natural element of despotism. It provides pretexts for increased taxation, enlarged patronage, and the suspension of liberties. The war against revolutionary France is denounced as a war for the extension of despotic power abroad and its consolidation at home.

Section XXXI — On the Idea That We Have Arrived at Perfection in Politics

The claim that the British constitution is perfect and needs no reform is itself a symptom of the despotic spirit. All human institutions are improvable; to deny improvement is to defend abuse. The argument from perfection is a mask for the interests of the corrupt.

Section XXXII — On Political Ethics

Political ethics, as practised by the ruling class, is the art of concentrating power in the hands of the worst part of mankind. Government under the influence of the despotic spirit becomes an institution for enriching and aggrandising a few at the expense of the many.

Section XXXIII — On Trafficking with the Cure of Souls for the Purposes of Political Corruption

The sale of church livings, the translation of bishops for political services, and the use of ecclesiastical patronage to secure parliamentary influence are among the worst corruptions of the age. The church, which ought to teach virtue, is made an instrument of the despotic spirit.

Section XXXIV — Of Mr. Hume’s Idea That Absolute Monarchy Is the Easiest Death of the British Constitution

Hume’s famous argument that absolute monarchy would be a gentle euthanasia for the constitution is examined and rejected. There is no gentle death for liberty. The history of countries that lost their freedom shows that the transition is always painful.

Section XXXV — The Permission of Lawyers by Profession to Have the Greatest Influence in the Legislature

Lawyers, whose professional habits incline to technicality and precedent, are natural enemies of reform. A legislature dominated by lawyers is hostile to liberty. The legal profession is a nursery of the despotic spirit.

Section XXXVI — Poverty, When Not Extreme, Is Favourable to All Virtue

Moderate poverty is a school of virtue; enormous riches are a school of vice. The poor man, when not driven to desperation, is more honest, more industrious, and more public-spirited than the rich. Luxury, not poverty, is the great corrupter.

Section XXXVII — On the Natural Tendency of Making Judges and Crown Lawyers Peers

The translation of judges and law officers to the House of Lords compromises the independence of the judiciary. A judge who hopes for a peerage will be influenced in his judgments. The same applies to bishops translated for political service.

Section XXXVIII — All Opposition to the Spirit of Despotism Should Be Conducted with the Most Scrupulous Regard to the Existing Laws

Knox insists that reform must proceed within the law. Those who resist despotism must not themselves become despotic or violent. The law, even when abused, is the only safe foundation for liberty. The section is a warning against revolutionary excess.

Section XXXIX — The Christian Religion Favourable to Civil Liberty and to Equality Rightly Understood

Christianity, properly understood, is a religion of liberty and equality. It teaches the equal value of every human soul. The early church was a community of equals. The alliance of church and state has corrupted Christianity and made it an instrument of oppression.

Section XL — The Pride Which Produces the Spirit of Despotism Conspicuous Even on the Tombstone

The love of distinction follows men beyond the grave. Monumental inscriptions and elaborate tombs are expressions of the same spirit of pride and domination that produces despotism in public life. The humble gravestone is more honourable than the mausoleum of a tyrant.

Section XLI — Conclusion

Knox concludes by urging conciliation upon those in power. Let Parliament be reformed — this single measure will remove all grievances. The people must ultimately triumph; liberty will flourish all over Europe. The Spirit of Despotism must become the Spirit of Philanthropy.

Relationship to the Wiki

This is the most sustained contemporary critique of the Pitt government’s conduct during the French Revolutionary Wars from a liberal Anglican perspective. It complements the high-political narratives of the Belsham histories and the Burke pamphlets by providing the view from the reformist opposition. Knox writes as a mainstream clergyman and educator, not a radical — which makes his indictment of the “Spirit of Despotism” all the more striking. The work is a key source for the domestic political climate of the 1790s, the repression of reform movements, the debate on the French Revolution, and the constitutional arguments around Crown influence, parliamentary reform, and civil liberties.
