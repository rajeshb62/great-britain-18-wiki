Burnet — History of His Own Time, Vol. II

Gilbert Burnet (1643–1715), Bishop of Salisbury — Bishop Burnet’s History of His Own Time. Vol. II. From the Revolution to the Conclusion of the Treaty of Peace at Utrecht, in the Reign of Queen Anne. To which is added, The Author’s Life, by the Editor. — Printed for the Editor by Joseph Downing and Henry Woodfall, London, 1734.

This is Volume II of Burnet’s contemporary history, published posthumously in 1734. It continues directly from Vol. I (which ended with the Revolution of 1688–1689) and covers the reigns of William & Mary, William III alone, and Queen Anne — from the Revolution settlement to the Treaty of Utrecht (1713).

The volume contains 810 page-files from the 1734 first edition (New York Public Library copy), extracted as 47,271 lines (~1.2 MB) of plain text.

Scope note for the 17th-century wiki: Only Books V and VI (covering William & Mary and William III, 1689–1702) fall within the 17th century — roughly the first half of the volume. Book VII (Queen Anne, 1702–1713) is early 18th century.

Book-by-Book Summary

BOOK V — William & Mary (line 2,450)

Covers 1689–1694. Burnet begins writing on 1 May 1705, looking back on the reign. Key topics:

  • The Revolution settlement — the Convention Parliament, the Declaration of Rights, the offer of the crown
  • The establishment of William and Mary
  • The first parliament under the new sovereigns — the Bill of Rights, the Toleration Act, the Mutiny Act
  • The Jacobite resistance — the first Jacobite rising (1689–1690): Dundee’s rising in Scotland, Killiecrankie, the defence of Londonderry, the siege of Enniskillen
  • The War of the Grand Alliance (Nine Years’ War) begins — England joins the coalition against Louis XIV
  • The Battle of the Boyne (1690) — William’s victory in Ireland, James II’s flight to France
  • The Battle of Beachy Head (1690) — the Anglo-Dutch naval defeat by the French
  • The Battle of La Hougue / Barfleur (1692) — the decisive Anglo-Dutch naval victory, the destruction of French naval power
  • The Massacre of Glencoe (1692) — Burnet’s account of the Scottish tragedy
  • The establishment of the Bank of England (1694) and the financial revolution
  • The death of Queen Mary (Dec 1694) — Burnet’s poignant account of her final illness and her reputation
  • Burnet’s own role: appointed Bishop of Salisbury, his involvement in ecclesiastical affairs, his relations with the court

Burnet was a close confidant of both William and Mary. His account of Mary’s death is particularly moving — he attended her in her final hours. He also provides detailed insider accounts of the ecclesiastical settlement, the disputes between High Church and Low Church parties, and the negotiations with the Nonjurors (clergy who refused to swear allegiance to William and Mary).

BOOK VI — William III (line 9,571)

Covers 1695–1702. The period after Mary’s death, with William ruling alone. Key topics:

  • The funeral of Queen Mary — the unprecedented attendance of both Houses of Parliament
  • The parliamentary inquiry into naval miscarriages (1695)
  • The recoinage crisis — the reform of the currency under Somers and Montagu
  • The Treason Trials Act (1696) — reform of treason procedure
  • The Assassination Plot (1696) — a Jacobite conspiracy to kill William, the discovery and trials
  • The Peace of Ryswick (1697) — the end of the Nine Years’ War, the terms, Louis XIV’s recognition of William as king
  • The first Partition Treaty (1698) and the question of the Spanish succession
  • The standing army controversy — the parliament’s demand to disband the army in peacetime
  • The Second Partition Treaty (1700) and the death of Charles II of Spain
  • The death of the Duke of Gloucester (1700) — the last surviving child of Princess Anne, precipitating the succession crisis
  • The Act of Settlement (1701) — the Hanoverian succession established, the limitations on the crown
  • The exiled Jacobite court at St Germain and James II’s death (1701)
  • The Grand Alliance renewed (1701) — the War of the Spanish Succession begins
  • The death of William III (March 1702) — his last illness, his character

Burnet’s account of the Partition Treaties and the Spanish succession negotiations is particularly valuable — he was involved in the diplomatic discussions. His portrait of William III in old age, the king’s declining health, his lonely determination, and his final days is drawn from personal observation.

BOOK VII — Queen Anne (line 18,269)

Covers 1702–1713. The reign of Anne and the War of the Spanish Succession. Key topics:

  • The accession of Anne (March 1702) — her first speech, her promise to follow William’s footsteps
  • The beginning of the War of the Spanish Succession — the Grand Alliance against France and Spain
  • The Duke of Marlborough’s campaigns — the great victories: Blenheim (1704), Ramillies (1706), Oudenarde (1708), Malplaquet (1709)
  • The naval war — Sir George Rooke’s capture of Gibraltar (1704), the Battle of Málaga (1704), the capture of Barcelona (1705), the capture of Minorca (1708)
  • The Union of England and Scotland (1707) — Burnet was a Scottish bishop and gives a detailed account of the negotiations, the arguments for and against, and the final passage of the Act of Union
  • The party conflicts — the struggles between Whigs and Tories, the Godolphin ministry, the fall of the Whig junto
  • The Sacheverell trial (1710) — the high church狂热, the riots, the political fallout
  • The fall of Marlborough and the Harley-St John ministry (1710–1714)
  • The Peace of Utrecht (1713) — the terms: Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, the Asiento, the recognition of the Protestant succession
  • The Hanoverian succession — the arrival of the Electress Sophia’s son
  • The concluding reflections on the state of Britain

Burnet was a Whig bishop and his account of Queen Anne’s reign reflects Whig perspectives. He was deeply involved in the Union negotiations and provides one of the best contemporary accounts of how the Union was achieved — the bribery, the arguments, the Scottish resistance and eventual acquiescence. His account of the Sacheverell affair and the Tory reaction after 1710 has the quality of a participant watching his cause collapse.

Relevance to England 17th Century Wiki

Partial. Books V and VI (lines 2,450–18,268, roughly the first half of the volume) cover William & Mary and William III (1689–1702), which is still within the 17th century. This includes the Nine Years’ War, the financial revolution (Bank of England), the Jacobite threats, the Act of Settlement, and the immediate post-Revolution settlement. Book VII (Queen Anne, 1702–1713) is early 18th century and primarily relevant for the Union of 1707 and the War of the Spanish Succession.

Burnet remains a primary contemporary source throughout — he lived through all of these events as a bishop and courtier.
