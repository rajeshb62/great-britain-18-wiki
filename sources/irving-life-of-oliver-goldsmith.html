Completeness for this edition

Yes — for this A. L. Fowle 1900 two-volume packaging, Vol. I + Vol. II together are the complete Life of Oliver Goldsmith, with Selections from His Writings. Vol. I holds the full biographical narrative and the first block of selections; Vol. II continues and closes the selection programme (Citizen later letters + final miscellany). That is not every work Goldsmith wrote (no full Vicar of Wakefield text, no complete Bee, no full Citizen letter-numbering of the original serial, no History of England / animated nature, &c.): it is the complete Irving “Life + Selections” dual volume as printed by Fowle.

Volume I — Biographical Sketch

Continuous prose life (no numbered chapters in this imprint). Running headers “OLIVER GOLDSMITH” carry the narrative from birth through death, monument, and character.

Biographical Sketch

Irving opens by arguing that few writers win such personal kindness from readers as Goldsmith: the artless benevolence, whimsical yet amiable views of life, unforced humour dashed with melancholy, and mellow style seem to bespeak the man as well as the author. Private biography, he says, unlocks the pages—most characters and adventures are transcripts of Goldsmith’s own parti-coloured story; many ludicrous scenes are drawn from his own blunders; he was “buffeted into almost every maxim” he teaches.

Birth and family. Oliver Goldsmith is born 10 November 1728 at the hamlet of Pallas, county of Longford, Ireland, of a respectable but unthrifty stock that hands down “virtue and poverty.” His father, the Rev. Charles Goldsmith, marries young and poor, starves on a country curacy, then by a wife’s uncle’s death succeeds to the rectory of Kilkenny West. Popular tradition people the deserted Pallas house with fairies and a hobgoblin in jack-boots. Family life and the village schoolmaster (the wandering, superstitious Byrne, campaigning stories and fairy lore) feed the boy’s passion for romance, rogues, and adventure—plants of useful knowledge “overrun by the weeds of his quick imagination.” Henry, the elder brother, and the household economy later colour the Vicar of Wakefield and Deserted Village Auburn.

Trinity College, Dublin. Entered as a sizer (“poor scholar”) because the family can no longer afford a pensioner’s place: menial college duties, coarse black gown without sleeves, plain cap without tassel. Irving stresses the wound of distinctions that attach degradation to poverty. Fellow-student Edmund Burke is noted; Goldsmith’s college course is irregular, marked by scrapes, generosity, and limited academic glory. His father’s death deepens dependence on relatives.

Edinburgh, Leyden, and the philosophic vagabond. Sent to Edinburgh for medical study, he loses his way to lodgings on the first night and endures a landlady’s weekly rotation of one loin of mutton. He joins a medical society, falls into idle convivial habits, and plans Leyden. Letters paint Dutch costume and thrift; continental wandering (on foot, with flute and disputations) becomes the storehouse for The Traveller and the “Philosophic Vagabond” of the Vicar. Attempts at a medical degree, foreign universities, and a never-stable profession trail him homeward toward London.

London hack work. Grub Street years: ushering, proof-reading, and finally engagement by Mr. Griffiths of the Monthly Review (April 1757)—daily drudgery, copy altered by Griffiths and his literary wife, broken after months. John Newbery of St. Paul’s Churchyard becomes a real friend of authors (later caricatured affectionately in the Vicar). Essays for magazines, the Bee, Chinese letters, and children’s and compilation work keep him barely afloat. Credulous generosity (the white-mice / pledged-watch trick that suggests Prince Bonbennin in the Citizen) marks the man Irving loves.

Johnson, the Club, and reputation. Personal acquaintance with Dr. Johnson from a literary supper in Wine-office Court (31 May 1761); honourable mention of Johnson in the Bee and Chinese Letters had already won goodwill. Boswell appears as a pushing young Scot whose later gossip damages Goldsmith’s fame. Friendship with Reynolds, membership in the circle that becomes the Literary Club, chambers in the Temple, and the social awkwardness of a man of genius who “wrote like an angel, but talk’d like poor Poll” (Garrick’s epitaph joke) structure the middle Life.

The Traveller, the Vicar, and public fame. Publication of The Traveller; or, A Prospect of Society elevates him at a stroke: wits who had treated him as a bookseller’s drudge receive him as a peer; a canard that Johnson wrote the finest lines is defeated when Johnson marks only nine inserted lines near the end. Chambers in the Temple follow. The manuscript of the Vicar of Wakefield, long slumbering with Francis Newbery (nephew, not the uncle John—Irving corrects the common mistake), is hurried into print on the poem’s success. Medical practice among friends ends in a quarrel with an apothecary and Beauclerk’s joke: “whenever you undertake to kill, let it be only your enemies.”

The Deserted Village, theatre, and last years. The Deserted Village sells through multiple editions within weeks; critics may prefer The Traveller, but the public, Irving believes, loves Auburn best. Theatre brings The Good-Natur’d Man and, after struggles with managers and the town, She Stoops to Conquer—written in airy lodgings, visited by Reynolds and others, charity to beggars unbroken. Compilations, histories, and debt continue. In the spring of club banter, friends write mock epitaphs on “the late Dr. Goldsmith”; Garrick’s couplet stings; Goldsmith answers with Retaliation, epigrammatic portraits of intimates (including a free hand on Garrick). Illness, money distress, and death in London (4 April 1774 on Johnson’s Abbey tablet in this text) close the narrative. The Literary Club raises a Westminster Abbey monument by Nollekins; Johnson’s Latin inscription proclaims that he touched almost every kind of writing and adorned all he touched. Irving ends on character: faults visible, heart right, genius that sweetens temper and harmonises thought.

Volume I — Poetical Extracts

The Traveller; or, A Prospect of Society

Goldsmith’s comparative survey of nations: Italy’s beauty and decay, Switzerland’s hardy equality, France’s honour and politeness, Holland’s thrift, Britain’s freedom mixed with faction and the lure of empire. The poem balances local blessings against universal human want, and fixes the moral that happiness is less a matter of climate or constitution than of the tempered mind—preparing, in Irving’s Life, the fame that opened every later door.

The Deserted Village

Elegy for Auburn, the deserted village: schoolmaster, preacher, alehouse, and departing peasantry ruined by luxury and enclosure. Depopulation for the great man’s park, emigration, and the poet’s personal memory of Irish scenes (Lishoy and kindred landscapes in Irving’s notes) make the poem both social argument and home-sickness. Instant and immense sale followed The Traveller.

Retaliation

Club epitaphs in reverse: after companions mock-buried him in verse, Goldsmith draws epigrammatic portraits of the circle—Burke, Garrick, Reynolds, and others—mixing generous praise with good-humoured raillery, and paying Garrick in particular for refusal of plays, familiarity, and the “poor Poll” couplet.

The Hermit: A Ballad

Ballad of the hermit and the stranger: hospitality, concealed identity, and a moral of constancy and providence. Originally published in connection with the Vicar circle of tales; here printed among the poetical extracts.

Stanzas on Woman

Short lyric on woman’s shame, repentance, and the lover’s part—compressed pathos in the moral-ballad manner.

Song

Brief song lyric closing the poetical block before the essays.

Volume I — Miscellaneous Essays

Essay I — Clubs of London

A countryman newly in town tries to match a club to his temper and fails through obstinacy. He samples the Choice Spirits and other societies, lodges, and convocations—mirth, spleen, politics, and humdrum silence—finding that London can furnish company for every complexion (even Bedlam) while the seeker who will not conform remains unsuited. Comic ethnography of club life.

Essay II — Merry Andrew in St. James’s Park

The essayist sits by a shabby man who proves to have been sixteen years Merry Andrew to a puppet-show, now starving and still merry. Steak and tankard bought by the narrator become a lesson in cheerfulness without money: meat tastes sweetest when unpaid for; wit in rags is still pleasing. Street character sketch with a tavern coda.

Volume I — From The Bee

On Dress

The Mall, playhouses, and public walks blur ranks: dress no longer cleanly marks quality. Fashion levels and confuses; the essay mocks the labour and expense of appearances when a lady of no quality cannot be told from her betters at a glance.

The Sagacity of Some Insects

Addressed to the author of The Bee: natural-history moralising on insect industry, architecture, and community—minute creatures as mirrors of prudence and social art, described with the essayist’s mix of curiosity and fable.

On the Instability of Worldly Grandeur

An Islington alehouse-keeper’s rise and the Chinese student of great examples open a meditation on how worldly greatness collapses; empires and private fortunes share one dust. Grandeur is a rental, not a freehold.

On Eloquence

True oratory versus cool, artificial passion; the rewards of excellence; the English vulgar as an audience; and the moral stake of public speaking. Eloquence should move conviction and virtue, not merely decorate ambition—the Bee’s civic register.

Volume I — From The Citizen of the World (opening selection)

Editor’s preface frames the Chinese-letter project: polite nations’ few distinctions, the market for such correspondence, and the persona of Lien Chi Altangi. Vol. I then prints the early letters; Vol. II continues the series.

Introduction — A Character of the Chinese Philosopher

A merchant’s covering letter to a London correspondent introduces the bearer: a native of Honan, former mandarin helpful at Canton, honest, English-speaking, philosophic—Goldsmith’s frame for the whole series.

The Arrival of the Chinese in London — Motives for the Journey — Streets and Houses

Lien Chi announces himself in London: gratitude, poverty of return gifts, and first impressions of streets and houses compared with Nankin—ornament, layout, and the shock of a European capital to eyes trained in China.

The Description of London continued — Luxury of the English — Fine Gentleman — Fine Lady

Absence lengthens the chain to home; three days’ acquaintance yields only surface notes. English luxury, the manufactured fine gentleman, and the fine lady become the first sustained satire of metropolitan polish.

English Pride — Liberty — Newspapers — Politeness

Reserve mistaken for modesty proves pride. An Englishman fears contempt more than death; pride feeds national vices and virtues alike. Liberty, the press, and ceremonious politeness are read through that master passion.

Happiness Lost by seeking after Refinement — The Chinese Philosopher’s Disgraces (Fum Hoam)

The president of the Ceremonial Academy at Pekin rebukes the wanderer: enthusiasm for knowledge tears him from connections that make life pleasing; refinement purchases new disquiet. Disgrace and exile are moralised from China outward.

The Use of Wisdom only to make us Happy — Benefits of Travelling upon the Morals of a Philosopher

Lien Chi answers with captive wife and daughter, a son seeking him, and Confucius in hand: feel sorrow but do not sink; travel and wisdom must serve happiness, not mere curiosity. (Editor’s note flags borrowed Confucian rhapsody.)

The Funeral Solemnities of the English — Passion for flattering Epitaphs

English fear of death belied by sickbed ceremony; Chinese frankness about tombs contrasted with European passion for magnificent, flattering inscriptions. Mourning as social performance.

An Account of Westminster Abbey

The Chinese visitor in the Abbey: gloom of monuments, pride attending the “puny child of dust” to the grave, and the Man in Black as guide among philosophers, heroes, and kings—template for later Abbey satire.

The Reception of the Chinese from a Lady of Distinction

A fashionable invitation raises vain hopes of youth and beauty; the actual hostess and circle deflate the conquest fantasy. Female curiosity and Chinese celebrity are mutual spectacles.

Against Cruelty to Animals

Moral protest against casual English cruelty to beasts—another early letter in which the foreign observer indicts native hardness under a comic or mild surface.

Of the War now carried on between France and England, with its frivolous Motives

Treaties of perpetual peace annually broken; motives of European war look frivolous to an Asiatic politician. English and French self-images, colonial pretexts, and the gap between parchment friendship and endless campaigning.

The Story of the Chinese Matron

Framed by English versus Dutch styles of conjugality (passion versus prudent indifference), a Chinese matron’s tale tests widowhood, haste to remarry, and constancy—orientalised comic moral on marriage.

Some Account of the Republic of Letters in England

“Republic of letters” is absurd for Europe: no subordination, each member eager to govern, envy obstructing the temple of science that Chinese literary societies supposedly build in concert. Authors as a war of all against all.

The Chinese goes to see a Play

English theatre under roof and torchlight versus Chinese open-air multi-day drama. With the Man in Black, Lien Chi watches audience and acts—novelty, social seating, and a busy fifth act—closing Vol. I’s Citizen selection mid-career of the series.

Volume II — The Citizen of the World — Continued

Epistolary pieces in the voice of Lien Chi Altangi (and, in the captive’s strand, Hingpo). Titles follow the volume’s Contents / letter heads.

The Chinese Philosopher’s Son made a Slave in Persia

Lien Chi opens a letter brought by way of Smyrna: his son, having left China in filial piety to share his father’s exile, was seized with a caravan near the deserts of Tibet and led into the regions about the Aral Sea. Among Tartar masters who value only milk and raw flesh, learning and beauty win him nothing. The father balances private anguish against “true magnanimity”—rising each time one falls—and sets the captive-son strand that runs through later letters.

The Venders of Quack Medicines and Nostrums ridiculed

England is praised, with heavy irony, for an “art of healing” in which advertising professors never doubt. Every street sells infallible specifics that cure without loss of time or hinderance of business; patients who still die might as well have purchased health for half a crown at the corner. The letter jokes that when the living refuse pills, the profession ought next to revive the dead—already claiming to restore youth with a simple electuary.

The Character of the Man in Black, with some Instances of his inconsistent Conduct

Walking with the Man in Black, Lien Chi watches him rail against beggars while secretly relieving them. Compassion and pride contend: he slips a piece of silver to a petitioner, then lectures on imposture and prison enlargement; later he “buys” a shilling’s worth of matches from a wooden-legged sailor so as to give alms under a surly mask. The companion’s inverted economy of virtue is the comic centre of the letter.

The History of the Man in Black

Having seen “something reluctantly good” in the companion, Lien Chi presses for his history. The Man in Black narrates a life of early benevolence, family losses, and the lessons that taught him to hide charity under a crust of misanthropic talk. The biography explains the earlier street theatre: public hardness is a practised defence; private giving is the residual habit of a softer nature.

A Description of a Club of Authors

If learning were measured by books printed, England might outdo China: twenty-three new books in a day, thousands in a year. Lien Chi is taken to a club where authors, critics, and pretenders throng. Quantity of publication, not quality of thought, seems the national genius; the club is the microcosm of that industry.

The Proceedings of the Club of Authors

The Chinese observer prefers minute English peculiarities to sweeping national portraits—an “experimental inquiry” into genius. Inside the club, poets, panegyrists, and hack writers trade grievances: nobility ignores dedications; booksellers grind; mutual flattery and spleen fill the evening. The letter records speeches and squabbles rather than abstract theory of letters.

The Manner of Writing among the Chinese.—The Eastern Tales of Magazines, &c., ridiculed

English magazine “eastern tales” and Chinese literary forms are set against each other. A little man boasts of vamping a flaunted poetical panegyric for a returned duke and receiving nothing; others pitch their hopes at the nobility. Goldsmith’s satire hits both fashionable Orientalism in London print and the vanity of authors who write only for patronage.

The Philosopher’s Son describes a Lady, his fellow-captive

Hingpo writes of a Christian captive newly bought for Mostadad’s household. Even the eunuch who purchased her speaks of her beauty with emotion; her pride astonishes the slaves. The Persian has offered to make her one of four wives if she will change her religion. Hingpo’s report mixes philosophical self-command with unmistakable agitation at her fate.

A Continuance of his Correspondence.—The Beautiful Captive consents to Marry her Lord

Hingpo insists his uneasiness is not “so degrading a passion” as love but pity that so much excellence should be lavished on a wretch. Feasting, music, and torches prepare Mostadad’s bridal triumph; Hingpo is among the slaves ordered to carry the tyrant to the apartment. The letter closes the first arc of forced consent before the later escape.

The Correspondence still continued.—He begins to be Disgusted in the Pursuit of Wisdom.—An Allegory to prove its Futility

Hingpo doubts whether wisdom alone can make men happy; each step of refinement seems an inlet to new disquiet. An ancient Guebre tells the allegory of the Valley of Ignorance—simple inhabitants happy until curiosity and ambition lead travellers toward a “happy land,” only for flattery and premature unveiling to cast them into an Ocean of Doubts. The captive’s moral is that vigorous minds may consume the bodies that house them.

The Description of True Politeness.—Two Letters of different Countries by Ladies supposed equally Polite at Home

True politeness is contrasted with national forms of ceremony. Two sample letters—from ladies of different countries, each “polite” at home—expose how manners that read as grace in one culture read as absurdity in another. The Chinese frame treats politeness as moral consideration, not empty form.

The Behaviour of the Congregation in St. Paul’s Church at Prayers

Lien Chi attends prayers at St. Paul’s and reports the congregation’s behaviour: staring, whispering, ogling, and fashion on display rather than devotion. The letter is a set piece of foreign observation on English public religion as social theatre.

The Ardour of the People of London in running after Sights and Monsters

Despite frequent invitations, the philosopher remarks London’s passion for shows—monsters, rarities, and spectacles. Crowds rush wherever novelty is cried up. The letter links commercial exhibition culture to a restlessness that leaves little room for quiet reflection.

A Dream

A dream vision presents enchanted furniture of the East (including the looking-glass of Lao that reflects the mind as well as the face) and then dissolves into chimeras, castles, and dragons. Lien Chi invites Fum Hoam’s interpretation, joking that dreams go by contraries—so the vision’s women may be read as praise of English ladies.

The Absurdity of Persons in High Stations pursuing Employments beneath them, exemplified in a Fairy Tale

A fairy tale, prompted by a visit to a painter’s, allegorizes great persons who abandon proper stations for low employments. Kings, philosophers, and princesses scramble after inappropriate prizes (mice, green eyes, trivial quests), while moralising platitudes fail to console. The satire targets fashionable misemployment of rank and talent.

The Fairy Tale continued

The princess tries to soothe a prince inconsolable over a lost animal or quest; she preaches that incurable evils must be borne and that man is born to misfortunes. The tale presses the same moral: elevated persons making themselves ridiculous in pursuits beneath them, while courtiers and philosophers talk in empty maxims.

A Bookseller’s Visit to the Chinese

An old acquaintance introduces “Mr. Fudge,” whose civilities mask trade: he is a bookseller fishing for copy. Season, novelty, and the market govern his compliments. Lien Chi’s reserve and blunt refusals cool the visit; the letter sketches the London book trade’s mixture of flattery and calculation.

The Impossibility of distinguishing Men in England by their Dress

Elsewhere the rich wear their gold; in England those who carry most metal on their clothes often have least in their pockets. Dress is no safe index of rank or wealth. The Chinese observer finds English costume a democratic puzzle that confounds the social reading he learned in China and much of Europe.

The Character of an Important Trifler

Though naturally pensive, Lien Chi seeks gay company to dismiss the mind from duty. He falls in with a little beau who lives by being everywhere pleasure is sold—shouting, condemning, and performing frivolous earnestness. The “important trifler” is a social parasite whose whole art is presence without substance.

His Character continued, with that of his Wife, his House, and Furniture

The beau cannot be shaken off. A visit to his lodgings reveals four embroidered-bottom chairs, a once-japanned table, a cradle, broken china, and pretensions to high life through a Highland “parliament man.” Mrs. Tibbs’s accent and breeding disappoint her husband’s claims. The letter inventories shabby-genteel London domesticity.

The Difficulty of Rising in Literary Reputation without Intrigue or Riches

Literary fame, the letters insist, seldom rises on pure merit. Intrigue, fashion, and money pave the way; the poor man of genius earns reputation late, if at all. The Chinese frame contrasts true service to virtue through the press with the scarlet finery of empty dignities.

A Visitation Dinner described

Through the Man in Black, Lien Chi attends a clerical visitation dinner. The board groans under soup, pig, pork, and bacon until the company sinks into lethargic silence. A hungry beggar at the window is imagined claiming every surplus morsel as his property once nature is satisfied—turning gluttony into a social indictment.

The Chinese Philosopher’s Son escapes with the Beautiful Captive from Slavery

Hingpo cuts his way through eunuchs, seizes coursers from Mostadad’s stables, and flees north with Zelis amid fire and terror. In three days they reach Terki in a mountain valley, free to enjoy “satisfactions consistent with virtue.” The romance strand pivots from captivity to precarious liberty.

Proper Lessons to a Youth entering the World, with Fables suited to the Occasion

Fatherly counsel to a youth, laced with fables: how to read praise and blame, how a painter finds that faults condemned one day are beauties the next, and how half the world is pleased only when the other half is ignored. Practical worldly wisdom is taught through short exemplary stories.

The Great exchange Happiness for Show.—Their Folly in this respect of use to Society

People trade solid happiness for display; their folly nonetheless feeds trades and keeps society in motion. Lien Chi treats ostentation as a public utility even while mocking those who live for surfaces. The letter links private vanity to the circulation of industry.

The History of a Philosophic Cobbler

In a crowd watching a foreign ambassador’s entry, Lien Chi has his shoe torn and visits a cobbler. The mender’s talk opens a life history of marriage, a wife’s death over a long bill, and money hidden beyond recovery. The philosopher pays for the shoe and the narrative—amusement drawn from humble philosophy.

The Folly of attempting to learn Wisdom by being Recluse

Books may teach care for others while unfitting us for our own interest. Retreat from society often produces not angels or beasts but disappointed good-natured men who rail in solitude. The letter warns Hingpo (and the reader) against wisdom purchased only by withdrawal.

Quacks Ridiculed.—Some particularly mentioned

Returning to English medicine, Lien Chi names particular advertising healers and issues mock challenges in the style of learned dispute—botany, chemistry, and hard problems—while exposing Garbage Alley pretenders. The satire of specifics and nostrums is sharper and more personal than the earlier general letter on quacks.

The Fear of Mad Dogs ridiculed

Epidemic terror of mad dogs sweeps the town: stories of boys bitten, men dying years after a bite, whole families infected by a lapdog. Each morning brings a new disaster, as in ghost stories where each teller outbids the last. Lien Chi ridicules the panic more than the rare disease.

Fortune proved not to be Blind.—The Story of the Avaricious Miller

A vision leads a miller to dig for treasure under his mill; omens (a broken mug, a new tile, a great stone) feed his avarice until labour and disappointment moralise the tale. Fortune is not blind—she punishes greedy certainty. The letter uses the miller’s dig as a fable of misread providence.

The Shabby Beau, the Man in Black, the Chinese Philosopher, &c., at Vauxhall

A party to Vauxhall Gardens mixes the Man in Black, the shabby beau and his wife, a widow eager for water-works, and Lien Chi. Songs, waiting, missed spectacles, and competitive gentility turn the evening into comedy. Fashionable recreation is shown as disappointment managed by ceremony.

Life endeared by Age

Through a Chinese exemplum—an old man freed after more than fifty years’ unjust imprisonment under Chinvang the Chaste—the letter reflects on how long suffering endears remaining life. Light dazzles the released prisoner; age and restored liberty sharpen the value of ordinary days.

The Description of a Little Great Man

Every town breeds “little great men” who fancy themselves famous and trade compliments on their extensive reputations. A German doctor and a monk divide a panegyric’s glory between them. Local celebrity is a ceremonial game invisible beyond its parish.

The Necessity of Amusing each other with New Books insisted upon

New books are a social lubricant: they give the fashionable something to praise while fashion lasts and hide emptiness from the public. Lien Chi defends the continual supply of novelty as a need of polished nations, even when the books themselves are slight.

The Behaviour of a Shopkeeper and his Journeyman

London shops hang pictures at the door as Pekin shops hang boards promising honesty. Buying silk for a nightcap, Lien Chi meets a powdered master and men of extreme civility who can mould a customer’s will—persuading him into a morning-gown as well. Commercial politeness is a technology of influence.

The Preparations of both the Theatres for a Winter Campaign

The two patent theatres arm for the season as if for war—authors, actors, and managers in campaign. Modern plays that spare the audience the fatigue of thinking are compared to tragic pantomime and opium. Theatrical competition is national news in miniature.

The Sciences useful in a Populous State, prejudicial in a Barbarous one

Sciences become useful only after population, property, law, and luxury have created demand for them; forced on a barbarous society they degenerate or remain exotic. The letter sketches a stadial progress from hunter and shepherd to husbandman before letters can serve the state.

Anecdotes of several Poets who lived and died in circumstances of great Wretchedness

English (and European) poets who starved or died in misery supply a catalogue of literary wretchedness. Merit acknowledged too late, patrons fickle, and fame posthumous: the Chinese observer collects cases that darken the glamour of the republic of letters.

The trifling Squabbles of Stage-players ridiculed

Almost turned Englishman by sympathy with British arms abroad, Lien Chi still hates war—and hates still more the irreconcilable oppositions threatened from the stage while Europe is already at variance. Players’ quarrels over bouncing heroes and copper-tailed actresses look absurd beside real campaigns; he would have actors fed, not fattened, on the public.

The Races of Newmarket ridiculed.—The Description of a Cartrace

Newmarket racing and the training of blood animals are mocked; a “cartrace” description reduces genteel sport to farce. The same nation whose valour figures abroad wrangles over horses and jockeys at home. Sporting fashion is another English excess under the Chinese eye.

The Ladies Advised to get Husbands.—A Story to the purpose

Because female education is left to foreign masters, Lien Chi half-seriously proposes a female academy: wives taught to manage husbands, maids to catch them, and the strategic uses of fashionable illness. A story drives the advice home. Courtship and marriage are treated as arts as teachable as music or hair-frizzing.

The Folly of remote or useless Distinctions among the Learned

Learned men chase remote, useless distinctions while missing living knowledge. A fable of a “manfish,” a prude turned shrimp, and a coquette turned oyster moralises coquetry, pedantry, and misdirected pursuit. Scholarship without proportion becomes as empty as flirtation without catch.

The English subject to the Spleen

Past thirty, Englishmen “lie in of the spleen” at intervals, nursing the fit with drinking, idleness, and soft furniture. Foreigners with long chins or tarnished coats suffer for it; failing that, natives beat each other. The rich, having more sensibility, suffer the disorder more violently.

The Influence of Climate and Soil upon the Temper and Disposition of the English

Soil and climate shape plants, animals, and men—poisons lose force abroad; Macedonian serpents and Fez lions supply comparative natural history. The letter extends the common eighteenth-century climate argument to English temper, linking weather and earth to national disposition.

The Manner in which some Philosophers make Artificial Misery

The mind invents distress as it rises: the beggar wants a meal; fed and clothed, he wants ease; eased, ambition taints the next wish. Philosophers who manufacture theoretical misery mirror this ladder of discontent. Goldsmith’s moral is that desire widens with every gratification.

The Fondness of some to admire the Writings of Lords, and such who are rich

Title and wealth draw admiration to mediocre writing, while habit and necessity sharpen real authors who may never enjoy their fame. Fashion promotes literary reputations that scarce survive the possessor. The letter returns to the economics of reputation already sounded in the club scenes.

The Philosopher’s Son is again separated from his beautiful Companion

Hingpo’s flight does not secure permanent union: he is again parted from Zelis. The letter (in the father’s counsel and the son’s report) treats separation as another trial of fortitude after the Persian escape, keeping the romance strand unfinished within this volume’s selection.

The Condolence and Congratulation upon the Death of the late King ridiculed.—English Mourning described

Chinese mourning is white, deep, and prolonged; English mourning is black, brief, and largely a matter of clapping on sables. When the great die, mandarins order mourning but do not always pay for it—Lien Chi resents being obliged both to wear black and to buy it. Official grief is ceremony without cost to the court.

A Description of the Courts of Justice in Westminster Hall

Westminster Hall’s courts supply a set piece of English law as public spectacle: forms, faces, and the business of justice under one roof. The Chinese visitor reads the hall as another London theatre where authority and interest perform.

A Life of Independence Praised

Moralists praise generosity; few teach the ignominy of receiving. Every favour accepted forfeits a measure of native freedom. Lien Chi prefers the sturdy gloom of laborious independence to the fawning simper of thriving adulation, and warns his son against a life of continual dependence.

The People must be contented to be Guided by those whom they have appointed to Govern.—A Story to this Effect

In every society some are born to teach and some to learn, some to work and some to enjoy, some to govern and some to obey. A free people must still yield part of liberty and judgment to governors in exchange for security. A tale of a queen, a minister, and a fruitless search for a desolate village illustrates the paradox of judging government by impossible standards.

The Chinese Philosopher begins to think of quitting England

A letter from Hingpo reports the fruitlessness of efforts to recover Zelis; the father offers little consolation. Homesickness and satiety with English spectacle gather; Lien Chi contemplates ending his English residence. The series begins to wind toward leave-taking.

The Arts some make use of to appear Learned

Chinese scholars venerate forms—philosophical caps, whiskers, slippers, fans, even a standard for nails—yet often remain empty of wisdom. English counterparts have their own props of learning. Appearance of erudition is a costume drama in both civilisations.

The intended Coronation described

The young king’s coronation approaches; a country knight takes lodgings, his wife lays in silks, his daughter has her ears bored. Lien Chi is shoved two stories higher to make room for “very good company.” He confesses a confused description—neither Garter’s order nor the length of a lady’s train—preferring comparison with Emperor Whangti’s marriage to the moon.

An Election described

Every seventh year England keeps a parliamentary feast. Lien Chi mixes with crowds inspired by anger, jealousy, politics, patriotism, and punch; magistrates are chosen amid confusion; speakers pathos over foreign drams and the distillery. Electioneering is carnival and combat together.

A City Night-piece

A nocturnal walk through the city contrasts august facades with the shops of candle-vendors, small-coal men, and broom-sellers. Night exposes poverty, solitude, and the other London that pageantry hides. The piece is among the most atmospheric of the English letters.

On the Distresses of the Poor, exemplified in the life of a Private Sentinel

The misfortunes of the great draw declamation and admiring pity; true magnanimity is the obscure man who braves adversity without audience. A private sentinel’s life—service, wounds, privateering, lost fingers and leg, and the harsh difference between king’s ship and privateer pensions—grounds the moral in one battered body.

On the Absurdity of some late English Titles

Titles and pageants of vanity multiply under feeble or despotic courts; strength and freedom prefer majestic simplicity. The young monarch’s reported contempt for useless household appendages (cooks, scullions, gentlemen’s gentlemen) is praised as a return to frugality and true glory.

The Manner of Travellers in their usual Relations ridiculed

Travellers’ relations are mocked for empty generalities and self-importance—the opposite of the “experimental” minute detail Lien Chi claimed earlier. Fashionable travel writing is another English (and European) vanity trade.

The Conclusion

The Chinese philosopher closes the English correspondence: final scenes of streets, modest repasts (roast mutton and potatoes), and leave-taking from the island of sights, clubs, and spleen. The Citizen sequence in this volume ends as a farewell rather than a completed biography of its speaker.

Volume II — Miscellaneous

Shorter Goldsmith pieces printed after the Citizen letters in the second volume.

The History of Hypatia

Hypatia of Alexandria, daughter of the philosopher Leon (Theon), is celebrated for beauty and learning under Theodosius the younger. A woman who invades the privileges of male philosophy is likened to a neutral being between the sexes—admired by both, happy in neither. The piece narrates her fame and her brutal end: dragged to the church called Caesarea, stripped, cut to pieces, and burned by a fanatic crowd—“the glory of her own sex and the astonishment of ours.”

On Justice and Generosity

Lysippus embodies a greatness of soul whose generosity prevents the demand and spares the receiver confusion. He obliges as much by grace in giving as by the gift’s size, and has done offices even for professed enemies. The essay weighs open-handed bounty against strict justice, asking when liberality becomes self-stripping folly.

The Story of Alcander and Septimius

From a “Byzantine historian”: in late antique Athens, still a seat of learning, Alcander and Septimius form a friendship tested by crime, tribunal, and war. False accusation, battlefield recognition, and a temple in flames bring the friends through jealousy and terror to acquittal and shared honour. The tale is a classical friendship romance in Goldsmith’s moral-essay manner.

On Friendship

Friendship is treated as an exchange of hearts that fashion, interest, and multiplicity of claims continually threaten. Examples of loyalty under judicial and military stress (echoing the Alcander strand) support a maxim of constancy against the world’s more numerous “friendships” of convenience.

Asem, the Man-hater

Asem, soured into misanthropy, is granted a vision or trial by a genius of the place that exposes the folly of universal hatred. Thunder, vanished illusion, and a return to the water-side leave him repentant: to keep from vice himself and pity it in others. The orientalised fable converts the man-hater back to social feeling.

Sabinus and Olinda

A paired tale of lovers (or exemplary pair) under trial, thematically continuous with the friendship and constancy essays. Separation, fidelity, and recognition structure the narrative; Providence and human loyalty intertwine in the resolution.

On the different Schools of Music

A “school” in the polite arts is a succession trained by an eminent master’s lessons or works. Goldsmith ranks three principal musical schools—Pergolesi in Italy, Lully in France, Handel in England—with some arguing for Rameau as a fourth inventor of beauties of his own. Pergolesi is given first place for expression even without unmatched variety of movement.

Shenstone and his Gardens

Poets form the most sanguine illusions of distant happiness; imagination arrogates possessions the owner relishes more bluntly. William Shenstone’s landscaping at the Leasowes is the occasion for reflecting on taste, improvement, and the poet’s confidence that keeps spirits up—how much less would be done if a man knew how little he can do. The essay joins garden aesthetics to literary character.

Political Frugality

Frugality is a pagan and Christian virtue, yet too modest to seem essential to heroism; it agrees better with politics as the base of states. Luxury, superfluity, and the thirst for riches breed effeminacy and expense. The piece reads economy as public virtue, not mere private meanness, and ties national strength to restraint.

Upon Unfortunate Merit

Every age has favourite pursuits that amuse the idle and relieve the industrious; some geniuses are born adapted to the vogue, while others who might have excelled in another season go unrewarded. Merit out of fashion is “unfortunate merit.” The essay mourns the mismatch of talent and times.

Deceit and Falsehood

A framed narrative (including an innkeeper, signs, and the fate of the painter Caravaggio on the road in the volume’s telling) introduces a letter on deceit as an “overmatch” for simple honesty. Falsehood in trade and life multiplies until the generous and the artless are undone. The moral is practical suspicion without abandoning virtue.

Scheme for raising an Army of Amazons proposed

In the spirit of projectors (and with a bow to the advertisements of Jacob Henriquez), the essay proposes disciplining a female brigade—light carbines, bayonets, white feathers—arguing that young ladies of fashion might raise companies at their own expense if they liked their colonels. Comic military detail carries a satire on both patriotic schemes and gender fashion.

On National Prejudice

National prejudice is attacked as a bastard sprout of the noble plant of love of country. One may love one’s country and defend its laws without hating or despising all other nations. The essay separates patriotic bravery from xenophobic contempt.

On Taste

Taste is grounded in sensibility shared with virtue; the two cannot be disjoined without violence to both, yet both need instruction. Nature is not equally generous with critical capacity: of two youths under the same preceptor, one anticipates the master while the other toils in vain. Genius and stupidity are daily facts, not flattering equalities.

On the Improvement of Taste

Having defined true taste, the essay points the way to improve it: form the heart before overloading the understanding; give the young striking instances of moral beauty (Livy’s Romans and the Greeks, Alexander and Philip the physician, Henry IV and the famine of Paris) so that passion lists on the side of humanity. Classical reading should train observation of character while Latin and Greek are learned; taste is nature improved by art, feeling tutored by instruction. Horace’s lines on the critic who knows what is owed to country, friend, and office close the didactic arc of the volume.
