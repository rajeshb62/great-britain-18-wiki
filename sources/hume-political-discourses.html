Hume — Political Discourses

David Hume (1711–1776) — Political Discourses. — The Scott Library edition, with an introduction by William Bell Robertson. The Walter Scott Publishing Co., London and Felling-on-Tyne, 1906. Originally published 1752.

Hume’s collected economic and political essays, among the most influential works of 18th-century British thought. The essays laid the foundations of classical economics — the quantity theory of money, the price-specie-flow mechanism, the critique of mercantilism, the analysis of public credit — all before Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations (1776). The political essays address the great constitutional questions of the day: the original contract, passive obedience, party politics, and the Hanoverian succession.

The text is 12,192 lines, extracted from the Pennsylvania State University Library copy.

Essay-by-Essay Summary

Economic Essays

Of Commerce (line 974) — Opens by distinguishing “shallow thinkers” from “abstruse thinkers” in political reasoning. Argues that commerce and manufactures are the foundation of national power — a state cannot maintain military force without a prosperous commercial sector. Rejects the view that agriculture is the only source of national wealth. Distinguishes between the happiness of individuals (which requires industry, knowledge, and humanity) and the power of the state (which requires disciplined military force), and argues they are mutually reinforcing rather than opposed.

Of Refinement in the Arts (line 1537) — A defence of luxury against its moral critics. “Luxury is a word of a very uncertain signification” — Hume argues that the refinement of the arts and the pursuit of comfort and elegance are not vices but positive goods that stimulate industry, knowledge, and humanity. The ages of greatest luxury are also the ages of greatest virtue and liberty. The essay is a direct attack on the republican austerity tradition and a defence of commercial society.

Of Money (line 2021) — The classic statement of the quantity theory of money. Money is “not one of the subjects of commerce, but only the instrument which men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another.” Argues that the absolute quantity of money in a country is irrelevant to its real wealth; only the proportion of money to commodities matters for prices. The only real advantage of abundant specie is in foreign wars. Expresses scepticism about paper credit and banks, which artificially increase the money supply and raise prices to the disadvantage of domestic manufacturers. Develops the price-specie-flow mechanism that later became the centrepiece of classical monetary theory.

Of Interest (line 2515) — Argues that the rate of interest is not determined by the quantity of money (contra Locke) but by the rate of profit on mercantile capital. Low interest is a sign of flourishing commerce, not of abundant specie. The three determinants of interest are: the demand for borrowing (driven by the profit to be made from borrowed capital), the supply of loanable funds (driven by the growth of commercial wealth), and the security of the lender (driven by the legal system). All three improve with commercial development.

Of the Balance of Trade (line 2999) — The most influential economic essay in the collection. A devastating critique of mercantilist fears that a nation’s gold and silver can be permanently drained away by an unfavourable balance of trade. Hume shows that any such drain is automatically self-correcting: a decrease in the money supply lowers domestic prices, which makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, restoring the balance. The four great arguments against mercantilism: (1) the automatic mechanism of specie-flow, (2) the impossibility of permanently retaining all the world’s gold, (3) the benefits of free trade, (4) the foolishness of policies against the natural course of commerce.

Of the Jealousy of Trade (line 3658) — Extends the argument of the previous essay to the political level. Argues that the prosperity of a neighbouring nation is not a threat to Britain’s commerce but an opportunity. Trading nations are not rivals in a zero-sum game; the growth of commerce anywhere stimulates industry and demand everywhere. A plea for free trade principles before they had a name.

Of the Balance of Power (line 3814) — Discusses whether the modern system of balancing power among European states is a recent invention or has ancient precedents. Traces the idea from ancient Greece through Renaissance Italy to 18th-century Europe. The modern balance of power, Hume argues, is more effective because it involves more independent states and a more complex system of alliances.

Of Taxes (line 4097) — Discusses the incidence of taxation and who ultimately bears the burden. Argues that taxes on luxuries are preferable to taxes on necessaries. Addresses the question of whether high taxes discourage industry — Hume thinks they can actually stimulate it up to a point, as people work harder to maintain their accustomed standard of living.

Of Public Credit (line 4297) — The most prescient essay in the collection. Hume argues that national debts, if they reach a certain magnitude, must inevitably cause national bankruptcy. The system of funding (borrowing rather than taxing) is a modern invention that “mortgages the public revenues” and gives a dangerous power to the monied interest. Warns that either the nation will destroy public credit or public credit will destroy the nation. The essay was quoted extensively by later critics of the national debt, including the 1817 “Imlac” pamphlet in this wiki.

Of Some Remarkable Customs (line 4925) — A brief essay on peculiar customs of various nations, including the Roman practice of letting military commands by auction, the Dutch use of public banks, and the English reliance on newspaper publicity.

Of the Populousness of Ancient Nations (line ~5500-8050) — The longest essay in the collection. A massive historical and demographic argument that the ancient world (Greece and Rome) was not more populous than modern Europe, contrary to the prevailing view among classical enthusiasts. Uses evidence from military sizes, city populations, food supply, marriage customs, and slavery to demonstrate that modern commercial society supports a larger population than ancient societies ever did.

Political Essays

Of the Original Contract (line ~8350) — A devastating critique of the social contract theory of government, which Hume treats as a philosophical fiction. Government originates not in consent but in “usurpation or conquest” and is maintained not by contract but by “habit and custom.” The only legitimate foundation of government is utility — it secures justice and order, and that is why people obey it. An attack on both the Whig theory of contract and the Tory theory of divine right.

Of Passive Obedience (line 8791) — Criticises the extreme Tory doctrine that subjects must never resist the sovereign under any circumstances. But also criticises the extreme Whig position that resistance is always justified against bad government. The truth, Hume argues, is somewhere in between: resistance is justifiable only in extreme cases of oppression, but it is not never justifiable.

Of the Coalition of Parties (line 8950) — Argues that while party distinctions may be unavoidable in free governments, the dangerous parties are those that disagree about the fundamentals of the constitution (monarchy vs. republic, succession of the crown). A plea for moderation and for narrowing the grounds of political division to matters of policy rather than principle.

Of the Protestant Succession (line 9232) — Written during the Jacobite threat, Hume argues in favour of the Hanoverian succession on purely utilitarian grounds. The Protestant succession has brought stability, liberty, and prosperity. The Stuart claim rests only on hereditary right, which is not absolute. But the essay is notable for its cool, unsentimental tone — it defends the settlement not out of love for the House of Hanover but because it works.

Idea of a Perfect Commonwealth (line 9696) — Hume’s only utopian essay. Skeptical about political blueprints in general (“political projectors” are either dangerous or ridiculous), he nonetheless sketches a model republic based on representative government, a bicameral legislature, territorial subdivision, and rotation in office. The model is designed to be practical rather than ideal — it draws heavily on English and Dutch practice.

That Politics May Be Reduced to a Science (line ~10150) — Argues that regularities in political behaviour can be studied scientifically. The same political institutions tend to produce the same effects in different times and places. A foundational essay for the modern social sciences.

Of the First Principles of Government (line ~10450) — Distinguishes between the two foundations of political authority: opinion of interest (people obey because they think the government serves their interests) and opinion of right (people obey because they think the government has a rightful title to rule). Both are forms of opinion; force alone never maintains a government.

Of Political Society (line 11025) — Discusses the conditions under which political society can be maintained, the importance of law and regular administration, and the comparative advantages of large and small states.

Relationship to the Wiki

The most important single source on 18th-century economic and political thought in the wiki. Hume’s essays directly address the great issues of the age: the growth of commerce, the national debt, the balance of trade, the constitutional settlement, the party system, and the foundations of political authority. They provide the intellectual framework that contemporaries used to understand the events described in the Campbell, Belsham, Burnet, and Smollett volumes. The essay on public credit, in particular, speaks directly to the financial history covered by the Massie, Colquhoun, and Carey sources.
