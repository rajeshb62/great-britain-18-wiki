Campbell — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. III

John Campbell (1708–1775) — The Naval History of Great Britain, Commencing with the Earliest Period of History, and Continued to the Expedition against Algiers, under the Command of Lord Exmouth, in 1816. Including the History and Lives of the British Admirals. — Volume III. Printed for Baldwyn and Co., London, 1818.

This is Volume III of an 8-volume set. It continues directly from Vol. II (Chapter XVIII mid-stream) and covers from the closing years of William III’s reign through the entire reign of Queen Anne (1702–1714), taking British naval history from 1697 to the Peace of Utrecht and the Hanoverian succession.

The volume contains 554 page-files (OCR’d from the Columbia University Library copy, barcode cu09367306), extracted as 23,608 lines (~1.1 MB) of plain text. The printed volume runs to 505 pages of content plus front matter and library plates.

Why this is the 18th-century volume: While the first two chapters (~160 pages) close out the 17th-century reign of William III, the bulk of the volume — chapters XX and XXI, covering roughly 343 pages — falls squarely in the 18th century: Queen Anne’s reign, the War of the Spanish Succession (1702–1714), the capture of Gibraltar (1704), the Union of England and Scotland (1707), the Peace of Utrecht (1713), and the dawn of British Mediterranean dominance.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAP. XVIII (continued) — William III to the Peace of Ryswick (line 152, ~16 pages)

Continues directly from Vol. II. Covers the tail end of the Nine Years’ War (1689–1697). Key topics:

  • Naval operations in the West Indies — French attacks on English colonies (Jamaica, Barbados), the defence of English plantations, Caribbean theatre engagements
  • The English capture of Port Royal (Acadia / Nova Scotia) under Sir William Phips
  • The Peace of Ryswick (1697) and its terms affecting the colonies and the naval balance
  • The state of the fleet and colonies as the war drew to a close — dockyard conditions, manning levels, colonial defences

CHAP. XIX — William III Post-Ryswick, 1697–1702 (line 2598, ~145 pages)

Covers the final years of William III’s reign after the Peace of Ryswick. Despite being technically 17th century (William died March 1702), this chapter already looks forward to the 18th-century geopolitical order. Key topics:

  • The peacetime reduction of the fleet — the administrative challenge of maintaining naval readiness with a peacetime budget
  • English naval operations in the Mediterranean against Louis XIV’s growing naval power
  • The Scottish Darien Scheme (1698–1700) — the disastrous colonial venture in Panama, the English refusal to support it, and its role in precipitating the Act of Union (1707)
  • The diplomatic prelude to the War of the Spanish Succession — the death of Charles II of Spain (1700), Louis XIV’s acceptance of the Spanish throne for his grandson Philip of Anjou
  • English naval preparations for the coming war — fleet expansion, dockyard mobilisation
  • Memoirs of prominent naval figures — the chapter is built around biographical sketches that function as mini-biographies of the era’s leading commanders:
    • George Legge, Baron Dartmouth — James II’s admiral who failed to intercept William’s invasion fleet in 1688
    • Sir John Berry — naval commander in the Second and Third Anglo-Dutch Wars
    • Arthur Herbert, Baron Herbert of Tor Bay — the admiral who brought the fleet over to William of Orange
    • Anthony Hastings — captain and commissioner of the navy
    • John Neville — naval commander in the West Indies
    • Sir Francis Wheeler — commander in the Mediterranean campaign
    • Henry FitzRoy, Duke of Grafton — illegitimate son of Charles II, killed at the storming of Cork
    • Sir John Ashby — admiral at the Battle of Barfleur
    • Edward Russell, Earl of Orford — the victor of Barfleur-La Hougue, First Lord of the Admiralty

CHAP. XX — Queen Anne, Accession to the Union, 1702–1707 (line 7141, ~140 pages)

The opening phase of the War of the Spanish Succession (1702–1714) — this is where the 18th century proper begins. Key topics:

  • The accession of Queen Anne (March 1702) and the immediate declaration of war against France and Spain — the Grand Alliance re-formed
  • Sir George Rooke’s expedition to Cadiz (1702) — the failure at Cadiz redeemed by the spectacular capture of the Spanish treasure fleet at Vigo Bay
  • The Battle of Vigo Bay (1702) — destruction of the Franco-Spanish treasure fleet, one of the most consequential naval actions of the early 18th century
  • The capture of Gibraltar (1704) — Sir George Rooke’s bold seizure of the Rock, perhaps the single greatest prize of the war, establishing British control of the Mediterranean gateway for centuries
  • The Battle of Málaga (1704) — the largest naval battle of the war, an Anglo-Dutch tactical draw against the French under the Comte de Toulouse
  • The Mediterranean campaign — Sir Cloudesley Shovel’s operations, the Toulon blockade, support for the Austrian Habsburg claimant Charles III
  • The capture of Barcelona (1705) — the English fleet’s crucial role in the Mediterranean campaign under the Earl of Peterborough
  • English operations in the West Indies — defending the colonies, capturing French islands
  • Naval administration and finance under Queen Anne
  • The Union of England and Scotland (1707) — the naval implications: a unified British navy, Scottish access to English trade routes, the birth of British naval dominance

CHAP. XXI — Queen Anne, Union to End of Reign, 1707–1714 (line 13432, ~203 pages)

The climax and conclusion of the War of the Spanish Succession. Key topics:

  • The disaster off the Scilly Isles (1707) — Sir Cloudesley Shovel’s flagship Association and three other ships wrecked on the Western Rocks, over 1,400 men lost including Shovel himself, a catastrophic navigational failure that spurred the Longitude Act
  • The Battle of Toulon (1707) — the failed Anglo-Dutch assault on the French Mediterranean base
  • Minorca captured (1708) — the British seizure of Port Mahon, establishing a permanent Mediterranean naval base to complement Gibraltar
  • Admiral Byng’s victory at Passaro (1718) — the destruction of the Spanish fleet (a coda extending slightly beyond Anne’s reign)
  • The capture of Sardinia and Sicily — the consolidation of British Mediterranean supremacy
  • The Peace of Utrecht (1713) — terms: Gibraltar and Minorca ceded to Britain, the Asiento (slave-trading monopoly) granted to Britain, French recognition of the Hanoverian Protestant succession
  • The death of Queen Anne (1714) and the accession of George I — the Hanoverian succession and its implications for British naval policy
  • Memoir of Vice-Admiral John Benbow — his controversial West Indies campaign against the French, the mutiny of his captains, his death from wounds (1702), the subsequent court-martial and punishment of his cowardly captains — one of the most famous disciplinary episodes in Royal Navy history

Relevance to England 18th Century Wiki

High relevance. The volume is predominantly 18th-century content. Chapters XX–XXI (roughly 343 of 505 pages) cover the War of the Spanish Succession, the capture of Gibraltar, Minorca, the Union of 1707, and the Peace of Utrecht — defining events of early 18th-century British history. Even Chapter XIX (William III’s final years, 1697–1702) is transitional: the Darien Scheme, the death of Charles II, and the diplomatic prelude to the Spanish succession all set up the 18th-century geopolitical order. The biographical memoirs (Benbow, Rooke, Shovel, Byng, Russell) provide character sketches of the leading naval figures of the period.

What this volume does NOT cover: The later 18th century — the War of Jenkins’ Ear, the Seven Years’ War, the American Revolution, the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars — all come in later volumes (IV–VIII) that would need to be added to this wiki for comprehensive coverage.
