Wakefield — A Reply to Some Parts of the Bishop of Landaff’s Address

Gilbert Wakefield (1756–1801) — A Reply to Some Parts of the Bishop of Landaff’s Address to the People of Great Britain. — Second Edition. London: Sold for the Author by J. Curnell, Middle-Row, Holborn, 1793 [text dated Hackney, 30 January 1798].

Gilbert Wakefield was a radical Unitarian clergyman and classical scholar, educated at Jesus College, Cambridge. He was an outspoken critic of the war with Revolutionary France and of government repression of dissent. In 1799 he was prosecuted for seditious libel and imprisoned for two years in Dorchester Gaol, where his health failed and he died shortly after his release. This pamphlet was among the writings cited against him at his trial.

The pamphlet is his reply to Bishop Richard Watson of Landaff, who had published a loyalist Address to the People of Great Britain (1798) supporting Pitt’s war against France. Wakefield argues that the war is unjust, that Pitt’s ministry is insincere in its peace overtures, that the government is using the war to suppress liberty at home, and that the pro-war clergy are betraying the gospel. The second edition includes additions and retrenchments.

The text is 2,884 lines, extracted from a microfilm scan. OCR quality is moderate — the 18th-century long-s (ſ) is preserved, with some debris at page breaks and extensive scanning artifacts. The pamphlet concludes with a 37-item advertisement of Wakefield’s own published works.

Date discrepancy: The title-page imprint reads 1793, but the pamphlet’s closing line gives “Hackney, January 30th, 1798.” Watson’s Address to the People of Great Britain was published in 1798, making the 1793 imprint date suspect — possibly an OCR error on the microfilm title page, or a retained imprint from an earlier edition of a different work bound with the pamphlet. The 1798 colophon date is almost certainly correct, as it matches Watson’s publication and the political context (the war with France, Malmesbury’s mission, the Irish Rebellion looming).

Structure and Chapter Breakdown

The pamphlet is a sustained point-by-point refutation of Watson’s Address, organised around specific quotations from Watson which Wakefield reproduces and then answers. It has no formal chapter divisions but falls into these thematic sections:

1. Opening: On the Independence of a Prelate (lines 80–505)

Wakefield opens with deference to Watson as “a Prelate of high and deserved reputation” but immediately raises a structural objection: a bishop with £2,000/year in ecclesiastical preferments cannot be considered an independent witness on matters of war and government reform. The contest is no longer between parties “in and out of place” but “between CORRUPTION and REFORMATION.” Every man who profits from existing inequalities is “an interested supporter of our existing forms.” Wakefield challenges the parallel between the early Christian preachers (whose opposition to worldly interest testified to their truth) and a modern ecclesiastical dignitary.

“The same effects would, I think, eventually take place… from a radical reformation in the representative part of our constitution. Can we then rationally expect, that the pensionaries of Church and State… should view, with a suitable discrimination and a clear disinterestedness, the imperfections of our establishment?”

Wakefield paints the choice he faces: on one hand, “persecution, and probably imprisonment, or even military execution” for speaking his mind; on the other, “from a subversion of the government by foreign or domestic violence, our portion would be, fearful expectation of those tremendous evils.” He quotes Virgil’s Fama, Juvenal, and Quintilian on sensus communis.

2. On Pitt’s Finance System (lines 560–777)

Watson had praised Pitt’s new system of raising the year’s expenses within the year (pay-as-you-go). Wakefield counters that the measure was adopted “not for its wisdom, or its justice; but because the funding scheme can be pursued no longer, from an absolute inability to raise the supplies of the year by any tax, that can be imposed on an impoverished and discontented people.” The national expenditure is “that mill-stone, which will infallibly sink them and their system together in perdition.”

3. The Income Inequality Argument — The Ground-Floor Metaphor (lines 625–777)

This is the pamphlet’s most celebrated passage. Wakefield takes Watson’s call for a tenth of every man’s property and demonstrates its regressive effect:

“Suppose now, my income to be £200, and his £2,000. I with my eight children can furnish ourselves with no more than the actual necessaries of food, raiment, and habitation, with our resources. Take from me a tenth part of this income, and you distress me beyond description: exact from him in the same proportion, and you abridge nothing but extravagant superfluity, or immoderate accumulation.”

Wakefield then demolishes Watson’s architectural metaphor — that if all foundations sink uniformly, the building’s symmetry is preserved — with a savage rejoinder:

“For alas! the ground-floor of this grand and stable edifice, where myself, and my mess-mates of the swinish multitude, were regaling ourselves… on our cheese-parings and candles-ends; our ground-floor, I say, is sunk for ever in damps and darkness; only to make, forsooth! a more firm foundation for our aristocratical and prelatical superiors, who are frisking and feasting in the upper-rooms with unalloyed glee and their customary unconcern.”

4. On the People’s Supposed Willingness to Pay the Debt (lines 779–854)

Watson claimed he had met not a single farmer, tradesman, or manufacturer who would not rather pay the principal of the national debt than be harassed by taxes. Wakefield retorts that these people have no such principal to draw on: “Their income is successive, and precarious. We can contrive, but with difficulty, peradventure, to furnish a guinea for each month; but if you come upon us for twelve guineas, in a mass, we have them not.”

5. Five Arguments (numbered 1–5) That the Ministry Never Sincerely Wanted Peace (lines 878–1278)

Wakefield answers Watson’s claim that the war is now just and necessary, since France refuses terms:

Argument 1 (lines 922–981): The character of Pitt and Grenville — “self-opinionated, arrogant, audacious, defamatory, and despotical” — makes a sincere peace application to men they have called “the most horrible of monsters” psychologically impossible. “Is it to be endured, that our infatuation and intrigues shall force Holland into a crusade against her interest, deliver her up to an enraged enemy, and then from superabundant kindness strip her of all the means of restoration to prosperity, her foreign settlements, and retain them, truly! for our own indemnification and aggrandisement?”

Argument 2 (lines 986–1034): The ministry’s established conduct — overwhelming national debt, devastation of the human species (“the death of a fellow creature is no more to them, than the fall of an autumnal leaf”), sham plots, false alarms, visionary assassinations, persecution of fellow-citizens, military despotism in Ireland — proves they cannot suddenly become “benevolent, pacific, haters of bloodshed.”

Argument 3 (lines 1037–1075): The ministers had so reviled and insulted the French that no rational person could expect the French Republic to negotiate cordially with them.

Argument 4 (lines 1076–1108): The reappointment of Lord Malmesbury as negotiator was itself “a gross insult to the French nation.” His earlier published account of a private conversation, “impressed with the clearest marks of exaggeration, management, and diplomatic artifice,” made him “peculiarly obnoxious to his opponents.”

Argument 5 (lines 1131–1278): The tyrannical domestic measures of the ministry — “a diffusion of corrupt humours through every vein of the body politic” via posts, peerages, borough-mongers, loan-jobbers, military officers, pensioners — form so great a contrast to republican equality that Pitt cannot sincerely wish reconciliation. “Is it for such a crew to wish and promote a pacific communication with the French republic?” Wakefield invokes Achilles’ refusal of Hector’s pact (Iliad xxii, Pope’s translation): “No thought but rage, and never-ceasing strife, / Till death extinguish rage, and thought, and life.”

6. Argument 6: Peace Would Destroy the Ministry (lines 1280–1319)

Peace would expose the “real state of our finances” and strip ministers of their power, leaving them “naked to popular indignation.” They cannot be supposed desirous of accelerating the moment when “that gigantic monster of ministerial depravity, who has stalked abroad with such effrontery” will be “divested of his cloak by the gentle, but searching, energies of peace.”

7. On Military Capability and the Invasion Scare (lines 1360–1955)

Wakefield dismisses Watson’s confident assertion that France dare not invade. He notes that “60,000 or 70,000” French troops landing in England would mean the kingdom “would be lost for ever,” because the poverty of the lower orders would make any change desirable:

“I believe from my soul, that within three miles of the house, where I am writing these pages, there is a much greater number of starving, miserable human beings, the hopeless victims of penury and distress, than on any equal portion of ground through the habitable globe.”

He illustrates this with Aesop’s fable of the Sensible Ass (in Croxall’s version): the ass refuses to flee the enemy because it will carry panniers regardless of its master. “All the alteration, which they can feel, is, perhaps, in the name of their sovereign.”

Wakefield mocks the volunteer forces and militia through a comic dialogue: a city light-horseman finds himself impossibly far from the enemy, a supplementary militiaman is suddenly lame, a voluntary cavalier is seized by an unaccountable spasm, a provincial associator’s eyes are full of ashes. The Bishop’s bravado is compared to Lord Hawkesbury’s earlier boast of marching to Paris.

He argues that the French armies are not “a collection of the vagabonds and outcasts of society, mere profligate mercenaries” but “a selection from all orders of citizens by a rigorous requisition” — a fair representative of the whole community. The fact that they have not restored monarchy proves the republic’s popular support.

8. On Social Distinctions and Hereditary Aristocracy (lines 1957–2001)

To Watson’s question whether there ought to be no distinctions in society, Wakefield answers: no distinctions should prevail “but what arise from personal merit and public services.” Hereditary honours transmitted to undeserving children are “a stupidity, I should think, which few will be forward to defend.” An advocate for such inequality “shall receive no commendations at my hands. He deserves well neither of Humanity nor the Gospel.”

9. On Religion and the Church Establishment (lines 2002–2105)

Wakefield largely concurred with Watson’s remarks on religion where explicit, but then delivers a devastating attack on the church-state alliance:

“As to an ecclesiastical establishment in alliance with the state, or rather dependent on it, I consider such a confederacy as a monstrous solecism in Christian polity; — as a fraudulent usurpation over that liberty, with which Christ has made us free; — as an impious prostitution of the simplicity and sincerity of the Gospel… to secular and interested purposes; — in short, as the prominent characteristic of that antichristian tyranny, which is now ripe for summary vengeance and eternal extirpation.”

He calls archbishops, bishops, deans, canons, prebendaries “the despicable trumpery of priestcraft and superstition” but protests personal goodwill toward clergy. He doubts he could “lead a French infidel to accept the pure religion of the scriptures” more easily than “a bigotted superstitious Churchman,” since “conviction may be produced in less time, than prejudices can be untaught.” He invokes Timotheus the flute-player, who charged double fees from pupils trained by a previous master. “Men, who receive emoluments under a church establishment, may be as sincere believers as they please; but they have no right to demand from others an acceptance of their own conviction as disinterested and independent testimony.”

10. Refusal to Defend the Present Administration (lines 2113–2257)

To Watson’s call to defend the country against invasion, Wakefield declares himself not among the loyal: “My life and my books are all the personals, that I value: and neither of them (for I have not tasted lotus) shall be hazarded in defence of the present administration.” His post is his study:

“If the French come, they shall find me at my post, a watchful centinel in my proper box, MY STUDY, among the venerable dead: sometimes investigating the origin of man and primaeval history… sometimes musing with the divine professors of the tuneful art… and sometimes meditating with the men of Galilee on mortality and immortality.”

He contrasts “we men of peace” who see “a gleam of glory through the mist” of revolution, with those who “have brought us to this most alarming crisis” and should “buckle on their panoply in defence of monarchy against republicanism.”

11. Conclusion: The Proposal (lines 2378–2601)

Wakefield proposes an alternative to Watson’s calls for perseverance in war:

“Change your ministry; restore your constitution, correct your abuses, and calm your spirit: then… solicit peace; and, take my word for it, the French republic, so far from insisting on any concessions of humiliation and disgrace, will come forwards to embrace you.”

If even this should fail, “still the good effect of this advice will be an inestimable acquisition, A vigorous and generous, UNANIMITY among ourselves.” Without this experiment, the continuation of war is “a desperate profligacy in the ministers; and a ruinous infatuation in the people” — “BOLD IMPIETY.”

The pamphlet closes with a remarkable tribute to an unnamed man (possibly Charles James Fox, or perhaps a more radical figure — the text is ambiguous): “for diffusive political wisdom, gentleness of nature, and genuine philanthropy, unalloyed by a single particle of bitterness, animosity, or resentment, has not, and, perhaps, never had, a superior among his species.”

Colophon: Hackney, January 30th, 1798.

12. Advertisements: Wakefield’s Published Works (lines 2628–2884)

The pamphlet concludes with a 37-item advertisement of Wakefield’s own publications, sold by J. Cuthell, Middle-Row, Holborn. These include:

  • Silva Critica (5 parts, 1789–1795) — philological commentary on classical and sacred authors
  • Editions of Horace, Virgil (Georgics), and a 3-volume Lucretius with Bentley’s unpublished notes
  • The Evidences of Christianity (2nd ed., 1793)
  • Poemata (1776)
  • An Enquiry into the Opinions of Christian Writers of the First Centuries, concerning the Person of Jesus Christ (1784)
  • An Essay on Inspiration (1781)
  • A New Translation of St. Matthew, with a copious Commentary (1782)
  • Four Marks of Antichrist
  • Remarks on Dr. Horsley’s Ordination Sermon (1788)
  • A New Translation of those Parts only of the New Testament which are wrongly translated in our common Version
  • A Short Enquiry into the Expediency and Propriety of Public or Social Worship (3rd ed., 1792)
  • An Examination of Thomas Paine’s Age of Reason (2nd ed., 1794)
  • The Spirit of Christianity compared with the Spirit of the Times (1794)
  • Remarks on the General Orders given by the Duke of York to his Army (1794)
  • Editions of Gray’s Poems, Pope’s Works, Pope’s Iliad and Odyssey
  • A Letter to William Wilberforce on Vital Christianity (2nd ed., 1797)
  • A Letter to Jacob Bryant on the War of Troy (1797)

The Lucretius edition note protests that “the alleviation of his enormous expenses, from the patronage of professed scholars, universities, colleges, and public libraries, has been almost — NONE.”

Rhetorical Characteristics

The pamphlet is notable for its extraordinary learning deployed in the radical cause. Wakefield’s classical references include Virgil (Aeneid, Eclogues, Georgics), Juvenal, Quintilian, Homer (via Pope’s translation), Euripides (the satyr play), Horace, and Lucretius. Biblical references are woven throughout. The tone shifts between deferential courtesy toward Watson personally and savage condemnation of the ministry and the church establishment. The architectural metaphor of the sinking ground-floor, the comic dialogue of the volunteer soldiers, the fable of the Sensible Ass, and the declaration of his study as his battle-post are among the most vivid passages.
