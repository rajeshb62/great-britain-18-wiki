Postlethwayt — Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce, Vol. I (A–K)

Author: Malachy Postlethwayt (1707–1767) Title: The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce, Vol. I Edition: Fourth edition, London: W. Strahan, J. and F. Rivington, J. Hinton, et al., 1774 Format: Folio, 273,340 lines (~12 MB OCR text) Raw text: Vol. I Part of: postlethwayt-universal-dictionary (combined entry covering both volumes)

Contents

Volume I contains all the front matter of the work — the extended “General Idea of the Contents,” the Introduction, and two substantial Preliminary Discourses addressed to Parliament — followed by the alphabetical entries from A through the letter K (ending with KINCARDINSHIRE, KALI, and KENT). This volume carries the bulk of the work’s theoretical and analytical framework; the alphabetical entries in the second half of the alphabet are in postlethwayt-universal-dictionary-vol2.

Front Matter

”A General Idea of the Contents”

An extended analytical table of contents — approximately 100 numbered sections — that functions as a self-contained prospectus for the dictionary. It is divided across the following broad purposes:

For British traders — from the supreme merchant to every subordinate class of respectable traders; the practical conduct of commercial affairs.

For persons of distinction — who desire a political knowledge of trade and commerce, the nature of the national finances, and the political systems of commerce in the several states and empires of the world.

Commercial policy — the emolument arising from the labour, industry, and ingenuity of all commercial artists; the mutual advantage of Great Britain and her American plantations; the reciprocal benefit of Great Britain and Ireland; the landed and trading interests harmonised; the moneyed interest compared with the landed and trading — reductions of interest, stockjobbing, bubbling, and gaming as the destruction of trade and traders.

Public finance — the immensity of the national debts and tax-incumbrances and their destructive consequences to the trade of the kingdom; the qualifications of the British senator; the commercial constitution of parliament; the pernicious consequences of taxes on native commodities; the revenue from the Conquest to the reign of George III.

Trade theory and practice — importation and exportation in a practical and political light; the balance of trade; augmentation and diminution of coin; the several societies for the discovery of new arts, trades, and manufactures; the utility of the Royal Society to trade; the jurisdiction of the admiralty relative to commercial concerns; the office of ambassadors, residents, and consuls in foreign states; the computation of annuities on lives, leases, and reversions; commercial arithmetic and the foreign exchanges and their arbitration; the whole art of banking.

The Mercantile College — a proposed new institution for training young British merchants; also a new institution for the education of young persons of distinction for parliamentary business; the constitution of the several banks throughout Europe; the standard of gold and silver throughout Europe; the intrinsic value of foreign coins; trading in bullion to advantage.

Marine affairs and insurance — insurances of shipping and merchandise, with great variety of curious cases, including the political discussion of the point of injuring the ships and merchandise of enemies in time of war; foreign weights and measures; laws, customs, and usages of merchants regarding foreign and inland bills of exchange; freight, bills of lading, masters and owners of ships, charter parties, averages, barratries, bottomry, shipwrecks, demurrage; the business of the custom-house and its several departments; the Act of Navigation; account of the foreign shipping frequenting the ports of England.

London as the trading metropolis — the superior importance of the city of London and its commercial connections over the whole kingdom; the commercial power of the trading citizens over the nation on all state emergencies; the supreme dignity of London as a corporation, showing that the greatest families in the kingdom originally sprang from trading citizens who distinguished themselves as chief magistrates; the bad policy of want of harmony between the court and the city; anecdotes of eminent citizens; the incorporated city companies and the bodies of trade in Paris.

French commercial regulation — all the material French edicts, arrêts, and laws concerning the chief trades in France, extracted from Savary; the peculiar regulations of French trading corporations requiring a masterpiece of workmanship as proof of qualification; Colbert’s wise and political measures while superintendent of the commerce and navigation of France.

Circulation and credit — the circulating cash requisite to carry on the trade of a nation; the nature and effect of paper credit in commercial states and empires; the par and equation between gold and silver; Sir Isaac Newton’s representation on the necessity of lowering the price of guineas from 31s 6d to 21s; the state of the coin and coinage of England from the Revolution to George III; the practical arts of assaying gold and silver; the art of assaying gold and silver from their ores, with knowledge of all metallurgical operations to judge of mines and minerals upon true philosophical principles; subterranean treasures and the art of mineralogy.

Natural productions — the vegetable productions of nature as the great objects of traffic geographically and commercially described; philosophical experiments to judge of the quality of natural productions; the productions of art, including capital commodities and manufactures in other countries, to whet the invention of British artists; the judges, inspectors, and overseers of manufactures in France compared with the woollen manufactories of Great Britain, which are “shamefully neglected.”

World commerce — public fairs throughout Europe; the Asiatic and African caravans; the commerce of the Chinese and the East Indies; the Turkey, Arabian, and Persian trades; the African trade and the inland trade; monopolies in general.

Political arithmetic and public debt — the funds of Great Britain; the consequences of “the destructive practice of funding and borrowing in times of war”; the injurious effects of becoming tributaries to foreigners; the excessive dearness of all English commodities as the principal cause of this nation being undersold at all the markets in Europe by the French and Dutch; the connection between dear English commodities and universal smuggling.

The great ends of the work — to promote a happy and lasting commercial union between Great Britain and her American colonies “at this critical juncture”; to suggest ways and means whereby the commercial grievances may be relieved; to save the nation the future expense of engaging in the Continental Wars of Europe.

The Introduction

A philosophical essay in its own right — running over 100 lines — arguing that the exercise of trade and commerce is “the soul of the whole British empire.” Postlethwayt surveys world history to demonstrate that idleness and luxury bring ruin to great empires: the Assyrian monarchy, “degenerating into a libidinous and intemperate course of life,” was conquered by Cyrus the Great; the Persian monarchy was “over-run with ease, luxury, and riot”; Alexander the Great, “no sooner devoted himself to pleasures, and to soften himself in idleness and immoderate excess,” became “the scorn and contempt of his own soldiers”; the Roman monarchy fell when the Turks, “constantly inured to hardships and perpetual toil, found the others a people uneasy with their plenty.” Even Mahomet, “like a subtle legislator, enjoined it as a law, which even their very emperors were not to be exempted from — namely, to eat no more than the labour of his hands could purchase every day.”

Postlethwayt argues that commerce civilises: “What would our gentry, our nobility, or our sovereign be better than those of Barbary, was it not for our superiority of traffic and navigation?” He recounts the remarkable true story of a young German nobleman who, told by his prospective father-in-law that he would not give his daughter to a man without a manual trade, learned basket-making in six months — and when the wars of the Palatinate drove both families from their estates, maintained them in Holland by his craft. This story illustrates the contrast between German and English practice: “amongst their nobility, the younger of whole families are employed in their armies, there is rarely found one of them destitute of some manual art.”

The introduction draws extensively on Bishop Spratt’s History of the Royal Society to urge the English nobility and gentry to devote themselves to practical and experimental philosophy. Their country seats give them “the best opportunity and freedom of observation”; their “familiar way of conversing with their neighbourhood will always supply them with intelligence.” The introduction concludes with a passage on English naval greatness: “the English greatness will never be supported or increased in this age by any other wars but those by sea.”

Preliminary Discourse the First

“The Present Commercial Grievances of the Nation Delineated: With their Causes and Cure briefly attempted. Also Strictures upon the Ill State of the Finances of the Kingdom; and a Plan to cement a more interesting Commercial Union between Great Britain and her American Colonies. Humbly Addressed to the Parliament.”

A sustained economic argument — approximately 2,600 lines — that the excessive dearness of all English commodities at foreign markets is caused by the weight of the national debt and the “oppressions of taxes in consequence thereof.” The argument proceeds in several stages:

On the competitiveness of English manufactures — To sell abroad, English commodities must be as cheap as those of rival nations. The Dutch exempt their exports from all port-duties; France has nearly followed their example. Though the Statute of 8 Geo. I c. 15 (1722) removed port-duties on British manufactures, and drawbacks are allowed on inland duties, the compound effect of multiple taxes continues to burden English goods at their exportation. France has been able to undersell England in staple woollen manufactures by ten to twelve per cent since 1735, and by twelve to sixteen per cent after the Seven Years’ War.

On the cascade of taxes — A pane of glass costing one shilling before a penny-per-pound tax was immediately advanced to eighteen pence — “600 per cent advance on account of this tax.” A pair of shoes rose two shillings on account of a twopenny-per-pound leather tax — above 1,000 per cent. When Parliament added three shillings per barrel on beer (one farthing a pot), brewers and victuallers doubled the duty into their own pockets, and journeymen raised their wages threepence per day; the public paid above eight times what the government received. The conclusion: taxes on necessaries, multiplying through every tradesman’s hands in the chain of production, may enhance prices to the consumer at “five hundred, and sometimes even a thousand fold” above the original tax.

On the land tax — The four-shilling land tax in time of peace raises rents upon tenants, who reimburse themselves by advancing the price of the produce of their lands; the land tax also terminates ultimately on the consumer, just as all other taxes do.

On smuggling — The excessive dearness of English commodities is the chief cause of smuggling throughout Great Britain and her American colonies.

Proposed remedies — The work is intended to suggest ways and means to relieve the commercial grievances of the kingdom; one great end is to promote a commercial union with the American colonies that would reduce the need for Continental wars, which have hitherto contributed so greatly to the enhancement of taxes.

Preliminary Discourse the Second

“Animadversions on the Foundation laid for a more interesting Commercial Union between Great Britain and British America, by the Definitive Treaty of Peace concluded at Paris, 10th February 1763.”

Takes up from the first discourse to consider the terms of the Treaty of Paris and their implications for British-American commercial union. Addresses the national ferments caused by ministerial instability, arguing that ministers cannot govern comfortably while the people experience unredressed grievances. Urges that the post-war settlement provides an opportunity to put British-American commercial relations on a new and lasting footing, and that the nation should avoid future Continental entanglements which drain resources needed at home. The discourse also animadverts on the conduct of alliances with European potentates and the balance of benefit from the Seven Years’ War.

Alphabetical Entries: A through K

A

The letter A — the largest single section in the volume — opens with a cascade of minor terms, foreign coins, and weights and measures, followed by entries of mounting commercial importance:

ABASSI — a silver coin in Persia, described in shape and size with its current rate of exchange.

ACCEPTANCE — a term in the traffic of inland and foreign bills of exchange; the act of accepting a bill drawn upon a party, creating a legal obligation.

ACCOUNT / ACCOMPT — an extended treatment of all commercial accounts: account current, account of sales, account of charges, account in joint partnership.

ADMIRAL / ADMIRALTY — the office of admiral, the jurisdiction of the English Admiralty over commercial concerns at sea; the French Ordonnance de la Marine and its provisions for commercial jurisdiction; cognizance of prizes, wreck, bottomry disputes, and maritime contracts.

AFRICA — a major geographical and commercial survey of the continent, organised by region: West Africa (Guinea, the Gold Coast, the Slave Coast, the Kingdom of Benin, the Cape Verde Islands), North Africa (Barbary), and East Africa; trade in gold, ivory, gum, and slaves; the British African trade and the policy question of the inland trade; the Royal African Company and the separate traders; the Senegal River trade after the Treaty of Paris.

AGIO — a bank term for the premium at which bank money circulates above current coin; the varying agios at Amsterdam, Venice, Hamburg.

AMERICA — on the American plantations and their mutual advantage with Great Britain; the articles of commerce; the Treaty of Paris and the territorial changes; the policy of commercial union and the avoidance of measures that would drive the colonies toward independence.

ANNUITIES — the computation of annuities on lives, leases, and reversions; the parliamentary funds; tables of compound interest (five separate tables appearing in the middle of the volume).

ARABIA — the commerce of Arabia Felix, including the frankincense and coffee trades, the Red Sea route to India.

ASIENTO — the South Sea Company’s contract for supplying enslaved Africans to the Spanish American colonies; the history of the Asiento from its granting to Britain under the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) through its operation; the terms, including the annual ship (the “permission ship”), the factories at Porto Bello, Panama, and other ports; the disputes with Spain that led to the War of Jenkins’ Ear.

ARTS AND TRADES — of the several regulated trades and crafts in France; the French system of masters and journeymen; the masterpiece examination required for admission to corporate freedom.

B

BANK / BANKSTOCK — one of the longest and most detailed entries in the volume. Defines the three kinds of banks: (1) public banks such as Amsterdam, where magistrates manage depositors’ bullion and never lend it out (a magistrate at Amsterdam was sentenced to death for borrowing money for a single day); payments are by transfer on the bank’s books, commanding an agio of 3–6% above current money; (2) incorporated joint-stock banks such as the Bank of England, whose security rests on large sums lent to government on parliamentary faith, and which is praised for “exquisitely well kept” accounts “with a variety of checks” making fraud nearly impossible; (3) private bankers in Lombard Street, individuals or partnerships trading on their own stock.

The BANKSTOCK sub-entry is the most remarkable feature: a complete double-entry ledger for a model banking business, showing every account a banker would keep:

  • Opening capital: Cash £20,000, South Sea Stock at 115, India Stock at 187, Bank Stock at 139½
  • Loans on pledged stock at 5%
  • Bills of exchange: a specimen bill drawn on Clifford & Sons, Amsterdam, for 6,000 florins at 35s 4d per £, discounted at 4½% for 1 month 10 days
  • Bottomry: a loan to a Captain of an Indiaman at 40%
  • Privateer adventure: £5,000 for a ¼ share in the “Duke and Prince Frederic Privateer,” returning £30,000 in prize money
  • Gold and silver bullion at £3:17:10–11 per oz; pillar pieces of eight at 5s 5½d per oz
  • Lottery tickets, jewels, promissory notes — all with specimen DR/CR entries

The entry also contains a lengthy critique of land-bank schemes (“absurd and chimerical; land is immovable and cannot return capital like money”), notes on financing new inventions (especially after Royal Society certification), and a discussion of the difference between domestic and foreign banking (called “remitters” in England). Cross-references to banks at London, Amsterdam, Genoa, Venice, Hamburg, and Paris.

BILLS OF EXCHANGE — a comprehensive legal treatise — approximately 5,440 lines — covering the law and practice of bills across England, Scotland, France, the Holy Roman Empire, the Netherlands, and Italy:

  • English law: built around the statutes (particularly 9 & 10 Will. III) and leading cases including Jackson v. Pigot on negotiability
  • Scottish law: extensive treatment of acceptance, protest, re-exchange, horning and caption (the Scottish debt-enforcement process), annualrent, and prescription, with reported Scottish cases
  • French law: the complete Ordonnance de Commerce of 1673 (Tit. V, arts. I–XXVII), reproduced verbatim
  • Imperial cities of the Holy Roman Empire: Hamburgh (exchange rules at the Hamburgh Bank), Frankfurt (two imperial edicts of 1666 and 1676), Augsburg (14 articles on acceptance and usage), Leipzig/Saxony (Electoral decree of John George)
  • Amsterdam: negotiating bills at Amsterdam, the role of bank transfers, agio, and brokers
  • Bologna: 17 articles on Italian exchange practices

BOHEMIA — the commerce and productions of the kingdom of Bohemia; its glass manufactures and linen trade.

BOOK-KEEPING — the art of debtor and creditor “exhibited in a new concise light”; the method of double-entry accounts for a merchant’s business.

BOUNTIES — the British system of export bounties and fishery bounties; the herring bounty of 23 Geo. II c. 24 (£3 per annum per £100 employed, plus a ship bounty of £1 10s per ton); mandatory rendezvous at Shetland and Scotland; crew requirements; sworn certification on return.

BROKAGE / BROKERAGE — the fees payable to a broker for negotiating commercial transactions; the regulation of brokers in England and France.

BRUNSWICK-LUNENBURG — the duchy and its commerce; the cities of Hamburgh, Harburg, and Lunenburg and their trade.

C

CALLICOE-PRINTING — the trade in printed, painted, and stained calicoes; the statutory prohibitions against wearing East Indian, Chinese, and Persian printed silks and calicoes in Great Britain; the regulations for warehousing and re-exporting them; the rules for domestic calico printing.

CAPTAIN of Merchantmen — the duties, liabilities, and legal position of the master of a merchant vessel; his obligations in regard to cargo, crew, and owners; the laws of barratry.

CARIBBEE ISLANDS — the commerce of the British, French, Dutch, and Danish Caribbean colonies; sugar, rum, molasses, and the provisions trade.

COMPANIES — a major entry covering both the Livery Companies of the City of London and the great trading companies of England:

Livery Companies — the twelve principal companies in order of precedence, with their incorporation dates and founding monarchs: the Mercers (1393, Richard II), the Grocers (1345, Edward III, “formerly called pepperers”), the Drapers (Henry VI, tracing to Edward III’s admission of Flemish weavers), the Fishmongers (united 1536, Henry VIII, formerly two companies), the Goldsmiths, the Skinners (1327, Edward III), the Merchant Taylors (1501, Henry VII, “formerly called taylors and linnen-armourers”), the Haberdashers, the Salters, the Ironmongers, the Vintners, the Clothworkers. A second tier of companies is listed in full: Dyers, Brewers, Leathersellers, Pewterers, Barbers, Surgeons, Armourers, Bakers, and some fifty more, all noted as fraternities incorporated with charters of privilege.

Trading companies — from the Merchant Adventurers (Edward IV) through the Muscovy Company (Edward VI), the Turkey/Levant Company (Elizabeth I), the East India Company (Elizabeth I, 1600), the Virginia and West India Merchants, and the Greenland and Eastland companies. Postlethwayt notes the historical shift from exclusive companies to free trade: “experience hath shewn, that the trade of the nation has advanced, in proportion as monopolies have been laid aside.”

CUSTOM-HOUSE — among the longest entries in the entire dictionary, functioning as a complete administrative manual for the English customs service. Organised alphabetically (the article continues across multiple letters C through I), it covers:

Specimen forms (with originals from Southampton, 17 January 1730): a sufferance for passenger Moses Wharton on the Providence of London from Rotterdam (baggage: 30 ells plain hollands linen, 1½ lb Dutch wrought silk, 3 lb tea, an Indian damask gown — the dutiable items noted for entry, the prohibited items for prosecution); the sufferance entry book; an over-entry certificate for James Cross (33 lbs thrown silk, with detailed repayment calculation breaking down old subsidy, new subsidy, ⅓ subsidy, ⅔ subsidy, impost 1692); a tobacco damage certificate for Dennis Dove.

Book-keeping system: the Collector’s sufferance book, land-waiters’ report books, general pocket-books, post-entry books, separate registers for over-entries and damages; the rule against erasures (errors must be struck through and corrected alongside); the Surveyor’s monthly check of all ships cleared, with post-entries standing out six days after clearance.

Goods Inwards — importation laws, prohibited goods, landing hours, navigation acts. Goods Outwards — export procedures, re-export, drawbacks. Goods Coastwise — cockets, bonds, certificates. Fees — tables of lawful and prohibited fees, penalties for extortion. Fish Laws — herring, pilchard, cod, salmon; packing regulations. Bounty for the British White-Herring Fishery — the full statutory scheme from 23 Geo. II c. 24. Iron from American colonies — pig and bar iron import regulations, stamping, certificates.

E

EAST-INDIA COMPANY — a massive legal compendium: the complete statutory framework governing the United Company from William III through George II. Key provisions:

  • The company’s monopoly of trade “to all places between the Cape of Good Hope and Straits of Magellan for ever,” subject to redemption by Parliament on three years’ notice and repayment of £4,200,000 capital
  • Ships must post £2,500 security per 100 tons; goods must be sold publicly “by inch of candle”
  • Persons trading without authorisation forfeit “ship and goods, and double the value thereof”; British subjects found in the East Indies without lawful authority are guilty of “high crime and misdemeanor”
  • Prohibition on contributing to foreign East India companies (penalty: treble the value or one year’s imprisonment)
  • Wrought silks, Bengals, and printed calicoes of East Indian, Chinese, or Persian manufacture prohibited to be worn in Great Britain; may be imported only into London and re-exported
  • A specimen bond for the exportation of prohibited East India goods from the Custom-house of Southampton (1730), listing three pieces of Indian taffety, four Indian damask night-gowns, one piece of cherriderry, nine remnants of chints, seven pieces of pelongs

EXCHANGES (foreign) — the rates of exchange between London and all the principal commercial cities of Europe; the computation of arbitrated exchange; the nature of exchange as compared with the transport of specie.

G

GOODS INWARDS / GOODS OUTWARDS / GOODS COASTWISE — the detailed procedural rules governing the import, export, and coasting trade: landing and lading hours, the navigation acts, the cocket system, drawbacks and bounties.

GREENWICH HOSPITAL — its establishment, maintenance by sixpenny deductions from naval wages, and its provision for wounded and aged seamen.

GRONINGEN — one of the seven United Provinces; its commercial situation, agriculture, and trade.

GUINEA (Africa) — the two great regions of Guinea (Upper and Lower); the kingdoms of Benin, Bonny, Calabar, and the Slave Coast; the commerce in gold, ivory, gum, and enslaved Africans; the European trading forts and factories; the British African Company and the trade to the American plantations.

GUIENNE (France) — the province and its subdivisions: Quercy, Condomois, Armagnac, Gascony, Soules, and Labourd; the commerce in wine (Bordeaux), prunes, and wool.

GUM — a major entry on the several kinds of commercial gums: gum arabic, gum lac, gum olibanum, gum tragacanth, gum anime, gum elemi, and gum guaiac; their sources, uses in manufactures, and trade regulations; the methods for testing their quality by philosophical experiment.

GUNPOWDER — its composition, manufacture, and trade; the regulatory framework.

H

HAMBURGH — a major commercial entry: the city’s situation on the Elbe, its status as a free city, its banking and exchange rules, the Hamburgh Bank and the payment of exchange by bank transfers; the regulations for merchants resident in Hamburg; the treaty rights of English merchants.

HAMPSHIRE — its situation, agriculture, and commerce; its woollen manufactures and the Winchester cloth trade.

HANOVER — the Hanoverian dominions and their commerce; their connection with Great Britain through the Hanoverian succession.

HANSE-TOWNS — the history of the Hanseatic League; its original formation as a league among German trading cities; its treaties with England and other powers; its decline; the continuing privileges of the Hanseatic merchants in England.

HATS — a considerable article of commerce: the manufacture of English beaver and felt hats; the regulations on hat-making and the prohibition on exporting hatters’ materials to the American colonies (creating a colonial grievance); the French hat trade.

HAWKERS AND PEDLARS — persons who carry goods for sale from house to house or from market to market, without a fixed shop; the licensing system and the fees payable; penalties for unlicensed hawking.

HEMP — in the same class with flax; the cultivation and preparation of hemp in Russia and the Baltic; its vital importance for the naval stores supply; the statutory provisions for encouraging the growth of hemp in the American colonies.

HERRING-FISHERY — under the article FISHERIES (given at length): the British white-herring fishery on the Scottish coasts; the bounty system; the French and Dutch competition in the herring trade; the regulations for curing, packing, and exporting herrings; the buss fishery and the seasonal rendezvous.

HOLLAND (the province) — the commerce of North and South Holland; Amsterdam as the great entrepôt of European trade; the system of warehousing and transhipment; the fishing industry of Enkhuizen and other ports; the Bank of Amsterdam; the Dutch East India Company.

HOPS — a plant of the reptile kind whose flower is essential to the brewing of beer; the cultivation of hops in Kent and other counties; the trade in hops between England and the Continent; the statutory regulations on the sale of hops and the stamping of hops sacks.

HUDSON’S BAY — the Bay and Straits; the Hudson’s Bay Company and its exclusive trade in furs; the commerce in beaver, otter, marten, and other peltry; the Company’s posts and the routes of the inland trade with the native nations.

HUNGARY — a kingdom situate between 16 and 23 degrees of longitude; its commerce in cattle, wine, and mineral wealth; the mines of gold, silver, and copper; the fisheries of the Danube and the great lakes; note on the extraordinary cheapness of carp (“1,000 carp have been sold for a crown, and in some places less”).

HYPOTHECATION — of ships and salvage; the hypothecation of a ship before departure for a bottomry loan, giving the lender a lien on ship and cargo; the rules governing salvage of goods from a wreck.

I

ICELAND — an island belonging to Denmark; the fisheries of cod, herring, and halibut that attract English, Dutch, and other fishing vessels; the regulations on the Iceland fishery under English law.

IMPORTATION — the bringing into a kingdom of merchandise from foreign parts; the distinction between prohibited, dutiable, and duty-free imports; the navigation acts as the foundation of the importation system.

IMPOST — a tax or duty laid by the sovereign authority on goods imported or exported; the several kinds of imposts in England (subsidies, tonnage and poundage, additional duties).

INDIA TRADE — See EAST-INDIA TRADE, East-India Company.

INDIA-HOUSE OF SPAIN — the Casa de Contratación at Seville (later Cadiz) for the management of the Spanish colonial trade; its functions in licensing merchants, registering silver, and enforcing the laws of the Indies.

INDIAN ISLANDS — a survey of the East Indian islands including Java, Sumatra, the Moluccas (Ternate, Tidore, Banda), and the government structure of the Dutch East India Company in each.

INDOSTAN / THE EMPIRE OF THE GREAT MOGUL — a comprehensive geographical and commercial survey of the Mughal empire; the principal provinces and their products; the trade routes; the situation of the European trading nations (English, Dutch, French, Portuguese) in the subcontinent; the Mughal court and the conditions of trading at the imperial courts; Bengal, Bombay, and the routes of the coastal trade.

INSURANCE / ASSURANCE — one of the longest entries in the dictionary:

Definition and specimen policy: a complete marine policy for a voyage from St. Petersburgh to London on the ship William and Mary, specifying the full perils clause (seas, men of war, fire, enemies, pirates, rovers, thieves, jettisons, letters of mart, surprisals, arrests, restraints, barratry of the master and mariners, “and all other perils, losses, and misfortunes”), the sue-and-labour clause, the Lombard Street standard clause, the convoy warranty clause, the premium and subscription block, and average clauses (corn and fish free from average unless general or ship stranded; sugar, tobacco, hemp, flax, hides and skins free from average under 5%; other goods free under 3%).

Premiums and abatements: premiums running “very high, sometimes 30 or 40 per cent” when a ship has been long missing; a customary 2% abatement at loss settlement; table showing net receipts at each premium level (at 10% → insured receives 88; at 40% → receives 53).

The incorporation of the insurance companies (1720): the Royal Exchange Assurance and London Assurance companies; their offer of £600,000 toward the civil list debt in exchange for charters; the 6 Geo. I c. 18 creating exclusive corporate rights; shares transferable; forgery of policies made felony without benefit of clergy.

Legal cases (20+): Da Costa v. Scanderet (fraudulent concealment of intelligence), Knight v. Cambridge (barratry), Bond v. Gonfale (1704, Holt CJ on convoy warranty), Green v. Young (embargo conversion to fire-ship), Goddard v. Garrett (no interest → void), Harman v. Vanhatton (bottomry + insurance both recoverable), Newnham, Perkins and Stoakes (1678, the Mayflower-ketch fraud where a master scuttled his own ship).

INVOICE — an account of merchandise shipped or to be shipped, specifying the particulars of each article, its quality, quantity, and price; specimen invoice forms.

IRELAND — a survey of the island’s geography, commerce, and trade with Great Britain; the staple exports (wool, linen, provisions); the restrictions on Irish trade imposed by English statute; the reciprocal benefit of Britain and Ireland.

IRON — the least heavy of all metals except tin; the production of iron in Britain, Sweden, Russia, and the American colonies; the bar iron and pig iron trades; the statutory provisions for encouraging iron imports from the colonies (to discourage smelting in America and preserve the naval stores supply).

J

JAPAN / JAPON — a great country consisting of several large islands; the Dutch East India Company’s exclusive access to Japanese trade (through Nagasaki and the Deshima island factory); the peculiarities of the trade; the commodities exchanged; the prohibition on Christian missionaries and the extraordinary constraints placed on Dutch traders as the price of access.

JERSEY — an island in the English Channel; its particular privileges and their basis in ancient charters; the wool trade.

JETSAM — See Flotsam, and Lagan.

K

KALI — a grey salt brought from Alicant and the Levant; its uses in the manufacture of glass and soap.

KENT — bounded with Sussex on the south-west, Surrey on the north; its hops, wool, and maritime trade; the ports of Dover, Deal, and the Cinque Ports.

KINCARDINSHIRE — a county of Scotland, its situation and trade.

Annuity and Interest Tables

Embedded within the volume are five tables of compound interest, a table of the value of annuities on lives, and conversion tables for the computation of leases and reversions — practical tools for the merchant and financier that Postlethwayt considered essential parts of a complete commercial reference.

Material Articles Index (Letter A)

The volume closes with the beginning of the “Material Articles” index — a condensed digest of the most commercially important entries, arranged alphabetically. The Letter A section of this index lists the key articles under A and directs readers to the relevant full entries.
