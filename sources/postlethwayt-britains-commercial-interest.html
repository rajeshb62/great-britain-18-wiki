Postlethwayt — Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved (1757)

Author: Malachy Postlethwayt (1707–1767)
Title: Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved; In a Series of Dissertations on Several Important Branches of her Trade and Police: Containing a Candid Enquiry into the Secret Causes of the Present Misfortunes of the Nation, with Proposals for their Remedy. Also the Great Advantages which would accrue to this Kingdom from an Union with Ireland
Edition: First edition, printed for D. Browne (London), 1757
Format: Two volumes; Vol. I only in this source (548 pp.)
OCLC: 6290741
Raw text: postlethwayt-britains-commercial-interest.txt (21,004 lines, 743 KB)

Summary

Postlethwayt’s Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved (1757) is a collection of nineteen political-economic dissertations written during the early years of the Seven Years’ War. Unlike his earlier prospectus for the Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce (1749), this is a full-length policy treatise — 548 pages — offering Postlethwayt’s analysis of Britain’s commercial decline and his proposed remedies. The entire work is framed as a sustained address to John Manners, Marquess of Granby (later Duke of Rutland), a prominent military commander and statesman.

The preface opens by declaring that “the nation is, at present, reduced to a very melancholy state” and that Britain is “destitute, at this juncture, of all maritime allies.” The central argument is structural: high domestic costs — driven by under-cultivation, high rents, and excessive taxes — undermine Britain’s ability to compete with France. Postlethwayt argues that the only path to recovery is agricultural improvement, legislative union with Ireland (following the Scottish model of 1707), colonial integration, and fiscal discipline.

Contents

Volume I

Dissertation I (pp. 1–55) — Of the Landed Interest, in relation to the cultivation of waste land

Postlethwayt opens his treatise with the fundamental proposition that “every essential object of traffic and commerce, requisite to the sustenance and convenience of human life being produced by the earth,” the more land that is cultivated, the greater the national prosperity. His core argument is that cultivating waste lands will lower grain prices, reduce the cost of labour and manufactures, and restore Britain’s competitive position against foreign rivals.

Contains the book’s most important quantitative calculation: England contains approximately 62,000 square miles of productive land (after deducting “irregularity, towns and rivers” from a gross area of 92,800), of which only about 31,000 square miles are currently under cultivation. Postlethwayt calculates that 86 square miles of new land should be brought into cultivation each year just to keep pace with the natural increase of population.

A central paradox explored: high rents appear to benefit landlords in the short term but ultimately impoverish them, because they drive up the price of everything the landlord must buy — labour, manufactures, provisions. A gentleman with £1,000 per annum who spends £500 and saves £500 “will be deceived” if he thinks rising rents increase his real wealth, because the purchasing power of his savings falls proportionally. Postlethwayt calculates that a 20–30% fall in rents (from increased cultivation) would make everything correspondingly cheaper, so that “70 or 80 l. would purchase more” than 100 l. does at present — the landlord would actually be better off.

Dissertation II (pp. 56–73) — The Productions and trade of England compared with those of Scotland and Ireland, and the British plantations

Extends the argument of Diss I by comparing Britain’s position with its constituent kingdoms and colonies. Postlethwayt argues that however great the advantages England has over its other territories, “when duly considered, they will be found to be less considerable than is commonly imagined” — because the same problems of under-cultivation and high costs affect all parts of the British dominions. The dissertations throughout enforce the theme that each part of the empire should serve the whole rather than compete internally.

Dissertation III (pp. 74–101) — A summary of various general principles of agriculture, founded on experience, for the improvement of lands

A practical agricultural manual embedded within the policy framework. Postlethwayt discusses drainage techniques for wet lands (“such as lye on a slope are drained by cutting trenches”) and distinguishes three types of wet land requiring different treatment. Covers soil improvement, crop rotation, and the management of waste grounds. This is the most “hands-on” section of the book, drawing on practical agricultural experience rather than political arithmetic.

Dissertation IV (pp. 102–123) — Farther considerations on the establishment of public granaries

Proposes state-managed grain stores modelled on French practice (“as recommended to the practice of France, by some of their ablest writers”). Postlethwayt notes that the French law forbidding merchants to keep corn above three years was well-intentioned but counter-productive — it prevented speculation that could stabilise prices across good and bad harvests. He argues that “three or four plentiful harvests running are no new thing” and that “great scarcities have not happened till after such repeated plenty,” making public granaries essential to prevent the cycle of glut and dearth.

Dissertation V (pp. 124–142) — Further considerations on trade, agriculture, and public granaries

Continues the argument for state intervention in grain markets. Postlethwayt addresses objections to his scheme and provides comparative examples from European practice.

Dissertation VI (pp. 143–172) — Farther considerations on the connections of Trade between Great Britain and Ireland and the British plantations

Analyses the trading relationships among the British Isles and American colonies. Postlethwayt argues that Ireland and the plantations should function as complementary parts of a single imperial economy, with each territory specialising in what it produces best. The current system of trade restrictions — particularly those against Irish exports — undermines this natural complementarity.

Dissertation VII (pp. 173–196) — Of such productions, manufactures, and trades, as England, Scotland, Ireland, and the British plantations should chiefly cultivate

A division-of-labour argument for specialisation within the British imperial system. Postlethwayt argues that the various parts of the empire should be directed toward productions in which they have natural advantages, so they “rival and compete with foreign nations, and not with each other.” This anticipates the classical argument for free trade within an imperial customs union.

Dissertation VIII (pp. 197–203) — Of the connection between the landed and trading interests of the kingdom

A brief but pointed treatment of the interdependence of agriculture and commerce. Postlethwayt argues that the supposed conflict between “landed” and “trading” interests is a fallacy — each depends on the other, and policies that benefit one at the expense of the other ultimately harm both.

Dissertation IX (pp. 204–244) — An abstract of some philosophic remarks, relating to the cultivation of land

Draws on philosophical and practical writings on agricultural improvement. Postlethwayt compiles observations from various authors to support his case that agricultural improvement is the foundation of national wealth.

Dissertation X (pp. 245–267) — The landed and trading interests connectively considered

Addresses the gentry and planters directly, urging them to adopt improved agricultural methods as a patriotic duty. Postlethwayt argues that “country gentlemen and planters may promote the benefit of their estates, in conjunction with that of the nation” — private profit and public good coincide when estates are efficiently managed.

Dissertation XI (pp. 268–290) — The union of Great Britain and Ireland, considered

Opens the major second-half theme of the book. Postlethwayt’s immediate motivation is revealing: “One motive to the drawing up of these papers is, the late great scarcity of grain, and the deplorable distress and calamity of the bulk of the people in consequence thereof” — this is the famine of 1756–57. He argues that the grain shortage is inseparable from the broader question of Irish union. A legislative union (modelled on the Anglo-Scottish union of 1707) would integrate Irish agriculture with British markets, solve the grain problem, and give both kingdoms a common interest in prosperity.

Dissertation XII (pp. 291–334) — The foregoing subject farther considered

A historical survey of proposals for Irish union from the reign of Queen Anne to the present. Postlethwayt draws on earlier writers who had urged union, noting that the arguments have been made repeatedly but never acted upon. The historical record — “from the beginning of the reign of Queen Anne to the present time” — shows that every generation has recognised the benefits of union but lacked the political will.

Dissertation XIII (pp. 335–376) — The union of England and Ireland continued

Focuses on the illicit wool trade from Ireland to France — the single most damaging leak in the British commercial system. Postlethwayt writes that “Long experience has shewn, that no laws hitherto enacted, however severe, have been sufficient to prevent the people of Ireland from running their raw wools to France, and their manufactured goods to foreign countries.” Only union can stop this, he argues, because it would make it “the interest of Ireland to discourage that practice” — substituting Irish commercial interest for British legislative coercion. The wool smuggling directly undermines the British textile industry, Britain’s most valuable export.

Dissertation XIV (pp. 377–400) — Of the union of England and Ireland continued

Examines two capital errors in British policy toward Ireland: 1) the Act to prohibit Irish cattle imports, and 2) the destruction of the woollen manufacture of that kingdom. Postlethwayt describes both as “destructive” and argues that “England has scarce ever exercised her power in commercial affairs but she has suffered for it.” The cattle acts (passed in the 1660s prohibiting Irish livestock from English markets) are now “generally allowed destructive.” The suppression of Irish woollen manufacturing (under William III) drove Irish wool to France, where it fed the very rival whose textile exports now competed with England’s.

Dissertation XV (pp. 401–420) — Of the union of the Isle of Man with England

Discusses the Isle of Man’s role as a smuggling entrepot. Postlethwayt writes that “it is too notorious to be doubted, that this Isle has many years been, and still continues to be, a common storehouse for all manner of foreign goods and merchandises that pay high duties in Great Britain or Ireland, or are prohibited to be imported into these kingdoms.” Its merchants have “constant supplies of large quantities of tobacco” and other contraband. Incorporation into the British customs system would close this loophole. (The Isle of Man was indeed brought under British customs control through the Isle of Man Purchase Act 1765, eight years after this work.)

Dissertation XVI (pp. 421–460) — A succinct view of the constitution of the British plantations in America

The most extensive treatment of the American colonies in the book. Postlethwayt categorises the colonies by their form of government:

  • Crown colonies — Nova Scotia, New Hampshire, the Jerseys, New York, Virginia, the two Carolinas, Bermuda, the Summer Islands, the Bahamas, Jamaica, Barbados, and the Leeward Islands
  • Proprietary colonies — Pennsylvania and Maryland
  • Charter governments — Massachusetts Bay (the chief of which is “the province of Massachusetts-Bay, commonly called New England”)

He analyses the causes of the colonies becoming “the present seat of war” (the Seven Years’ War in North America) and proposes measures to restore their “strength and stability.” The key problem: the colonies, left to their own devices, have developed in ways that undermine imperial unity, and the French have exploited these divisions.

Dissertation XVII (pp. 461–480) — Further considerations on the causes of the present state of our affairs in America

Argues explicitly for greater imperial cohesion. Postlethwayt writes that “our great aim tends towards such a union amongst all his Majesty’s dominions, as will promote the mutual strength and vigor, as well as the mutual prosperity of them all; for the happy general union that we would cement, is no less constitutional than commercial.” This is among the earliest systematic arguments for what would later be called imperial federation.

Dissertation XVIII (pp. 481–500) — The cause of the present state of our affairs in America farther considered

Contains the book’s most valuable trade data. Postlethwayt provides a detailed enumeration of colonial produce exported to Europe from the continental colonies:

“The produce of his majesty’s colonies on the continent of America, shipped to Europe, from South and North Carolina, Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, New-Jersey, New-York, Connecticut, Massachusetts-Bay, New-Hampshire, and Newfoundland, chiefly consisting of corn, rice, pitch, tar, turpentine, skins, furs, tobacco, timber, masts and yards, fish, oil, ships for sale, etc. are computed yearly to amount to the value of £1,500,000 sterling and upwards; and the freight hereof, many of them being bulky commodities, amounts to £350,000 sterling at least.”

This gives a concrete picture of the economic value of the mainland colonies on the eve of the Seven Years’ War — roughly £1.85 million annually in produce and freight.

Dissertation XIX (pp. 501–548) — A short review of the conduct of France, in relation to her North American colonies

The culminating dissertation, comparing French and British colonial administration. Postlethwayt examines the Crozat grant in Louisiana (the monopoly granted to Antoine Crozat in 1712), noting that France’s claim covered territory bounded by “New Mexico, and the lands of the English of Carolina, west and east; and by the river Illinois, and the gulf of Mexico, north and south.” He warns that the French claim “will take in more than two thirds of the gulf” if France means to claim all lands not actually possessed by Spain or England.

Postlethwayt argues that French colonial administration has been more effective than British — the French crown has invested systematically in its American colonies while Britain has left hers largely to their own devices. “How far the limits of the country the French intended to possess in North-America, before the war, might extend, was not known; and to what degree they design to carry their conquests, if they are able, no one will doubt.” The work ends with Britain at a critical juncture, needing to reform both its domestic economy and its imperial structure to meet the French challenge.

Historical Context

The book was published in 1757 — the third year of the Seven Years’ War, and arguably the lowest point of the war for Britain. The opening years had brought a catalogue of disasters: the loss of Minorca (May 1756), the execution of Admiral Byng (March 1757, shot “to encourage the others”), Braddock’s defeat on the Monongahela (July 1755), the fall of Oswego (August 1756), and the fall of Fort William Henry (August 1757). The ministry was unstable — the Newcastle ministry had fallen in November 1756, replaced by the short-lived Devonshire-Pitt ministry, which itself collapsed in April 1757, leading to the formation of the Pitt-Newcastle coalition just as the book appeared.

Postlethwayt writes in the preface that the nation is “destitute, at this juncture, of all maritime allies” — a reference to the diplomatic revolution of 1756, in which France and Austria became allies, leaving Britain isolated in Europe. His argument that neutral trade benefits France more than it harms her reflects the specific problem of the 1750s: Spain, the Dutch Republic, and other neutrals carried French goods, effectively nullifying Britain’s naval superiority.

The work also engages with the Irish famine of 1756–57, the last major pre-Famine food crisis in Ireland, which killed tens of thousands. Postlethwayt’s opening of Diss XI — attributing his motive to “the late great scarcity of grain, and the deplorable distress and calamity of the bulk of the people” — places the book in the context of this immediate humanitarian crisis.

Key Themes

Agricultural Improvement as National Policy

Postlethwayt’s central claim is that Britain’s competitive decline stems from under-cultivation of land. His calculation that only half of England’s productive land is cultivated is the empirical foundation of the entire work. The solution is not mercantilist regulation but supply-side agricultural reform: more land in cultivation → cheaper food and raw materials → lower labour costs → cheaper manufactures → restored export competitiveness.

Imperial Specialisation vs. Internal Competition

The British empire’s various parts should not compete with each other but should be directed toward different productive activities based on comparative advantage. This argument appears in Diss VII and recurs throughout the Ireland-America dissertations.

The Case for Irish Union

The longest sustained argument in the book (Diss XI–XV, nearly 200 pages) is the case for legislative union between Great Britain and Ireland — 44 years before the Act of Union of 1801. Postlethwayt’s argument is thoroughly economic:

  • Prevents Irish wool smuggling to France
  • Integrates Irish agriculture into British markets
  • Removes the destructive trade restrictions that harm both kingdoms
  • Follows the successful model of Anglo-Scottish union (1707)

Colonial Administration and the French Threat

Postlethwayt’s analysis of the American colonies (Diss XVI–XIX) diagnoses a structural weakness: Britain has left its colonies to develop without systematic support, while France invests heavily in its American empire. The closing comparison of French vs. British colonial administration is a direct warning about the stakes of the Seven Years’ War.

Neutral Trade and Naval Power

A distinctive argument: neutral carriers in wartime do not preserve British commerce but instead undermine British naval superiority. By carrying French goods, neutral states “extend the commerce of France in time of war, more than she herself does in times of peace,” and enable French merchant ships “to be converted into private ships of war” and even become “a part of the royal navy of France.” The policy of neutral trade “augments the maritime force of France.”

Public Debt and Fiscal Reform

Postlethwayt argues for raising supplies to carry on wars within the year, without further increasing the public debts — a radical position in the midst of a war that would ultimately double the national debt. He references his own earlier tract on this subject and argues that perpetual taxes and mounting debt are a structural drag on British competitiveness.

Ministerial Instability

The preface alludes directly to “our ministerial distractions” which “have disturbed and distracted the whole nation, as well as quite alienated the regard of our foreign friends, and given spirit and influence to our enemies.” The work is partly an appeal to the Duke of Rutland to “become the happy instrument of uniting the wisest and the ablest men in the nation in the service of their king and country.”

Sources Referenced by Postlethwayt

  • William Petty — political arithmetic; the area and population calculations for England follow Petty’s methods
  • John Locke — on money, interest, and property
  • Charles Davenant — on the balance of trade and public revenue
  • Joshua Gee — The Trade and Navigation of Great Britain Considered (1729), a key influence on Postlethwayt’s colonial arguments
  • French writers on public granaries — unnamed but referenced in Diss IV
  • Bishop Fleetwood — Chronicon Preciosum (1707), referenced by Massie (not directly by Postlethwayt, but both writers draw on the same sources)
  • Various agricultural writers — the source of the practical farming advice in Diss III and IX

Significance

Postlethwayt’s Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved is the most comprehensive single treatise on British commercial policy published during the Seven Years’ War. It bridges the period between the older mercantilist writers (Child, Davenant, Gee) and the classical economists (Smith, who would cite the Universal Dictionary in the Wealth of Nations). Its arguments for agricultural improvement, Irish union, and imperial integration anticipate policies that would be enacted in the decades following — the Isle of Man Purchase Act (1765), the Free Trade movement, and the Act of Union with Ireland (1801).

The work is particularly valuable for its on-the-ground view of the British imperial crisis in 1757 — before Pitt’s victories at Plassey, Quebec, and Quiberon Bay transformed Britain’s fortunes. It captures Britain at its lowest point in the war, and its analysis of neutral trade, ministerial instability, and colonial vulnerability reflects genuine concerns that were widely shared by contemporaries.
