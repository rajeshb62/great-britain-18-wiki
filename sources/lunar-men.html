Uglow — The Lunar Men

Jenny Uglow — The Lunar Men: The Friends Who Made the Future 1730–1810. — Faber and Faber, London, 2002. — 588 pp. + plates.

A group biography of the Lunar Society of Birmingham — the informal circle of provincial manufacturers, doctors, clergymen, inventors, and gentlemen that gathered around the full moon from the 1760s to the 1790s. Uglow traces the intertwined lives of Erasmus Darwin, Matthew Boulton, James Watt, Josiah Wedgwood, Joseph Priestley, James Keir, William Small, William Withering, Richard Lovell Edgeworth, Thomas Day, John Whitehurst, and others as they drive the Industrial Revolution, the chemical revolution, the canal age, and the radical politics of the late eighteenth century.

The book is structured in five parts (Waxing, First through Fourth Quarter, and Waning), covering the chronological sweep from the birth of the first Lunatic (Erasmus Darwin in 1731) through the dispersal of the group after the Priestley Riots of 1791 and the deaths of the last survivors in the 1810s–1820s.

The text is 12,407 lines, extracted from the Faber and Faber ebook edition (2010).

Part I: WAXING

Prologue: ‘Surprise the world’

Introduces the Lunar Society as a small, informal club meeting on the Monday nearest the full moon. Uglow argues that this provincial group of manufacturers, professionals, and amateurs — unlike aristocrats or university men — collectively pushed British society over the threshold of modernity. Five core figures are introduced (Darwin, Boulton, Watt, Wedgwood, Priestley) alongside supporting characters (Keir, Whitehurst, Small, Withering, Edgeworth, Day). Sets the context of eighteenth-century scientific enthusiasm, collecting mania, and the overlap between popular science and industrial ambition.

Part II: FIRST QUARTER (Chapters 1–9)

1. Earth, Elston & Electricity

Erasmus Darwin’s birth at Elston, Nottinghamshire in 1731. Childhood in the severe winter of 1739–40. The Darwin family’s yeoman background and Dissenting milieu in the Midlands. Education: Chesterfield School, then St John’s College, Cambridge (1746), and medical study in Edinburgh (1750–52). Sets up practice in Lichfield (1756). Darwin’s early experiments in electricity, his stammer, his huge appetite for knowledge — and actual food — and his first marriage to Mary Howard in 1757.

2. Toys

Matthew Boulton’s birth in Birmingham (1728), son of a silver-stamper and buckle-maker. Boulton’s apprenticeship and inheritance of the family business. His marriage to Mary Robinson; her early death. Boulton’s expansion of the Soho Manufactory, founded in 1762 on Handsworth Heath outside Birmingham. Boulton’s flamboyant personality, business ambition, and skill at entertaining aristocratic customers and foreign visitors. His obsession with quality, his growing reputation as the leading toy-maker (metalwork, buttons, buckles, steel jewellery) in England.

3. Scotland

The Scottish Enlightenment’s influence on the Lunar circle. William Small’s arrival in Birmingham — Small had been Professor of Natural Philosophy at William and Mary College, Virginia, where he taught Thomas Jefferson. Small’s role as the key link between Darwin, Boulton, and the Birmingham circle. James Watt’s early life in Greenock, his ‘trading’ in mathematical instruments in Glasgow, his friendship with Joseph Black and discovery of latent heat. Small and Watt’s correspondence.

4. The Doctor’s Bag

Erasmus Darwin’s medical practice and reputation in Lichfield. His consultations, his bluntness with patients, and his innovative treatments. Darwin’s work on the evolution of the species in Zoonomia (1794–96), begun much earlier. His friendships with fellow doctors and his role as physician to the Lunar circle. Darwin the poet — translations of Linnaeus, The Botanic Garden, the ‘crushes’ and love letters of his romantic life after Mary’s death. Marriage to Elizabeth Pole (1781) and move to Derby.

5. Pots

Josiah Wedgwood’s birth in Burslem, Staffordshire (1730). The Wedgwood family of potters. Smallpox at age eleven left him with a permanently weakened leg. Apprenticeship to his brother Thomas. Partnership with Thomas Whieldon (1754–59), the leading Staffordshire potter. Experiments in glazes and clays. Wedgwood’s scientific approach to ceramics, his meticulous notebooks. The establishment of his own works at Ivy House, Burslem (1759). His marriage to Sarah Wedgwood (1764). The beginnings of Etruria (1769).

6. Heading for Soho

Boulton’s Soho Manufactory — the largest and most advanced factory in England. Boulton’s search for skilled craftsmen and his cultivation of aristocratic clients. Boulton’s ‘renaissance in metal’: ormolu vases, silverware, Sheffield plate, steel jewellery. The financial strains of the Manufactory. Boulton’s partnership with John Fothergill. His quest for a suitable engine partner — the search for a better steam engine that led to James Watt.

7. Ingenious Philosophers

The formation of the Lunar Society as a regular meeting group (1765–68). Key members: Small, Darwin, Boulton, Whitehurst, Keir. The eclectic experiments they conducted — electrical, pneumatic, chemical, mechanical. The informal, ludic character of their meetings: ‘a little philosophical laughing’. The role of correspondence: the Lunar men wrote constantly to each other, their letters forming the true record of the group’s collective mind.

8. Reaching Out

The Lunar Society’s expansion. Thomas Day enters the circle — Rousseau’s disciple, eccentric, wealthy. Day’s failed experiment raising two foundling girls to be his ideal wife (Sabrina and Lucretia), as described in Edgeworth’s memoirs. Richard Lovell Edgeworth joins — engineer, inventor, educational theorist, serial husband. Edgeworth’s many projects: land improvement, telegraphs, wheel carriages. The group grows: James Keir the chemist; John Whitehurst the clockmaker and geologist.

9. Steam

Watt’s improvement of the Newcomen engine. The separate condenser (1765), Watt’s decisive insight. The partnership of Watt and Boulton (1775). Financial backing from Boulton. Watt’s anxiety and perfectionism versus Boulton’s optimism and salesmanship. The first engines installed at Soho, Shropshire, and Cornwall. ‘I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have — Power.’ The patent battles. The engine’s revolutionary impact on mining, manufacturing, and transport.

Part III: SECOND QUARTER (Chapters 10–19)

10. They Build Canals

The canal mania of the 1760s–70s. James Brindley, the self-taught engineer. The Bridgewater Canal (1761) and its impact. The Grand Trunk Canal linking the Mersey, Trent, and Severn. Wedgwood’s strategic interest: canals meant cheap transport of clay and finished pottery. Wedgwood and Boulton as promoters of canal schemes. The Staffordshire and Worcestershire Canal. Erasmus Darwin as canal investor and poet of the ‘Navigation’ in The Botanic Garden.

11. Painting the Light

Joseph Wright of Derby — the painter of the Lunar circle. Wright’s great conversation pieces: ‘A Philosopher giving that Lecture on the Orrery’ (1766), ‘An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump’ (1768). Wright’s technique — the dramatic use of candlelight and moonlight. His portraits of Darwin, Whitehurst, and others. The connection between the Lunar circle’s experiments and Wright’s artistic vision of science as awe and mystery.

12. Magic & Mechanics

The overlap between science and mesmerism, entertainment and demonstration. Itinerant lecturers and electrical demonstrations. John Canton, Joseph Priestley’s early electrical work. The psychological dimension of the new science: where does experiment end and magic begin? The Lunar men’s performance of science for audiences.

13. Derbyshire Explorata

The Lunar men as explorers and field geologists. Whitehurst’s Inquiry into the Origin and Formation of the Earth (1778). Whitehurst’s work on the strata of Derbyshire, his systematic study of the Peak District. Exploration of the limestone caves (Mam Tor, Castleton, the Blue John mines). John Whitehurst’s clockwork: his famous astronomical clock for the King of Portugal.

14. Chemical Reactions

James Keir’s chemical experiments. Keir’s Dictionary of Chemistry (1789) and his translation of Macquer’s Chemical Dictionary. Keir’s glassworks at Stourbridge and later chemical works near Tipton. The group’s engagement with the new chemistry of Black, Lavoisier, and Priestley. Keir’s defence of phlogiston. The Lunar men’s debates over the nature of heat, air, and chemical combination.

15. Trials of Life

Personal lives of the Lunar men in the 1770s. Darwin’s grief after Mary’s death (1770) and his second courtship. Wedgwood’s leg — the amputation (1768) that nearly killed him. Edgeworth’s complicated domestic life — four wives, twenty-two children. Deaths and disruptions: Small’s early death (1775) deeply affected the group. Boulton’s loss of his first wife; his second marriage to Mary’s sister. The challenges of balancing philosophical friendship with business imperatives.

16. Rousseau & Romance

Thomas Day’s philosophical romance with the ideas of Rousseau. Day’s Emilius and Sophia (translation of Emile). His attempt to raise an ideal wife — the foundling experiments. The failure of the experiment, his eventual marriage to Esther Milnes. Edgeworth’s friendship with Day and their shared educational ideals. The tension between Rousseauan simplicity and the commercial-industrial drive of the other Lunar men.

17. Vases, Ormolu, Silver & Frogs

Boulton’s ormolu vases and the Soho metalwork. Boulton’s campaign to capture the luxury market. The Frog Service — Catherine the Great’s great dinner service from Wedgwood (1773–74), 952 pieces decorated with 1,244 views of British scenes and country houses. The rivalry and mutual support between Wedgwood and Boulton. The Portland Vase — Wedgwood’s four-year struggle to reproduce it in jasper.

18. Running the Show

The management of the great Lunar businesses. Boulton at Soho — managing 700+ workers, organising production from raw metal to finished goods. Wedgwood at Etruria — division of labour, discipline, pricing strategy, the showroom in London. Watt’s struggle to supervise engine installations across England, Scotland, and Cornwall. The financial organisation: partnership agreements, patent fees, customer credit, and cash flow.

19. Eddies

The late 1770s: slow years and personal setbacks. The American War’s impact on trade. Boulton’s financial difficulties. Watt’s depressive moods. Wedgwood’s expansion into new markets. Darwin’s literary ambitions taking shape. Priestley’s growing reputation as writer and theologian.

Part IV: THIRD QUARTER (Chapters 20–29)

20. Experiments on Air

Priestley’s discovery of ‘dephlogisticated air’ (oxygen) in August 1774, using a burning glass to heat mercuric oxide. The mouse experiment (March 1775). Priestley’s attachment to phlogiston theory. His publication Experiments and Observations on Different Kinds of Air (1774–77). The debate with Lavoisier over the nature of oxygen and the overthrow of phlogiston. Priestley’s refusal to abandon phlogiston — a rare instance of the circle’s experimental openness becoming intellectual obstinacy.

21. ‘What All the World Desires’

Boulton and Watt’s triumph with the rotative steam engine (1781). The sun-and-planet gear, the double-acting engine. William Murdoch’s contributions. The Cornish engine market. The boom in orders for draining mines, driving mills, and powering factories. Boulton’s aggressive marketing — sending Watt’s engines across England, Scotland, and to continental Europe. The patent extended by Act of Parliament (1775) to 1800.

22. ‘Bandy’d Like a Shuttlecock’

The struggle over Watt’s patent extension. Competitors, pirates, and imitators. The lawsuits — Boulton and Watt v. Bull, Hornblower, and others. Legal battles that nearly broke them. The role of the older Lunatic members in defending Watt’s priority. The social costs: Watt’s obsessive secretiveness, his resentment at the ‘bandying’ of his reputation in the press and Parliament.

23. Plants & Passions

Erasmus Darwin’s greatest literary work — The Botanic Garden (1791), a scientific poem in two parts: ‘The Economy of Vegetation’ and ‘The Loves of the Plants’. Darwin as poet and populariser of Linnaean botany — the sexual system of plants rendered in poetic form with considerable erotic charge. The controversy — conservative critics attacked Darwin for combining sex, science, and atheism. The poem’s spectacular success among progressive readers.

24. Conquering Cornwall

Boulton and Watt’s Cornish engine campaign. The Cornish mining district — deep tin and copper mines requiring powerful pumps. The competition with other engine builders. The ‘duty’ measurements (Smeaton’s improved engine tests). Watt’s perfectionism in engine design. The Cornwall agents and engineers. The personal costs: Watt’s absences from home, his rivalry with his own partner Murdoch.

25. Dull Earth & Shining Stones

Mineralogy and geology in the Lunar circle. Whitehurst’s geological map of Derbyshire. Wedgwood’s use of local clays and minerals. Keir’s work on glass and alkali manufacture. The Lunar men’s interest in mining, mineral rights, and the industrial applications of geological knowledge. Fossils, crystals, and the age of the earth — speculations that challenged biblical chronology.

26. Creative Copying

The relationship between art, design, and industry in the Lunar circle. Wedgwood’s reproduction of the Portland Vase — the crowning achievement of four years of experimentation in jasper-ware (1786–1790). Boulton’s copying of antique vases in ormolu and silver. The aesthetic of industrial production: did copying cheapen art or democratise beauty? The Lunar men saw no contradiction between mechanical reproduction and artistic achievement.

27. Sons & Daughters

The children of the Lunar men. The education and careers of the next generation: Josiah Wedgwood II, John Wedgwood, Tom Wedgwood. James Watt Jr., Gregory Watt. Robert Darwin (Erasmus’s son, Charles Darwin’s father). Maria Edgeworth, the novelist. The Lunar daughters: Anne Boulton, Susannah Darwin. The tension between the fathers’ ambitions for their children and the children’s own desires. Tom Wedgwood’s brilliant but tragic life.

28. Bringing on the Artists

The Lunar men as patrons of the arts. Wedgwood’s employment of John Flaxman, George Stubbs, and others to design pottery. Boulton’s commissioning of Robert Adam for architectural and decorative designs. Wright of Derby’s portraits of Lunar figures. Erasmus Darwin’s influence on William Blake (illustrations for The Botanic Garden). John Hill, the botanical artist. The Birmingham Academy of Arts. The Lunar circle as patrons bridging Enlightenment science and Romantic art.

29. From the Nation to the Land

The Lunar men and the American Revolution. Many of the group sympathised with the American colonists: Priestley, Price, and the radical politics of the 1770s–80s. Boulton and Watt’s correspondence with Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Jefferson. William Small’s American connections — his teaching of Jefferson at William and Mary. The larger political context: the loss of the American colonies, the reshaping of British commercial policy. The Lunar men’s focus on domestic improvement, canals, and manufactures as Britain rebuilt.

Part V: FOURTH QUARTER (Chapters 30–40)

30. Fire

The Birmingham Riots of 1791 (the ‘Priestley Riots’). Priestley’s political and religious radicalism — his support for the French Revolution, his Unitarianism, his attacks on the Church of England. The ‘Church and King’ mob’s three-day rampage. The destruction of Priestley’s house, laboratory, and library at Fair Hill. Priestley’s escape. The failure of local authorities to intervene. The psychological impact on the Lunar circle: the end of the group’s confident, open-handed Enlightenment optimism.

31. Boulton’s Blazing Skies

Birmingham in the aftermath of the riots. Boulton’s position: a loyalist manufacturer who nonetheless protected his friends. The retrenchment of the Liberal cause. The emergence of reaction — Pitt’s repression, the suspension of habeas corpus. Boulton’s continued business success, overshadowed by fear and division. The growing tension between the older members over politics.

32. The Linnaean Row

The quarrel within the Lunar circle over Linnaean classification and Darwin’s poetic treatment of botany. The moral and philosophical disagreements. Wedgwood’s discomfort with the erotic aspect of The Loves of the Plants. The wider public debate over sexual language in science. The cooling of some friendships.

33. Protecting Our Interests

The patent extension fight reaches its climax. Boulton’s lobbying of Parliament. The counter-petitions from engine users, particularly in Cornwall. The compromise: the patent extended but with lower royalty rates. The immense wealth that followed. Watt’s growing fortune and his retreat into land purchases and gentlemanly life at Heathfield Hall. The transformation of the inventor into a landed proprietor.

34. Family & Feeling

The personal lives of the Lunar circle in the 1790s. Wedgwood’s declining health. Darwin’s controversies — his materialist philosophy attacked as atheistic. Edgeworth’s educational writings. The marriages of the next generation. Withering’s medical career, his work on digitalis (foxglove) in treating dropsy and heart conditions. The growing distance between the Lunatics as age, politics, and geography separated them.

35. Grand Projects

The Lunar men’s visionary schemes. Darwin’s speculations on steamships, manned flight, and global weather control. Edgeworth’s telegraph, his land improvement in Ireland, his educational theories. Wedgwood’s plans for improving the conditions of the working classes. The canal network — the Lunar men’s dream of a Midlands connected by waterways to every corner of Britain. The cutting of the Grand Junction Canal.

36. Turning

The division of the Lunar circle. Priestley’s emigration to America (1794). Watt’s withdrawal from active business. Keir’s chemical works flourishing. Boulton’s Soho Mint — his greatest late achievement, using steam power for coin striking. The Boulton and Watt sons taking over the engine business. The meetings becoming rarer, the correspondence thinner.

37. Riots

The repression of the 1790s intensifies. Pitt’s Treason Trials (1794). The suspension of habeas corpus. The persecution of Dissenters and Reformers. The Lunar men’s experience of surveillance and suspicion. The end of their public optimism. The French Revolution’s betrayal of its ideals — terror, war, the execution of Lavoisier (1794), a chemist dear to the Lunar circle.

38. Handing On

The Lunar sons in the 1790s. James Watt Jr. in Paris — mingling with Jacobins and being denounced by Burke. Tom Wedgwood’s experimental work on photography (silver nitrate and light), though he never fixed the images. Josiah Wedgwood II’s management of Etruria. The younger generation’s different values, their distance from their fathers’ cooperative ideals. The closing of the Soho Manufactory gates to visitors: the end of open-handed display.

39. ‘Fire Engines & Sundry Works’

The later careers of the surviving Lunatics. Boulton’s Soho Mint — steam-driven coin presses that struck millions of coins for the East India Company, the Bank of England, and foreign governments. William Murdoch’s gas lighting — lighting Soho with coal gas (1802). Boulton and Murdoch’s Nelson medallion for Trafalgar veterans (1805). Watt’s retirement to Heathfield — his workshop, his hobbies, his melancholy.

40. ‘Time Is! Time Was! …’

The closing years. Boulton’s last campaign: building Birmingham’s first theatre (1807) against Nonconformist opposition. The deaths: Priestley 1804, Boulton 1809, Edgeworth 1817, Watt 1819, Keir 1820. Only Samuel Galton lived to see the Reform Act of 1832. Maria Edgeworth’s final visits to the families, carrying her father’s memoirs. The legacy: a world of steam, iron, canals, gaslight, and chemically-founded industry — all imagined and brought into being by a handful of friends meeting by the light of the full moon.

Part VI: WANING

Epilogue: ‘Fiddledum, Diddledum’

Uglow’s concluding reflections on the Lunar Society’s legacy. The men who had been provincial outsiders became, in retrospect, the architects of modern industrial Britain. The Lunar Society’s informal, cross-disciplinary, collaborative model — Uglow draws a parallel with the collaborative culture of the internet age. The Lunar men’s extraordinary combination of science, art, commerce, and friendship. Their belief in progress, in the power of knowledge to improve the human condition. The quiet end: by the 1820s, the last survivors had scattered and died, but the world they made — industrial, interconnected, powered by steam — was permanent.

Key Themes

  • Collaborative science across disciplines: The Lunar model — manufacturers, doctors, clergymen, and gentlemen meeting informally, sharing experiments and ideas across every branch of natural philosophy.
  • The marriage of science and industry: Every discovery was immediately tested for practical application — chemistry to pottery glazes, steam power to mining, electricity to medicine.
  • The Enlightenment in the provinces: The Lunar men showed that Britain’s intellectual ferment was not confined to London and Edinburgh — the Midlands became a powerhouse of innovation precisely because it was outside the old institutional structures.
  • The political dimension: The Lunar men were generally liberal, Dissenting, and sympathetic to the American and French Revolutions — Priestley’s house was destroyed for this. The book traces the shift from Enlightenment optimism to conservative reaction in the 1790s.
  • Science, art, and the sublime: Wright of Derby’s paintings, Wedgwood’s ceramics, Darwin’s poems — the Lunar men produced not just industrial technology but an aesthetic of the industrial sublime.
  • The human cost: The stress on Watt, the tragedy of Tom Wedgwood, the failure of Day’s idealistic schemes, Priestley’s exile — Uglow never loses sight of the personal costs of their ambitions.
  • The generation gap: The Lunar sons (James Watt Jr., Tom Wedgwood, the Edgeworth children) belonged to a different world — more political, more conflicted, more Romantic.
  • The Lunar legacy: The book positions the Lunar Society as the crucible of modern industrial Britain — the first time that scientific research, engineering, manufacturing, and commerce were systematically integrated by a group of friends.
