Campbell — Lives of the British Admirals, Vol. VII (New Edition)

John Campbell (1708–1775), continued by Dr. Berkenhout (to 1779), Henry Redhead Yorke (to 1780), and William Stevenson (further continuations). — Lives of the British Admirals: Containing an Accurate Naval History from the Earliest Periods. A New Edition, Revised, Corrected. The Lives of the Most Eminent Naval Commanders from the Time of Dr. Campbell to the above Period, by William Stevenson, Esq. — Volume VII. Printed for C. J. Barrington, Strand, and J. Harris, Corner of St. Paul’s Church-Yard, London, 1817.

This is Volume VII of the revised “New Edition” of Campbell’s work. It covers the climactic final years of the American Revolutionary War (1780–1783), Captain Cook’s third and final voyage (1776–1780), the interwar period (1782–1793), and the French Revolutionary Wars (1793–1802), ending with the Peace of Amiens.

The volume contains 576 page-files (OCR’d from the University of Minnesota copy, shelfmark C150 v.7, barcode 31951002166414x), extracted as 21,907 lines (~1 MB) of plain text.

Scope: This is the most expansive volume in terms of chronological range — it jumps from the American Revolution through the end of the 18th century and into the first years of the 19th. It is the only source in this wiki that covers the French Revolutionary Wars and the campaigns of Nelson, Howe, and Jervis.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAP. I — 1780 to the Conclusion of the American War (line 100, ~4,369 lines)

The climax of the American Revolutionary War from a British naval perspective. Opens with the gloomy prospects facing Britain at the beginning of 1780: a war with her revolted colonies, France and Spain now full participants against her. Key topics:

  • Naval operations in the West Indies (1780) — Admiral Rodney’s command, the Battle of Martinique, the French under de Guichen, the Anglo-French struggle for the sugar islands
  • The Armed Neutrality of the North (1780) — Catherine the Great’s League of Armed Neutrality, the diplomatic isolation of Britain, the Russian manifesto on free bottoms / free goods
  • The relief of Gibraltar — the protracted Spanish siege, the British naval relief expeditions, the floating batteries
  • The capture of St. Eustatius (1781) — Rodney’s seizure of the Dutch island, the enormous prize haul (estimated at £4 million), the controversy over the looting and the diversion of effort
  • The Battle of the Chesapeake (1781) — Admiral Graves’s fleet action against de Grasse, the British failure to relieve Cornwallis, the strategic catastrophe that sealed the fate of Yorktown
  • The surrender of Cornwallis at Yorktown (October 1781) — the naval dimension of the defeat, the political fallout in London
  • The Battle of the Dogger Bank (1781) — the inconclusive Anglo-Dutch engagement in the North Sea, the last fleet action of the war
  • Operations in the East Indies — Suffren’s campaign against Sir Edward Hughes, the series of five battles between the French and British squadrons in Indian waters (1782–1783)
  • The final naval operations, the peace negotiations, and the Treaty of Paris (1783) — the recognition of American independence, the territorial settlements

CHAP. II — Captain Cook’s Third and Final Voyage, 1776–1780 (line 4,469, ~2,494 lines)

A self-contained account of Captain James Cook’s third voyage of discovery, conducted between 1776 and 1780. This is the voyage that sought the Northwest Passage from the Pacific side. Key topics:

  • The departure from England (1776) with the Resolution and Discovery
  • The voyage south — the Cape of Good Hope, Kerguelen Island, New Zealand, the Society Islands
  • The discovery of the Hawaiian Islands (1778) — Cook’s naming them the Sandwich Islands, the first European contact
  • The exploration of the Northwest Coast of America — from Oregon to Alaska, the search for the Northwest Passage through the Bering Strait
  • The return to Hawaii (1779) — the fatal confrontation at Kealakekua Bay, the death of Cook (14 February 1779)
  • The aftermath — the retreat of the expedition under Captain Clerke, the final attempt at the Northwest Passage, the return to England (1780)
  • Cook’s legacy as a navigator, explorer, and commander

CHAP. III — Miscellaneous Events, 1782 to the French Revolutionary War (line 6,963, ~4,259 lines)

A transitional chapter covering the interwar period between the end of the American War and the outbreak of the French Revolutionary Wars. Key topics:

  • The casualties of the sea — an opening meditation on the perils of maritime life, the annual loss of British seamen
  • Naval administration in peacetime (1783–1793) — the post-war reduction of the fleet, the state of the dockyards, the political debates over naval expenditure
  • The development of naval technology — copper-bottoming of ships, improved signalling, navigational advances
  • The Nootka Sound crisis (1790) — the Spanish seizure of British ships at Nootka Sound, the British naval mobilisation, the diplomatic resolution that established British rights on the Northwest Coast
  • The Russian Armament (1791) — the Ochakov crisis, the British fleet’s demonstration against Russia
  • The state of the navy in 1792 — strength, manning, and readiness on the eve of the French Revolutionary Wars
  • The French Revolution (1789) — the causes and early events, the impact on British naval thinking
  • The deterioration of Anglo-French relations, the execution of Louis XVI, the declaration of war by France (February 1793)

CHAP. IV — The First French Revolutionary War to the Peace of Amiens (line 11,222, ~10,663 lines)

The largest chapter in the volume, covering the entire French Revolutionary War (1793–1802). Opens with a remarkable passage acknowledging the difficulty of writing impartially about the French Revolution — the author notes that “we are still too near the events of the revolution to view it with impartiality.” Key topics:

  • The outbreak of war (1793) — the causes and circumstances involving Britain in hostilities with Revolutionary France
  • The naval mobilisation — the expansion of the fleet, the manning problem, the recruitment of seamen
  • The Mediterranean campaign — Lord Hood’s occupation of Toulon (1793), the evacuation, the destruction of the French fleet
  • The Battle of the Glorious First of June (1794) — Lord Howe’s victory over the French Atlantic fleet, the capture of seven French ships of the line
  • The West Indies campaign — the capture of French islands, the devastating yellow fever losses
  • The Cape of Good Hope captured (1795) — the British seizure of the Dutch colony, securing the sea route to India
  • The Battle of Cape St. Vincent (1797) — Sir John Jervis’s victory over the Spanish fleet, Nelson’s brilliant boarding action
  • The mutinies at Spithead and the Nore (1797) — the great naval mutinies, their causes and resolution, the impact on naval discipline and conditions
  • The Battle of Camperdown (1797) — Admiral Duncan’s victory over the Dutch fleet, the destruction of the Batavian navy
  • Nelson’s Nile campaign (1798) — the Battle of the Nile (Aboukir Bay), the destruction of the French Mediterranean fleet, the isolation of Napoleon’s Egyptian expedition
  • The Second Coalition (1799) — the Mediterranean operations, the capture of Minorca and Malta
  • The Baltic campaign — Nelson’s victory at the Battle of Copenhagen (1801) , the dissolution of the Armed Neutrality
  • The Peace of Amiens (March 1802) — the temporary cessation of hostilities, the terms, and the uneasy peace that would break down within a year
  • The volume closes with the state of the navy at the Peace of Amiens

Vol. VII is the chronological successor to Vol. V — it picks up in 1780 (where Vol. V ended, around the early American Revolutionary War) and carries the narrative forward through the French Revolutionary Wars to the Peace of Amiens in 1802.

What this volume does NOT cover: The Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815), Trafalgar (1805), and the expedition against Algiers (1816) mentioned in the title. These would require Volume VIII.
