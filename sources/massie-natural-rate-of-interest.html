Massie — An Essay on the Natural Rate of Interest

Joseph Massie (d. 1784) — An Essay on the Governing Causes of the Natural Rate of Interest; Wherein the Sentiments of Sir William Petty and Mr. Locke, on that Head, are considered. — London: Printed for W. Owen, at Homer’s Head, near Temple Bar, 1750. Reprinted as Joseph Massie on the Natural Rate of Interest, 1750, edited by Jacob H. Hollander, The Lord Baltimore Press, Baltimore, 1912.

A seminal mid-18th-century economic pamphlet by the little-known but influential writer Joseph Massie. Massie was a prolific economic pamphleteer in the 1750s–1760s and compiled the celebrated Alphabetical and Chronological Index of Commercial Books and Pamphlets — a bibliography of 2,377 items by 1764 that remains the most important guide to English economic literature before Adam Smith. He proposed (unsuccessfully, to the Treasury in 1760) writing a “commercial history of Great Britain” and a treatise on the “elements of commerce illustrated by Applications,” but the present essay is his most important theoretical work.

The text is 2,350 lines, extracted from the 1912 Johns Hopkins reprint (which itself reproduces the original 1750 edition, 62 pages + preface, at “Price One Shilling”). The original was out of print by 1758.

Structure of the Essay

Preface (pp. iii–iv)

An apologia for questioning Locke’s authority on interest. Massie professes great veneration for Locke but argues that the subject “cannot be too particularly considered.”

1. Extracts from Petty and Locke (pp. 11–18)

Massie reproduces verbatim the key passages he intends to criticise:

From Sir William Petty’s Political Arithmetic (3rd ed., 1699):

  • Petty claims the natural fall of interest is “the Effect of the Increase of Money”

From John Locke’s Treatise on Interest (Works, 1740 ed., Vol. 2):

  • Locke defines “natural Use” as the rate “which the present Scarcity of [Money] makes it naturally at, upon an equal Distribution of it”
  • Interest is raised in two ways: (1) when Money is little in proportion to Debts; (2) when Money is little in proportion to Trade
  • The borrower’s Profit is what makes interest possible — and this profit increases “the less Quantity of Money, in Proportion to Trade”
  • The natural Value of Money (as yielding interest) depends on the whole Quantity of Money in proportion to the whole Trade
  • Locke’s historical example: in Queen Elizabeth’s time high interest coincided with thriving trade

2. Critique of Locke on Debts (pp. 19–28)

Massie’s first major argument: Locke’s position that debts govern interest is contradicted by the facts.

  • If debts raised interest, the enormous increase in both private and public debts since 1600 should have made interest far higher — not lower. National debt alone rose from 45 to 78 million pounds between 1740 and 1750.
  • “Publick Faith is like a Merchant, whose Credit falls and rises, as his Trade declines or prospers” — the government pays more in wartime due to risk (the 1745 rebellion), not debt quantity.
  • Key distinction: what looks like high interest for risky borrowers is partly a “Praemium of Risque” (insurance premium), not pure interest. Calling it interest is “as improper as it would be to call that Praemium Interest, which a Merchant gives an Insurer to have his Ship or Merchandise insured against the Dangers of the Sea or Enemies.”
  • Borrowing and lending are not caused by scarcity of money but by its unequal distribution — when wealth concentrates in few hands, the rich must lend to the industrious rather than employ it themselves.

3. Critique of Locke on the Quantity of Money (pp. 35–45)

Massie’s second critique: the proportion of money to trade does not govern interest either.

  • Locke’s own rule for how much money a country needs (one-fiftieth of labourers’ wages, one-fourth of landholders’ revenue, one-twentieth of brokers’ returns) is circular — these quantities themselves vary with the value of money.
  • The price-of-wheat test: Massie compiles a systematic table of wheat prices and interest rates across five periods:

  • If money’s proportion to trade governed interest (using wheat price as proxy), interest should have been only 2% in 1646–1665 — but it was actually 7%. In the 1666–1705 periods, when money was becoming scarcer relative to trade, interest should have risen — instead it fell. The rule is contradicted by every period except the earliest.
  • A reductio ad absurdum: if the rule held, interest would have been 14%, 28%, 56%, even 112% at earlier periods when wheat was cheaper. “No one can imagine any Man ever paid 112 l. for the Use of 100 l. for a Year.”

4. Massie’s Positive Theory: Interest is Governed by Profits of Trade (pp. 45–54)

Massie’s central argument, presented with a clear theoretical structure:

First principle: Interest is founded on the profit that borrowed money is capable of producing. “He who borrows 100 l. in Money of his Neighbour for a Year, by the Use of which in Trade or otherwise … his Neighbour could, and he may, make a Profit of 5 l. … is as much 105 l. in his Neighbour’s Debt, as he would have been if he had actually borrow’d so much Money.”

The rule: “The natural Rate of Interest is governed by the Profits of Trade to Particulars.” This is not just a statement about individual borrowers — it is a general rule: what matters is the profit rate typical merchants can make, not extraordinary profits from special skill.

The equitable division: Following Locke’s own suggestion (Extract 4), Massie accepts that lenders and borrowers divide profits equally — one half to the lender as interest, the other half to the borrower as the reward for his labour and risk.

Proof from cross-country comparison:

CountryInterest rateExplanation
Holland3%Most traders per capita, lowest profits
Great Britain4%Next-highest proportion of traders
France, Germany, Portugal5–6%Fewer traders relative to population
West & East Indies7–9%Higher risk from distance, climate
Turkey10–12%Fewest traders, highest profits

5. The Superior Causes: What Governs Profits of Trade (pp. 49–58)

Massie traces profits back to their root causes:

First law of profit: “The Profits of Trade in general, are governed by the Proportion which the Number of Traders bears to the Quantity of Trade.” More competitors chasing the same trade = lower profits = lower interest. Fewer traders (or more trade) = higher profits = higher interest.

Why profits have fallen across Europe: Three causes:

  1. Increase of traders: constant influx of new competitors, especially young men underselling established merchants to build their business
  2. Decrease of trade relative to traders: the rise of rival trading nations (France, Sweden, Denmark, etc.) competing with England and Holland for foreign trade
  3. The East-India trade: the drain of silver to Asia reduces the quantity of trade available to European merchants — “The Riches of the West-Indies, are by the trading Nations of Europe carried to the East-Indies, without Producing any Advantages to the Trade of Europe in general”

Second law — the ultimate cause of trade itself: “The first and greatest Motive to trade is Necessity” — the inability of any man to procure all the necessities of life by his own labour. But beyond this, the quantity of trade depends on government and security of property.

Massie’s explicit argument: it is Government, and not Nature, which makes nations industrious or idle. Free governments that preserve “Men’s private Rights” (like Holland and Britain) produce more traders per capita, lower profits, and lower interest — because men “extend their Cares to Futurity” only when they have “a Prospect of enjoying hereafter the Fruits of their present Industry.” Despotic governments (Turkey, Spain) produce fewer traders, higher profits, and higher interest.

6. On Public Credit and the National Debt (pp. 29–35)

An extended digression on why government borrowing rates are irrelevant to the natural rate of interest:

  • “All Reasoning about natural Interest from the Rate which the Government pays for Money, is, and unavoidably must be fallacious”
  • The government’s rate is “founded in Necessity” (which has no bounds), while natural interest is “founded in Profit” (which does)
  • In peacetime, the government can borrow below the natural rate (public faith is “more extensively known” than private security)
  • Counter-intuitive claim: paying off the national debt would raise interest, not lower it — because the portion owed to foreigners (Dutch, Swiss) must be paid in coin or bullion, draining money from trade

Significance in the History of Economic Thought

Massie’s essay ranks as an important element in the pre-Smithian discussion of profits. The German economist Wilhelm Roscher credited Massie with refuting the direct association of the rate of interest with the amount of money — a refutation that “two years later Hume repeated with greater simplicity but no more force.” Massie established that:

  1. Interest is a share of profit, not a monetary phenomenon
  2. The rate of profit is determined by the ratio of traders to trade (a rudimentary theory of competition)
  3. The ultimate determinants of commercial activity are institutional — the security of property under free government

The tract was out of print within eight years of publication (by 1758), suggesting limited contemporary circulation, though it was rediscovered and valued by later historians.
