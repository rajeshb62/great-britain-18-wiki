Thurston — History of the Growth of the Steam-Engine

Robert Henry Thurston (1839–1903) — A History of the Growth of the Steam-Engine. — The International Scientific Series. D. Appleton, New York, 1878.

A comprehensive history of the steam engine by the founding professor of mechanical engineering at Stevens Institute of Technology. Thurston was one of the leading engineers of his generation and writes with both technical authority and a broad historical vision, tracing the steam engine as an evolutionary “growth” rather than a series of isolated inventions. The work is illustrated with 123 figures and numerous portraits of inventors.

The text is 17,688 lines, extracted from the University of Michigan Engineering Library copy.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

CHAPTER I — The Steam-Engine as a Simple Machine (line 755, ~1,870 lines)

Section I — The Period of Speculation: Hero to Worcester, B.C. 200 to A.D. 1650

A survey of all attempts to apply the power of steam before anyone had succeeded in making a practically useful engine. Opens with Herbert Spencer’s observation about the far-reaching effects of inventions, and places the steam engine alongside religion and science as one of the great civilizing agents of history.

  • Hero of Alexandria (c. 200 B.C.) — describes several steam devices including the aeolipile (a rotating sphere driven by steam jets), a steam fountain, and a mechanism for opening temple doors by steam. Hero’s treatise on pneumatics survived and was studied by every later experimenter. Thurston reproduces Hero’s designs with their proportions and dimensions, noting that the aeolipile “is still a favorite means of exhibiting the reaction of steam” and that “the machinery used for opening the temple doors was the first recorded instance of the application of heat to produce motion for a useful purpose.”

  • William of Malmesbury (1125) — records a clock at Rheims operated by steam, but the account is obscure and unverifiable.

  • Hieronymus Cardan (1550) — recognised that a vacuum could be produced by condensing steam, a theoretical insight well ahead of its time.

  • Giovanni Battista della Porta (1601) — described an apparatus using steam pressure to raise a column of water, the first device in which the boiler was separated from the working cylinder. Thurston calls this “an essential step in the succession.”

  • Salomon de Caus (1615) — built a steam fountain using a single vessel heated directly, described in his Raison des Forces Mouvantes. The apparatus was simply a metal globe filled with water and heated; the steam pressure expelled the water in a jet.

  • Branca (1629) — invented a steam turbine consisting of a wheel with buckets struck by a jet of steam from a fixed nozzle, anticipating the principle of the modern steam turbine.

  • David Ramseye’s patent (1630) — the first English patent mentioning steam, covering the raising of water by fire.

  • Jacob Besson and Agostino Ramelli — early machanicians whose illustrated books of machinery influenced later inventors.

  • Florence Rivault (1605) — discovered that water confined in a bomb-shell and heated would explode the shell, recognising the immense force of confined steam.

Section II — The Period of Application: Worcester, Papin, and Savery (line 1364)

  • Edward Somerset, Second Marquis of Worcester (1663) — built the first steam engine actually used for practical work, a “water-commanding engine” erected at Vauxhall for raising water. Thurston gives a detailed account of Worcester’s life — his immense wealth spent on experiments, his Civil War loyalty to the crown, his subsequent poverty and imprisonment. Worcester’s Century of Inventions describes a two-vessel apparatus with a separate boiler, working on the condensation principle. The cells and grooves remaining in the wall of Raglan Castle allow reconstruction of its arrangement. Worcester died poor and unsuccessful, “his widow, who lived until 1681, seemed to have become as confident as was Worcester himself that the invention had value.”

  • Denys Papin (1647–1712) — a French Huguenot who fled the persecution after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes. Became assistant to Huyghens in Paris, then worked with Robert Boyle in London, was elected to the Royal Society in 1680. Invented the double air-pump and the air-gun. Developed the “digester” for cooking bones, demonstrating the relationship between pressure and boiling temperature. In 1690, Papin invented the first steam-engine with a piston and cylinder — the fundamental form that became universal. A small quantity of water at the bottom of a vertical cylinder is heated, the steam raises a piston, the fire is removed and the steam condenses, and the atmospheric pressure forces the piston down, doing work. But Papin himself abandoned this design in favour of improving Savery’s inferior pump. “Papin, after having earned the honor of having invented the first steam-engine of the typical form which has since become so universally applied, forfeited that credit by his evident ignorance of its superiority.” He died in 1710 without seeing any of his devices practically successful.

  • Thomas Savery (c. 1650–1715) — inventor of “The Miner’s Friend.” Savery’s engine used steam pressure to force water upward from a mine, with no piston — the steam acted directly on the surface of the water. Described with technical precision by Savery himself, whom Thurston quotes at length. The engine was seriously limited: it could only raise water about 40 feet, it consumed enormous quantities of fuel, and the boilers were small (maximum 2.5 feet diameter). The danger of boiler explosion was real and terrifying. Savery’s boilermaker died by an exploding boiler. The engine was also inefficient because steam was used without expansion, and condensation in the cold receiver wasted enormous heat. After Savery’s death in 1716, Dr. Desaguliers improved the design with the two-way cock and the “rose” injection system, and Blakely introduced the automatic injection-valve. Smeaton’s duty trials in 1774 were the first scientific measurement of steam-engine performance.

CHAPTER II — The Steam-Engine as a Train of Mechanism (line 2613, ~1,600 lines)

The Modern Type of Engine as Developed by Newcomen, Beighton, and Smeaton

  • Thomas Newcomen (1663–1729) — a Dartmouth blacksmith and ironmonger who produced the first practical, self-acting steam-engine. Working with John Calley, Newcomen built his first engine at Dudley Castle, Staffordshire, in 1712 (sometimes dated 1705). The Newcomen engine combined Papin’s piston and cylinder with Savery’s separate boiler, added the beam and pump rod, and — crucially — made the valve-action automatic. The steam was admitted to the cylinder, the piston was raised by the steam pressure and a counterweight on the pump end of the beam; then cold water was injected into the cylinder, condensing the steam and creating a vacuum; the atmospheric pressure then drove the piston down, raising the pump rod. Newcomen’s great invention was the automatic valve gear, operated by the engine’s own motion through the “plug rod” and “hand gear.”

  • The first Dudley Castle engine had a cylinder 21 inches diameter and 7 feet 10 inches stroke, making 12 strokes per minute and raising 50 gallons of water from a depth of 51 yards. It cost £1,000 to build.

  • Henry Beighton improved the valve gear in 1718, adding the “cataract” to regulate the speed.

  • John Smeaton (1724–1792) — the great civil engineer who applied scientific measurement to the Newcomen engine. Smeaton rebuilt and improved engines across the British mining districts. His duty trials established the objective measurement of engine efficiency. He systematically experimented with different sizes, proportions, and valve timings. The “Smeaton duty” became the standard measure of engine performance — the foot-pounds of work produced per bushel of coal.

  • The defects of the Newcomen engine were inherent: the cylinder was heated and cooled every stroke, wasting enormous heat. The power was limited by the size of the cylinder. But Newcomen engines became the standard for British mine pumping, with hundreds erected across Cornwall and Newcastle through the 18th century. One engine was still running as late as 1850.

CHAPTER III — The Development of the Modern Steam-Engine (line ~4200, ~2,700 lines)

Section I — James Watt and his Contemporaries

  • James Watt (1736–1819) — born in Greenock, Scotland, of a family of merchants and engineers. His grandfather was a teacher of mathematics; his father was a ship-chandler and small-ware merchant. As a boy, Watt was delicate and studious, spending his time “drawing with his pencil, or turning his tools in his father’s workshop.” He learned his trade in London under a mathematical instrument maker, returning to Glasgow in 1756.

  • In 1757, Watt was appointed mathematical instrument maker to the University of Glasgow, where he made the acquaintance of Joseph Black (discoverer of latent heat) and other leading scientists.

  • The crucial moment (1763) — Watt was asked to repair a model Newcomen engine belonging to the university. The model, with a 2-inch cylinder, could only make a few strokes before stopping. Watt realised that the small cylinder had a disproportionately large surface area relative to its volume, causing excessive condensation. This set him thinking about the fundamental problem of heat waste in the Newcomen engine.

  • The separate condenser (1765) — while walking across Glasgow Green on a Sunday afternoon, Watt conceived the idea of condensing the steam in a separate vessel, connected to the cylinder but kept permanently cold, while the cylinder itself could be kept permanently hot. “The invention of the separate condenser was the great discovery of his life, and it may be questioned whether a more important mechanical invention was ever made by man.” He built a working model using a large brass syringe as a cylinder, with a tin canister immersed in cold water as the condenser. It worked immediately.

  • Watt’s other improvements: the steam-jacket (keeping the cylinder hot), the air-pump (removing uncondensed air and water from the condenser), the double-acting engine (steam acting on both sides of the piston, 1782), the expansion principle (cutting off steam early and allowing it to expand), the parallel motion (a linkage converting the beam’s arc into straight-line motion for the piston rod), the centrifugal governor (automatically regulating speed), the indicator (recording cylinder pressure throughout the stroke).

  • The partnership with Matthew Boulton (1775) — after years of struggle with the unreliable and impecunious Dr. Roebuck (who had taken a share of Watt’s patent), Watt finally met Matthew Boulton, the great Birmingham manufacturer. Boulton’s Soho manufactory was the most advanced engineering works in the world, employing over 1,000 men. Boulton took over Roebuck’s share, and Boulton & Watt became the most famous partnership in engineering history.

  • Watt’s patent of 1769 — carefully framed to cover the separate condenser and allied inventions. The patent was extended by Act of Parliament in 1775, after a famous struggle in which Boulton’s political influence proved decisive.

  • The rotative engine (1781–1783) — while the early Watt engines were all pumping engines, the rotative engine adapted steam power to driving mill machinery, opening up the vast market of manufacturing industry. The first rotative engines drove the Albion Mills in London (1786), a flour mill that “was considered the most complete establishment of its kind in the kingdom.”

  • The steam-engine indicator — Watt’s invention of the indicator (a small cylinder that recorded steam pressure at every point of the piston’s stroke) gave engineers a tool for understanding and improving engine performance that remained standard for 150 years.

  • Watt in social life: a member of the Lunar Society, friend of Erasmus Darwin, Joseph Priestley, and Josiah Wedgwood. His discovery of the composition of water (simultaneously with Cavendish) placed him among the leading scientists of his age.

  • Death of James Watt, 1819, at Heathfield, aged 83.

  • Jonathan Hornblower (1781) — invented a compound engine with two cylinders, using steam expanded first in a high-pressure cylinder and then in a low-pressure cylinder. Boulton & Watt opposed him as an infringer of their patent, and success eluded him.

  • William Bull and Richard Trevithick (later developers of high-pressure engines)

CHAPTER IV — The Modern Steam-Engine: The Second Period of Application, 1800–1850 — Steam Locomotion on Railways (line ~6900, ~2,500 lines)

  • Richard Trevithick (1771–1833) — a Cornish mining engineer who built the first high-pressure steam engines, dispensing with the condenser entirely. His engine of 1800 was the first to use the expansive force of steam at several times atmospheric pressure. Trevithick’s “puffing” engine exhausted into the chimney, creating the forced draught that became universal in locomotives.

  • In 1801, Trevithick built the first road locomotive, running it up a hill at Camborne with Captain Dick’s “puffer.” In 1803, his railway locomotive ran at Penydarren, hauling 10 tons of iron at 5 miles per hour. Trevithick pioneered the use of cast iron for boiler construction, but his engines were too far ahead of the metallurgy of the time.

  • Oliver Evans (1755–1819) — the American pioneer who built high-pressure engines in the United States, including the Oruktor Amphibolos, a steam dredging machine.

CHAPTER V — Steam Applied to Navigation (line ~9400, ~1,800 lines)

  • Papin’s early experiments — Papin had proposed steam navigation as early as 1690, but technical limitations prevented success.

  • The Marquis de Jouffroy (1776) — built a steamboat that navigated the Doubs in France.

  • John Fitch (1787–1790) — built and operated steamboats in the United States, establishing a regular service on the Delaware River. His boats used a bank of oars rather than paddle wheels.

  • William Symington (1788) — built a steamboat on the Forth and Clyde Canal, using a double-acting engine driving paddle wheels. His Charlotte Dundas (1801) towed two 70-ton barges 19 miles in 6 hours on the canal, “a performance which conclusively demonstrated the utility of steam-navigation.”

  • Robert Fulton (1807) — built the Clermont, which ran from New York to Albany on the Hudson River, “the first commercially successful steamboat in the world.” Thurston gives Fulton credit for persistence and practical engineering rather than originality.

  • The development of ocean steam navigation — the Savannah crossed the Atlantic in 26 days in 1819, the Sirius and Great Western in 1838.

CHAPTER VI — The Modern Steam-Engine: The Second Period of Application, 1800–1850 — The Stationary Engine (line ~11200, ~1,800 lines)

  • The expansion of the steam engine into every branch of manufacturing: cotton mills, ironworks, breweries, flour mills
  • The Cornish engine — the most efficient type of simple pumping engine, developed by Trevithick and others for the deep Cornish copper and tin mines
  • The development of the marine engine — the side-lever engine, the oscillating engine, the direct-acting engine
  • The invention of the steam-hammer by James Nasmyth (1839) for forging large iron shafts
  • The development of the surface condenser and superheating

CHAPTER VII — The Philosophy of the Steam-Engine (line ~13000, ~1,300 lines)

  • The scientific principles underlying the steam engine: the properties of steam, the laws of thermodynamics
  • The source of energy in coal — the sun’s energy stored through geological ages
  • The transformation of heat into work — the concept of the mechanical equivalent of heat
  • Count Rumford’s cannon-boring experiments establishing the convertibility of work into heat
  • James Prescott Joule’s determination of the mechanical equivalent of heat (423.9 kilogram-metres per heat-unit)
  • W. J. M. Rankine’s theoretical treatment of the steam engine as a heat-engine
  • The limits of efficiency — the Carnot cycle, the impossibility of converting all heat into work
  • The practical efficiencies attainable in different types of engines

CHAPTER VIII — The Steam-Engine of To-day (line ~16000, ~1,700 lines)

  • The modern types: the beam engine, the horizontal engine, the vertical engine
  • The compound engine and the triple-expansion engine — John Elder’s innovations in the compound marine engine
  • The development of the steam-boiler: from Watt’s simple cast-iron boiler to the modern multi-tubular, sectional, and water-tube boilers
  • The steam fire-engine — Latta’s first practical steam fire-engine of 1852
  • The agricultural steam-engine — the portable engine, the traction engine
  • The future of the steam-engine

Relationship to the Wiki

This is the first and most extensive technology / industrial history source in the wiki. It bridges the gap between the political and military history covered by the Campbell and Belsham volumes and the economic and social transformations of the Industrial Revolution. The Newcomen engine (1712) was contemporary with the War of the Spanish Succession and the Peace of Utrecht; Watt’s improvements (1760s–1790s) parallel the American Revolution, the French Revolution, and the wars covered in the Belsham and Campbell volumes. The steam engine was the single most important technological development of the 18th century, transforming British industry, transportation, and society in ways that the purely political sources do not describe.
