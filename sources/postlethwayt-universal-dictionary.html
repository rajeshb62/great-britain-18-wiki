Postlethwayt — The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce (4th ed., 1774)

Author: Malachy Postlethwayt (1707–1767)
Title: The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce: With Large Additions and Improvements, Adapting the Same to the Present State of British Affairs in America, Since the Last Treaty of Peace Made in the Year 1763. Summary

Postlethwayt’s Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce was the first comprehensive English commercial dictionary, published originally in 1751–1755 and modelled in part on Savary’s Dictionnaire universel de commerce (1723–1730). This fourth edition, published seven years after Postlethwayt’s death, represents the culmination of his life’s work — a massive reference work covering every aspect of British and international trade, manufacturing, commercial law, navigation, colonial administration, and economic policy. The list of 22 London booksellers who published this edition attests to its commercial importance, and Adam Smith cited the work in The Wealth of Nations.

The dictionary is far more than an alphabetical reference. It opens with an extensive “A General Idea of the Contents” — a ~100-section prospectus covering the entire scope of commercial knowledge, functioning almost as an independent treatise. Throughout the text, Postlethwayt weaves policy analysis, historical data, comparative commercial law (especially French edicts and arrêts), and practical guidance for merchants and manufacturers. The introduction also contains a remarkable philosophical essay on the relationship between commerce, civilisation, and national character.

The fourth edition is particularly valuable because it was updated to reflect the territorial changes of the Treaty of Paris (1763). Postlethwayt’s method was to retain his pre-war observations and add post-war remarks alongside them, enabling the reader “to judge from plain facts, the natural deductions drawn therefrom, of the different state and condition of the commerce and territories of the diverse powers concerned in the last war."

"A General Idea of the Contents” (Vol. I, front matter)

An extended analytical table of contents covering approximately 100 numbered sections that functions as an independent economic treatise. The full sequence, with Postlethwayt’s original numbering:

Purpose of the work (nos. 1–12): The dictionary is compiled to answer the following purposes — the peculiar benefit and advantage of British traders in general, from the supreme merchant to every subordinate class; for persons of distinction desirous of a political knowledge of trade; for those who would master the nature and constitution of the finances; for those who would acquire a knowledge of the political systems of commerce throughout the world; for those who would be acquainted with commercial and marine treaties between nations.

Commercial policy and imperial relations (nos. 13–25): Concerning the commercial policy of states relative to the mechanical and manufactural arts; the mutual advantage of Great Britain and her American plantations in diverse lights; the reciprocal benefit of Great Britain and Ireland; the landed and trading interests harmonized; the monied interest considered in various important views as compared with the landed and trading, including “reductions of interest, of stockjobbing, bubbling, and gaming, the destruction of trade and traders.”

Public finance and taxation (nos. 26–39): The constitution of trading and monied corporations, their use and abuse; the immensity of the national debts and tax-incumbrances, and “their destructive consequences to the trade of the kingdom, if not speedily prevented”; the qualifications of the British senator; the commercial constitution of parliament; the pernicious consequence of taxes on native commodities; the revenue from the Conquest to the reign of George III.

Trade theory and practice (nos. 40–57): Of importation and exportation in a practical and political light; the balance of trade; augmentation and diminution of coin; the several societies for the discovery of new arts, trades, and manufactures and their progress; the utility of the Royal Society to trade; the jurisdiction of the admiralty relative to commercial concerns; the office of ambassadors, residents, and consuls in foreign states; the computation of annuities on lives, leases, and reversions; commercial arithmetic and the foreign exchanges and their arbitration.

Banking, coinage, and credit (nos. 58–62): The new Mercantile College for training of young British merchants, and a new institution for the education of young persons of distinction for parliamentary business; the constitution of the several banks throughout Europe with illustrations; the standard of gold and silver throughout Europe with the intrinsic value of foreign coins; of trading in bullion, gold, and silver to advantage.

Marine affairs and insurance (nos. 63–73): Of insurances of shipping and merchandise, with “great variety of curious cases” and “the political discussion of the point of injuring the ships and merchandise of enemies in time of war”; foreign weights and measures; the laws, customs, and usages of merchants regarding foreign and inland bills of exchange; of freight, bills of lading, masters and owners of ships, charter parties, averages, barratries, bottomry, shipwrecks, demurrage, and the laws relating thereto; an ample representation of the business of the custom-house and its several departments; the Act of Navigation; an account of the foreign shipping frequenting the ports of England.

London and the commercial metropolis (nos. 74–81): The superior importance of the city of London as the trading metropolis; its commercial connections over the whole kingdom and its mercantile interest over the world; the supreme dignity of London as a corporation, showing “that the greatest families in the kingdom originally sprang from our trading citizens”; concerning the bad policy of want of harmony between the court and the city; anecdotes of the distinguishing services to the state of eminent citizens of London.

Comparative French commercial regulation (nos. 82–92): All the various material French edicts, arrêts, and laws extracted from Savary; the peculiar regulations of the French trading corporations; the strictness of their corporations in requiring proof of qualification by performance of a “masterpiece of workmanship”; their vigilance to manufacture goods of the best quality for foreign markets; Colbert’s wise and political measures while superintendent of the commerce and navigation of France.

Circulation and credit (nos. 93–94): On the circulating cash requisite to carry on the trade of a nation; the nature and effect of paper credit in commercial states and empires.

Coinage and metallurgy (nos. 95–102): The par and equation between gold and silver; Sir Isaac Newton’s representation upon the necessity of lowering the price of guineas from 31s 6d to 21s; the state of the coin and coinage of England from the Revolution to George III; the practical arts of assaying gold and silver; the art of assaying gold and silver from their ores, together with “the knowledge of all metallurgical operations to judge of mines and minerals of every kind”; of subterranean treasures and the art of mineralogy.

Natural productions and philosophy (nos. 103–113): Concerning the vegetable productions of nature as the great objects of traffic throughout the world, geographically and commercially described; variety of philosophical experiments to judge of the quality of natural productions; the productions of art reflecting great variety of capital commodities and manufactures, to whet the invention of British artists; the judges, inspectors, and overseers of the manufactures of France and the woollen manufactories of Great Britain, “which are shamefully neglected.”

World commerce (nos. 114–125): Colbert’s system; public fairs and caravans throughout Europe, Asia, and Africa; the commerce of the Chinese and the East-Indies in general; “the excessive cheapness of their arts, manufactures, and produce” and how all European nations are attracted to trade with them; the peculiarities of the Turkey, Arabian, and Persian trades; the African trade and the inland trade; monopolies in general.

Political arithmetic and public debt (nos. 126–140): Political arithmetic and its application to commercial affairs and finances; the funds of Great Britain; the consequences of “the destructive practice of funding and borrowing in times of war”; the injurious effects of becoming tributaries to foreigners by reason of the magnitude of the national debt; the excessive dearness of all English commodities and manufactures occasioned by taxes — “the principal cause of this nation being undersold at all the markets in Europe, by the French and Dutch”; the connection between dear English commodities and universal smuggling.

The great ends of the work (nos. 141–148): That the commercial grievances of England daily increase and the chief causes seem unattended to; that the performance is intended to suggest variety of ways and means whereby the commercial grievances may be relieved; that one great end is to promote “a happy and lasting Commercial Union between Great Britain and her American colonies at this critical juncture” (1774 — on the eve of the American Revolution); that the author conceives the nation cannot be of more service than “starting a design to save it the future expense of engaging in the Continental Wars of Europe, since they have hitherto contributed so greatly to the enhancement of taxes upon our trade in general.”

The Introduction: Commerce, Civilisation, and National Character

The introduction that follows the “General Idea” is a remarkable philosophical essay in its own right — running over 100 lines and covering the relationship between trade, industry, and national greatness through the lens of world history:

The moral foundations of commerce: Postlethwayt argues that “the exercise of trade and commerce is the soul of the whole British empire” and that “the arduous industry of traders [is] productive of the prosperity of the kingdom.” He draws a direct line from the diligence of commercial citizens to the greatness of the state — “commercial vigilance, activity, and industry, cannot be too much encouraged and inculcated.”

Historical warnings against luxury: A sweeping historical survey traces the fall of great empires to idleness and luxury: the Assyrian monarchy degenerating “into a libidinous and intemperate course of life” until conquered by Cyrus; the Persian monarchy “over-run with ease, luxury, and riot”; Alexander the Great who “no sooner devoted himself to pleasures, and to softer himself in idleness and immoderate excess” than he became “the scorn and contempt of his own soldiers”; the Roman monarchy, where the Turks “who had been constantly inured to hardships and perpetual toil, found the others a people uneasy with their plenty”; and even the Ottoman empire, where Mahomet himself “enjoined it as a law, which even their very emperors were not to be exempted from — namely, to eat no more than the labour of his hands could purchase every day.”

Commerce as the measure of civilisation: Postlethwayt asks pointedly: “What would our gentry, our nobility, or our sovereign be better than those of Barbary, was it not for our superiority of traffic and navigation?” He argues that commerce civilises — the Africans themselves could be “gradually nurtured in all the arts of humanity” through trade rather than slavery, and the failure to develop the African interior trade is one of Britain’s great missed opportunities: “What avails the insignificant advantage we have received by driving the French out of the river Senegal, in comparison to the great and important designs that have been proposed by the author, to enlarge and extend the whole of that commerce in times of peace?”

The nobility, gentry, and the ruling class: A substantial section of the introduction is devoted to the role of the landed aristocracy in commercial society. Postlethwayt relates the remarkable true story of a young German nobleman who, when asked by his prospective father-in-law what trade he had to support his daughter, was told “never to marry his daughter, though his heir, and would have two such great estates, but to a man that had a Manual Trade, by which he might subsist, if drove from his own country.” The young lord learned basket-making in six months and later, when the wars of the Palatinate drove both families from their estates, maintained both his father-in-law and his own family in Holland by making “white twig baskets” of such excellence that they became a general fashion. Postlethwayt uses this story to contrast German and English practices: “amongst their nobility, the younger of whole families are employed in their armies, there is rarely found one of them destitute of some manual art.”

The English gentry and experimental philosophy: Postlethwayt quotes extensively from Bishop Spratt’s History of the Royal Society to argue that the English nobility and gentry should devote themselves to practical and experimental philosophy. Their country seats, “being removed from the tumult of cities, give them the best opportunity and freedom of observation.” Their hospitality and “familiar way of conversing with their neighbourhood will always supply them with intelligence.” He urges: “When a general turn for the useful inventions, in regard to trade, has once taken root among our nobility and gentry, let them go on to the other kinds, which purpose the striking out of new mechanics.” This is a direct appeal to the landed class to become active participants in the commercial and technological improvement of the nation, not rentiers living off their estates.

The introduction concludes with a passage from Spratt about English naval power: “the English greatness will never be supported or increased in this age by any other wars but those by sea.” This maritime destiny places a special burden on the gentry and nobility to “enlighten and adorn” while “the other defend it.”

The City of London: Trading Metropolis, Corporation, and Livery Companies

The dictionary treats the City of London as a subject of extraordinary importance — no other English city or institution receives comparable treatment. The “General Idea of the Contents” devotes seven numbered sections (73–81) to the City, covering every dimension of its commercial and political power.

London as the Trading Metropolis

Postlethwayt describes “the superior importance of the city of London, as the trading metropolis” and its “commercial connections of its tradesmen and citizens over the whole kingdom, as well as its mercantile interest over the world.” The City’s significance is not merely economic — it exercises “the great influence of the trading citizens over the nation, on all state emergencies.” This is a political argument: the commercial classes of London are a national force whose weight is felt in war, finance, and the making of policy.

The Supreme Dignity of London as a Corporation

Postlethwayt asserts “the supreme dignity of London, as a corporation,” showing “that the greatest families in the kingdom originally sprang from our trading citizens, who have distinguished themselves as its chief magistrates.” This is a pointed argument about the social prestige of commerce: the greatest aristocratic families of England trace their origins to London merchants who served as Lord Mayor. Trade is not a demeaning occupation but the very source of the nation’s elite.

The Court and the City

A distinctive political argument appears in the warning against “the bad policy of want of harmony between the court and the city.” Postlethwayt emphasises “the weight the city has given, and ever will give, to the conduct of government, both at home and abroad, in support of the national power and dignity.” This reflects the concrete political reality of 18th-century London: the City’s financial power (controlling the national debt, the Bank of England, and the East India Company) gave it leverage over the Crown and ministry that no other interest could match. The dictionary includes “anecdotes of the distinguishing services to the state of many eminent citizens of London in commercial affairs.”

The Livery Companies: The Great Twelve

The COMPANIES entry in Vol. I provides a complete historical catalogue of the livery companies of the City of London, beginning with the principal twelve — their incorporation dates, founding monarchs, and the trades they represent:

  1. The Mercers (1393, Richard II) — “though then trading for the most part in stuffs of the native growth,” permitted to purchase £20 per annum in lands
  2. The Grocers (1345, Edward III) — “called pepperers before,” greatly inferior to what they now are at the time of incorporation
  3. The Drapers (1630, Henry VI) — “for the most part woollen,” a fraternity from Edward III’s time when he promoted the woollen manufacture by admitting Flemings and other nations “the free use of manual operation within his dominions, that so his subjects might learn the craft”
  4. The Fishmongers (united 1536, Henry VIII) — formerly two companies (stock-fishmongers and salt-fishmongers), “a vocation no less advantageous to this kingdom by their encouragement of the fishing trade”
  5. The Goldsmiths (Richard III) — “an ancient craft, for formerly those that sold worked likewise their own plate”
  6. The Skinners (1327, Edward III) — “first year of the reign,” made a brotherhood in Richard III’s reign
  7. The Merchant Taylors (1501, Henry VII) — formerly called “taylors and linnen-armourers,” first patent of arms 1480
  8. The Haberdashers (1447, Henry VI) — “formerly so called,” incorporated a brotherhood of St Catherine
  9. The Salters (1530, Henry VIII) — “a company of good esteem”
  10. The Ironmongers (1462, Edward IV) — incorporated when “the mines of our nation much improved”
  11. The Vintners (Edward III) — “formerly called wine tunners,” incorporated after Edward III conquered Normandy and “by that means engrossed most of the French vintage”
  12. The Clothworkers (Henry VIII) — incorporation time uncertain

The Remaining City Companies

Postlethwayt lists the remaining companies by name, noting they are “all of these are fraternities, and most of them incorporated, and have charters of privilege, and large immunities.” In full: the Dyers, Brewers, Leathersellers, Pewterers, Barbers, Surgeons, Armourers, White-Bakers, Wax-Chandlers, Tallow-Chandlers, Cutlers, Girdlers, Butchers, Sadlers, Carpenters, Cordwainers, Painters, Curriers, Masons, Plumbers, Innholders, Founders, Embroiderers, Poulterers, Cooks, Coopers, Bricklayers and Tylers, Bowyers, Fletchers, Blacksmiths, Joiners, Plaisterers, Weavers, Fruiterers, Scriveners, Bottlemakers, Horners, Stationers, Marblers, Wool-Packers, Farriers, Paviors, Lorimers or Loriners, Brown-Bakers, Wood-Mongers, Upholsterers, Turners, Glaziers, Clerks, Watermen, Apothecaries, and Silk-Throwsters.

Postlethwayt notes that “in the days of our forefathers, many of them were not known, not having brought their several trades and crafts to perfection, for many of which they were obliged to be beholden to strangers; but the natives of this kingdom being naturally ripe-witted, and of a toward genius, soon became arts-masters, and out-did their teachers; so that, at this day, no nation under heaven can exceed them (if the materials be alike) in all respects.”

The Trading Companies

The COMPANIES entry also traces the historical development of England’s great chartered trading companies — the bodies that first established England’s foreign commerce when private merchants operating “on their own bottom” were discouraged from risking their fortunes abroad. In chronological order:

  1. The Merchant Adventurers — incorporated by Edward IV; confirmed and enlarged by Queen Elizabeth
  2. The Merchants of Russia or Muscovy — incorporated by Edward VI, “greatly encouraged by Queen Mary,” confirmed by Elizabeth
  3. The Merchants of Elbing — incorporated by Elizabeth, “like a wise queen and patroness of trade and navigation, well knowing that by traffic alone the kingdom could be enriched”
  4. The Turkey Merchants or Levant Company — incorporated by Elizabeth, confirmed by James I with large additions
  5. The Merchants of Spain — incorporated by Elizabeth
  6. The East-India Merchants — incorporated by Elizabeth, Anno 1600
  7. The Eastland, Greenland, and French merchants — “settled in companies” in the latter end of Elizabeth and beginning of James I, “trading with great success, building many ships, continually employing several thousand persons”
  8. The Virginia or West-India Merchants — whose “great industry and sagacity tended to the first settling and improvement of our colonies in America”

Postlethwayt then notes the historical shift: “since the trade of this kingdom and the number of traders have increased, and the methods of assurance of shipping and merchandize, and navigation to all parts of the known world, have become familiar to us, these companies, in the opinion of most men, have been looked upon in the light of monopolies.” Their privileges were progressively reduced “in order to establish an absolutely free and general trade; and experience hath shewn, that the trade of the nation has advanced, in proportion as monopolies have been laid aside.” This is a striking endorsement of free trade from the leading commercial writer of the mid-century — though he notes the question of whether some exclusive companies (like the Royal Asiento) should continue is considered under the separate entries for each company.

London Compared to Paris

Postlethwayt draws a systematic comparison between the corporate bodies of London and Paris — “of its incorporated city companies; and of the bodies of trade in the city of Paris.” The French trading corporations were even more rigorously regulated, with strict admission requirements including the performance of a “masterpiece of workmanship” in the presence of sworn judges. This reflects the dictionary’s broader theme of French regulatory thoroughness vs. English neglect.

Sample Entries

EAST-INDIA COMPANY (Vol. I)

A massive legal compendium — one of the longest entries in the dictionary — consisting of the complete statutory framework governing the English East India Company, built around the statutes from William III through George II:

The company’s monopoly: The United Company’s exclusive trade is to “all places between the Cape of Good Hope and Straits of Magellan for ever,” subject to redemption by Parliament upon three years’ notice after 25 March 1780, and repayment of the capital stock of £4,200,000 and all arrears of annuities.

Security requirements: Ships must post £2,500 security for every 100 tons before sailing. Goods must be brought to a British port, unladen, and “openly and publicly sold by inch of candle” under forfeiture.

Penalties for interlopers: Persons going or trading to the East Indies without authorisation forfeit “ship and goods, and double the value thereof.” Any British subject found in the East Indies without lawful authority is guilty of “high crime and misdemeanor” and may be seized and brought to England. Trading under foreign commission carries a £500 forfeit.

Prohibition on foreign East India companies: British subjects may not contribute to or promote “any foreign company trading to the East-Indies, from any part of the Austrian Netherlands or any other place,” upon forfeiture of their share and treble the value. Non-disclosure of a foreign share carries treble the value or one year’s imprisonment.

Calicoes and silk restrictions: Wrought silks, Bengals, and stuffs mixed with silk or herba of East-Indian, Chinese, or Persian manufacture, and calicoes printed, painted, stained, or dyed there, were prohibited to be worn in Great Britain. They could be imported only into the port of London, secured in approved warehouses, and re-exported. A detailed warehouse-keeping regime is prescribed: keepers must enter every chest and bale in a book, transmit accounts on oath every six months to the customs commissioners, who lay them before Parliament.

The South Sea Company’s limited privilege: The South Sea Company could, by consent of the East India Company, send yearly to Madagascar four ships to take in negroes for Buenos Ayres — a revealing glimpse of the interlocking monopolies of the period.

Customs enforcement: A complete specimen bond for the exportation of prohibited East India goods, from the customs house of Southampton (1730), with the full form of the bond, the goods enumerated (three pieces of Indian taffety, four Indian damask night-gowns, one piece of cherriderry, nine remnants of chints, seven pieces of pelongs), and the conditions for its discharge upon production of a certificate from the chief magistrate or two known English merchants at the place of landing.

SHIPPING (Vol. II)

Dimensions, tonnage, and number of guns of men-of-war built in Spain, the Indies, France, England, and Genoa. The rules of the Spaniards, French, English, and Dutch regarding sailors and mariners, drawing on Don Geronimo de Uztáriz’s Theórica y Práctica de Comercio y de Marina (1724). The section also contains instructions and directions for building ships drawn from Spanish sources, including plans by Lieutenant-General Don Antonio Gastaneta.

NAVIGATION (Vol. II)

Of the practical art thereof in relation to ship-sailing. What a complete treatise of that art ought to contain, according to Sir William Petty: arithmetic in whole numbers, fractions, decimals, and logarithms; “vulgar, practical, mechanical geometry, performable by the scale and compass”; trigonometry, right-lined and spherical; how many stars are to be known; what instruments are best for use at sea, with the construction of them and the manner of using them.

COMPANIES (Vol. I)

The origin of the companies of the city of London — the principal twelve, the time of their incorporation and by which kings and queens. The Mercers (1393, Richard II); the Grocers, formerly called Pepperers (1345, Edward III); the Drapers (Henry VI, incorporating a fraternity from Edward III’s time when he promoted the woollen manufacture by “admitting the Flemings and other nations the free use of manual operation within his dominions, that his subjects might learn the craft”).

BANK / BANKSTOCK (Vol. I)

The BANK entry is an extended treatise on 18th-century banking — far more than a dictionary definition. It opens with Savary’s definition — “a traffic or commerce in money, which is remitted from place to place, from one city to another, by correspondents, and by means of bills of exchange” — followed by the classic etymology from Italian banca (bench), noting that a failed merchant’s bench was broken (banca rotta → bankrupt).

The three kinds of banks: (1) Public banks like the Bank of Amsterdam, managed by magistrates, where depositors’ bullion is never lent out — at Amsterdam a magistrate was sentenced to death for borrowing money for a single day; payments are by transfer on the bank’s books, commanding an agio of 3–6% above current money. (2) Incorporated joint-stock banks like the Bank of England, whose security rests on the great sums lent to government on parliamentary faith; the Bank is praised for keeping sufficient ready cash at all times, and its accounting described as “exquisitely well kept, with a variety of checks” making fraud nearly impossible. (3) Private bankers in Lombard Street, individuals or partnerships trading on their own stock and credit.

The Bank of Venice receives detailed treatment: state revenues of ~4,000,000 oz of silver per annum payable in bank money, all large bargains void if not paid in bank, the system’s breakdown when government overspent in wars and expanded transfers to ~1,600,000 oz without deposits, and the equilibrium restored by contracting transfers back.

The BANKSTOCK accounting model is the most remarkable feature: a complete double-entry ledger for a model domestic banking business, “delineated at one view,” showing every account a banker would keep:

  • Opening capital: Cash £20,000, South Sea Stock bought at 115, India Stock at 187, Bank Stock at 139½
  • Loans on pledged stock: £1,000 lent at 5% secured by £800 face value in Bank stock
  • Bills of exchange: A bill drawn on Clifford & Sons, Amsterdam, for 6,000 florins at 35s 4d per £ sterling, discounted at 4½% for 1 month 10 days
  • Bottomry: A loan to a Captain of an Indiaman at 40% (high-risk marine finance)
  • Privateer adventure: £5,000 expended for a ¼ share in the “Duke and Prince Frederic Privateer,” returning £30,000 in prize money — a 6× return
  • Gold and silver bullion: Buying/selling bar gold at £3:17:10–11 per oz, coin at £3:17:11; pillar pieces of eight at 5s 5½d per oz
  • Lottery tickets, jewels, promissory notes — all with specimen entries

The entry also contains a lengthy critique of land-bank schemes (absurd and chimerical; land is immovable and cannot return capital like money), notes on bankers financing new inventions (especially after Royal Society certification), and a discussion of the difference between domestic banking and foreign banking (called “remitters” in England). Cross-references to banks at London, Amsterdam, Genoa, Venice, Hamburg, and Paris.

INSURANCE / ASSURANCE (Vol. I)

A comprehensive article — one of the longest in the dictionary — covering both the practice and the law of marine and fire insurance, with a complete specimen marine policy and over 20 reported legal cases.

Definition: “Assurance, or Insurance — a security, or assurance, given, in consideration of a sum of money paid, in hand, of so much per cent, to an insurer, or assurer, to indemnify the insured from such losses as shall be specified in the policy of assurance.”

Specimen marine policy (the fullest form in the dictionary): A complete policy for a voyage from St. Petersburgh to London on the ship William and Mary, specifying the perils clause (seas, men of war, fire, enemies, pirates, rovers, thieves, jettisons, letters of mart and counter-mart, surprisals, taking at sea, arrests, restraints and detainments of all kings, princes, and people, barretry of the master and mariners, and all other perils, losses, and misfortunes), the sue-and-labour clause (assurers to contribute to defence and recovery), the Lombard Street standard clause (the policy to be as binding as “the surest writing or policy of assurance heretofore made in Lombard-street”), the convoy warranty clause in time of war, the premium and subscription block, and average clauses (corn and fish free from average unless general or ship stranded; sugar, tobacco, hemp, flax, hides and skins free from average under 5%; other goods free under 3%).

Premium and underwriting practice: Premiums are expressed per cent and can run “very high, sometimes 30 or 40 per cent” when a ship has been long missing (“lost or not lost”). A customary abatement of 2% is made at loss settlement, worked through a detailed table: at 10% premium the insured receives 88; at 15% → 83; at 20% → 78; at 25% → 73; at 30% → 68; at 40% → 53. Office-keepers charge 1s in the pound (5%) on premiums received; factors may charge 1% on sums insured and 1 or 2% on recovering losses.

The incorporation of the insurance companies (1720): Postlethwayt provides a legislative history of the Royal Exchange Assurance and London Assurance companies. They offered £600,000 toward the civil list debt in exchange for charters. By 6 Geo. I c. 18, two corporations were created with exclusive rights (except individual underwriters) to insure ships and merchandise at sea and lend on bottomry. Provisions include: stock of ready money sufficient for all losses; shares transferable and exempt from taxes; forgery of policies made felony without benefit of clergy; a 31-year charter redeemable by Parliament.

Marine vs. fire insurance: Fire insurance receives brief treatment through two leading cases — Sadlers Company v. Badcock (where Lord Chancellor Hardwicke ruled that insurance must cease with the insured’s interest, to prevent insuring strangers’ houses in hopes of burning them down) and Lynch v. Dalzel (1729, where Lord Chancellor King ruled fire policies are “not insurances of the things themselves… no body can warrant against accidents” but only “special agreements against such loss and damage as the persons insuring shall sustain”). The entry explicitly contrasts marine and fire: “interest or no interest” policies are permissible in commerce because goods might be insured in a foreign-prohibited trade, “but no such reason holds in the cases of insurances of houses from fire.”

War and enemy insurance (the “political discussion” referenced in the General Idea): A footnote directly poses the question — “If the person whose name is used in the assurance be in time of war taken to be no friend to the State… how does it consist with the insurance of the shipping and merchandise of enemies in times of war?” Legal cases establish that goods lawfully insured, then re-laden into “the ship of an enemy,” remain within the policy’s intention; but if prohibited goods (wool, leather, etc.) are insured in a foreign bottom and the ship taken, insurers are not liable as “the very foundation was illegal and fraudulent.”

Legal cases (20+ reported): Da Costa v. Scanderet (fraudulent concealment of intelligence), Knight v. Cambridge (barretry), Bond v. Gonfale (1704, Holt CJ on warranty to depart with convoy), Green v. Young (embargo conversion to fire-ship — insurers liable), Goddard v. Garrett (no interest → insurance void), Harman v. Vanhatton (bottomry + insurance both recoverable), Rooke v. Thurmond (1743, detailed policy form for the Polly from South Carolina to Cowes), and the notable Newnham, Perkins and Stoakes (1678, the Mayflower-ketch fraud where a master scuttled his own ship).

CUSTOM-HOUSE (Vol. I)

A massive multi-part article — among the longest in the entire dictionary, occupying approximately 94,000 lines of the text, organized as “The PRACTICAL BUSINESS of the CUSTOM-HOUSE” continuing alphabetically through letters C through I. It functions as a complete administrative manual for the English customs service.

Specimen forms (with originals from 1730): The opening section reproduces actual documents from the Custom-house, Southampton, dated 17 January 1730:

  • A sufferance for passenger Moses Wharton, arriving on the Providence of London (James Bell, master) from Rotterdam, with baggage listing (30 ells plain hollands linen, 1½ lb Dutch wrought silk, papers and books of account, 3 lb tea, an Indian damask gown and petticoat, old wearing apparel). The form includes the Collector’s direction on the back: dutiable items to be entered, prohibited items (tea, gown) to be prosecuted, the rest delivered.
  • The sufferance entry book: A two-sided ledger — left side for granting sufferances (columns for date, passenger’s name, ship, master, port, outward package), right side for their execution (examination results, by whom examined).
  • Over-entry certificate: For James Cross who over-entered 33 lbs of thrown silk, with sworn affidavit and a detailed repayment calculation breaking down subsidy types (old subsidy, new subsidy, ⅓ subsidy, ⅔ subsidy, impost 1692).
  • Tobacco damage certificate: For Dennis Dove whose Virginia tobacco was damaged and burned, claiming the statutory halfpenny per pound allowance.

The book-keeping system: A meticulous paper-based accounting regime is prescribed: the Collector’s sufferance book, land-waiters’ report books (blank books with, e.g., 12 leaves for a given ship), general pocket-books for ships that do not report, post-entry books for short-entered goods, and separate registers for over-entries and damages. A key rule — all accounts must be taken in the proper books at the time of examination, not on loose papers. No erasures; errors must be struck through with the pen and true figures written alongside with explanation. The Surveyor performs a monthly check: 14 days after each month, he delivers a list of all ships cleared, with post-entries standing out 6 days after clearance, for investigation and reporting to the Commissioners.

Key topical subsections across the alphabetical continuations:

  • Goods Inwards — importation laws, prohibited goods, landing hours, navigation acts (under G)
  • Goods Outwards — export procedures, re-export, drawbacks (under G)
  • Goods Coastwise — cockets, bonds, certificates (under G)
  • Fees — tables of lawful fees, prohibited fees, penalties for extortion (under F)
  • Fish Laws — herring, pilchard, cod, salmon, import/export restrictions, packing regulations (under F, H)
  • Bounty for the British White-Herring Fishery — the most detailed bounty section: £3 per annum per £100 employed (23 Geo. II c. 24), plus a ship bounty of £1 10s per ton for decked busses 20–80 tons. Requirements include: 12 Winchester bushels of salt per last, new barrels, two fleets of tanned nets, minimum crew of 6 for 20 tons + 1 per 5 tons above, mandatory rendezvous (Brassey’s Sound, Shetland, by June 22, or Campbelltown/Kirkwall by September 12), continuous fishing through the season, and sworn certification by the master before customs officers on return.
  • Salt duties — regulations for fishing vessels using duty-free salt (under F→G)
  • Iron from American colonies — pig and bar iron import regulations, stamping, certificates (under I)
  • Isle of Man — customs rules (under I)
  • Drawbacks, damages on goods imported, and premiums — cross-referenced to their own entries

Throughout, the section contrasts the English system (characterised by good laws shamefully neglected in execution) with the French (whose edicts, arrêts, and regulations are extensively documented from Savary).

BILLS OF EXCHANGE (Vol. I)

One of the longest single entries in the dictionary — approximately 5,440 lines — constituting a comparative treatise on the law and practice of bills of exchange across England, Scotland, France, the Holy Roman Empire, the Netherlands, and Italy.

The ledger entry (within the BANK section): The practical accounting illustration shows a specimen bill — “bills of exchange for cash, discounted, drawn by Clifford and Sons, Amsterdam, payable to N.O. at 2 usance” — with DR/CR bookkeeping in the model banking ledger, alongside lottery tickets purchased and loans on lottery tickets at 5% for 3 months.

The main BILL OF EXCHANGE article (starting at line ~62,924) covers:

  • General definition: trade bills and posted bills, with reference to French ordinances
  • English law: The entry is built around the relevant statutes (particularly 9 & 10 Will. III) and key cases such as Jackson v. Pigot on the negotiability of bills
  • Scottish law (~8,600 lines): Extensive treatment of acceptance, protest, re-exchange, horning and caption (the Scottish debt-enforcement process), annualrent, and prescription, with reported Scottish cases
  • French law: The complete Ordonnance de Commerce of 1673 (Tit. V, arts. I–XXVII) on bills of exchange, reproduced verbatim
  • Imperial cities of the Holy Roman Empire:
    • Hamburgh: Banking and exchange rules for bills at the Hamburgh Bank
    • Frankfurt: Two imperial edicts (1666 and 1676) regulating exchange
    • Augsburg: 14 articles on acceptance, usage (usance), days of grace
    • Leipzig/Saxony: Electoral decree of John George regulating bills
  • Amsterdam: Negotiating bills at Amsterdam, the role of bank payments (transfers on the Amsterdam Bank), agio, and brokers
  • Bologna: 17 articles on Italian exchange practices
  • Author’s concluding remarks: A critique of the existing bibliography on exchange and an argument for the universality of exchange law across trading nations

SWEDISH EAST-INDIA COMPANY (Vol. II)

A succinct history of the company: “It was the ruin of the late Ostend East-India Company that gave birth to the establishment of the Swedish East-India Company.” This entry shows Postlethwayt’s comparative method — treating each European East India company as a case study in commercial policy.

OSTEND EAST-INDIA COMPANY (Vol. II)

The Emperor Charles VI attempted to establish an East India company at Ostend in 1718 “but was compelled to desist from the design by the English and Dutch” — a revealing glimpse of the great power rivalry over Asian trade.

WOOL AND WOOLLEN MANUFACTORY (Vol. II)

A major entry tracing the progress of woollen manufactures in France and Spain, showing how both nations have made “extraordinary progress” that “must necessarily tend to the greater and greater injury of” British woollen exports. Cross-references entries on Biscay, Castille, Catalonia, Factors, and India-House of Spain.

The Preface (4th Edition)

The preface to the fourth edition is a substantial essay in its own right, explaining the rationale for the new edition and the author’s approach:

Reason for the new edition: The last war and the Definitive Treaty of Peace of 1763 “having made a great alteration in the state of the British trade and territories in America and Africa,” a new edition was required to reflect the changes.

Method of presentation: Postlethwayt retained his pre-war observations and added post-war remarks alongside them, so readers can compare the state of commerce before and after the peace. For the same territory (e.g., Canada, Florida, Senegal) the reader can see both what was said before 1756 and what the situation was after 1763.

Emphasis on manufacturing quality: The author was “remarkably elaborate in his endeavours to excite and stimulate” mechanics and manufacturers to improve the quality of their wares. He “sanguinely pleaded their cause with the government and legislature, that all wise encouragement should be given to this body of useful undertakers.”

French regulation vs. British neglect: The central policy theme — France excels through “rigid laws and regulations” that ensure quality, while Britain is “remarkable for good laws, but are shamefully neglectful in their execution.” The French “take no less care in the execution of their laws of trade, than in making them.” Postlethwayt gives the example of bakers adulterating bread and tricking the poor in weight: “In France these things are better guarded against.” The same applies to brewers and the regulation of manufacturing quality.

Practical utility: The dictionary has had real-world impact — manufacturers have written to the author “returning him their grateful thanks for the services he has laboured to do them” and he “has been happily instrumental to let several new manufactures on foot for the public interests.”

Historical Context

This fourth edition appeared in 1774 — on the eve of the American Revolution. The Treaty of Paris (1763) had transformed the British empire: Britain gained Canada, Florida, and dominance in India, while France lost its North American empire. Postlethwayt’s dictionary captures the immediate post-war period when Britain was adjusting to its new imperial responsibilities. The “General Idea” explicitly identifies “a happy and lasting Commercial Union between Great Britain and her American colonies at this critical juncture” as one of the great ends of the work — an aspiration that would be destroyed by the American Revolution within two years.

The dictionary is also a testament to Postlethwayt’s role as the leading British commercial writer of the mid-century. He was the first to produce an English equivalent of Savary’s French dictionary, and his work was cited by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations.

Key Features

  • First comprehensive English commercial dictionary, modelled on Savary’s French work but substantially expanded
  • Covers the entire range of British commerce — from the Act of Navigation to the regulations of brewers, from the East India trade to the circulating cash of the nation
  • Comparative international coverage — includes French edicts and arrêts, Spanish commercial regulations, Dutch shipping practices, and the commercial codes of other European nations
  • Post-1763 updates — reflects the territorial and commercial changes resulting from the Treaty of Paris
  • Practical focus — aimed at merchants, manufacturers, tradesmen, and “artizans of every respectable rank,” not just theorists
  • Policy advocacy — throughout the dictionary, Postlethwayt argues for specific commercial reforms: better enforcement of quality regulations, encouragement of manufacturing, and harmonisation of the landed and trading interests
  • Philosophical introduction — a sustained argument that commerce is the foundation of civilisation, with warnings drawn from ancient and modern history against the luxury and idleness that destroy empires
  • Appeal to the landed class — the nobility and gentry are urged to devote themselves to experimental philosophy and useful inventions, not merely to live as rentiers on their estates
  • Comprehensive legal reference — the dictionary systematically abstracts all British statutes of trade and navigation alongside comparative French, Spanish, and Dutch commercial law
