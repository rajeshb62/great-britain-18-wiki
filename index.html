Events

Union and Succession (1707–1716)

  • Act of Union 1707 (1707) — The union of England and Scotland into the Kingdom of Great Britain. Diplomatic negotiations, Scottish parliamentary debate, the securing of Presbyterian and Episcopal settlements, and the creation of a united parliament and free trade area. Read event page
  • Treaty of Utrecht (1713) — The Peace of Utrecht ending the War of the Spanish Succession. Britain gained Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, the Hudson Bay territory, and the Asiento slave trade monopoly. Marked Britain’s emergence as a global power. Read event page
  • Jacobite Rising of 1715 (1715–1716) — The ‘Fifteen — the first major Jacobite attempt to restore the Stuart line. The Earl of Mar raised the standard at Braemar, the indecisive Battle of Sheriffmuir, the surrender at Preston, and the collapse of the rising upon the arrival of the Pretender too late. Read event page
  • Septennial Act 1716 (1716) — Extended the maximum duration of Parliament from three to seven years. Passed by the Whig government after the ‘15 rising to reduce electoral instability, it entrenched Whig dominance for a generation. Read event page

Walpole Era and Mid-Century Conflict (1718–1748)

  • Battle of Cape Passaro (11 August 1718) — Admiral George Byng’s destruction of the Spanish fleet off Sicily. Fought before the formal declaration of war, it established British naval supremacy in the Mediterranean and ended Spanish ambitions in Italy. Read event page
  • South Sea Bubble (1720) — The spectacular rise and catastrophic collapse of the South Sea Company’s share price. A mania of speculation that engulfed the political class, led to a parliamentary inquiry, and propelled Robert Walpole to power as the first de facto Prime Minister. Read event page
  • Excise Crisis (1733) — Walpole’s proposed excise tax on tobacco and wine provoked a furious popular and parliamentary opposition. Walpole was forced to withdraw the bill — it was the first great test of his ministry and a rare defeat for the Robinocracy. Read event page
  • War of Jenkins’ Ear (1739–1742) — The war with Spain triggered by Captain Jenkins’s severed ear. The popular clamour for revenge forced Walpole into an unwanted war. Vernon’s capture of Porto Bello (1739) raised British spirits, but the disastrous Cartagena expedition (1741) demoralised the nation. Read event page
  • Cartagena Expedition (March–April 1741) — The largest amphibious expedition of the 18th century, 27,000 men against the Spanish fortress of Cartagena. A catastrophic failure due to disease, poor co-ordination, and Spanish defences under Blas de Lezo; 80–90% of the British force died. Read event page
  • Jacobite Rising of 1745 (1745–1746) — The ‘Forty-Five — Prince Charles Edward Stuart’s daring march from the Highlands to Derby, the Jacobite army’s retreat, and the final defeat at Culloden. The most serious threat to the Hanoverian succession. Read event page
  • Battle of Culloden (16 April 1746) — The final battle of the Jacobite rising of 1745. Cumberland’s disciplined government forces destroyed the exhausted Jacobite army in 40 minutes. The brutal pacification of the Highlands followed. Read event page
  • Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) — The peace ending the War of the Austrian Succession. Britain returned Louisbourg to France in exchange for Madras, a deeply unpopular concession that produced the cry ‘No peace without Louisbourg!’ Read event page

The Seven Years’ War (1756–1763)

  • Loss of Minorca (1756) — The French capture of the British Mediterranean base of Minorca. The subsequent court-martial and execution of Admiral John Byng for failing to ‘do his utmost’ — Voltaire’s pour encourager les autres. Read event page
  • Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757) — Robert Clive’s victory over Siraj-ud-Daulah in Bengal. A small engagement that established British military supremacy in India and laid the foundation of the British Raj. Read event page
  • Capture of Louisbourg (1758) — The siege and capture of the French fortress of Louisbourg, the key to the St Lawrence. A major amphibious operation led by Boscawen and Amherst that opened the seaway to Quebec. Read event page
  • Capture of Quebec (September 1759) — Wolfe’s daring ascent of the cliffs at Anse-au-Foulon and the Battle of the Plains of Abraham. Both Wolfe and the French commander Montcalm died. The battle decided the fate of Canada. Read event page
  • Battle of Quiberon Bay (20 November 1759) — Hawke’s daring pursuit of the French Brest fleet into the rocky Bay of Quiberon in a winter gale. The Royal Navy’s most brilliant tactical victory of the century; it destroyed French invasion plans and secured British naval supremacy for the remainder of the war. Read event page
  • Peace of Paris 1763 (1763) — The treaty ending the Seven Years’ War. Britain gained Canada, Florida, and supremacy in India; France ceded the Mississippi Valley. The peace marked Britain’s zenith as a global power — and planted the seeds of the American Revolution. Read event page

The American Crisis (1763–1776)

  • Wilkes Affair (1763–1774) — John Wilkes’s North Briton No. 45, his successive expulsions from Parliament, and the Middlesex election dispute. A landmark struggle for the liberty of the press, the right of electors, and the authority of Parliament. Read event page
  • Stamp Act Crisis (1765–1766) — Grenville’s Stamp Act provoked the first great colonial protest, the Stamp Act Congress, and the cry of ‘no taxation without representation.’ Repealed by the Rockingham ministry, but the Declaratory Act affirmed Parliament’s supremacy. Read event page
  • Boston Massacre (5 March 1770) — British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd, killing five. The ‘massacre’ became a defining propaganda event for the American patriot cause; Captain Preston and his men were defended by John Adams. Read event page
  • Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773) — American colonists dumped 342 chests of East India Company tea into Boston Harbour to protest the Tea Act. The British response — the Coercive Acts — drove the colonies toward revolution. Read event page
  • Declaration of Independence (4 July 1776) — The Second Continental Congress declared the Thirteen Colonies independent states, no longer part of the British Empire. Jefferson’s Declaration articulated the principles of the American Revolution and permanently severed the colonial bond. Read event page

The American War of Independence (1776–1783)

  • Saratoga Campaign (September–October 1777) — Burgoyne’s invasion from Canada ended in disaster at Saratoga. The American victory persuaded France to enter the war as an ally of the United States, transforming a colonial rebellion into a global conflict. Read event page
  • Gordon Riots (2–9 June 1780) — The most destructive urban riots in 18th-century London. Lord George Gordon’s Protestant Association marched on Parliament to protest Catholic relief; six days of arson and looting, 285 killed, the Bank of England and Newgate Prison attacked. Read event page
  • Siege of Yorktown (September–October 1781) — Washington and Rochambeau trapped Cornwallis’s army at Yorktown, Virginia, while de Grasse’s fleet blockaded the Chesapeake. The surrender effectively ended the American Revolutionary War and signalled the loss of Britain’s first empire. Read event page
  • Treaty of Paris 1783 (3 September 1783) — The definitive peace ending the American War of Independence. Britain recognised American independence, ceded Florida to Spain, Tobago and Senegal to France. Shelburne’s settlement was attacked as too generous. Read event page

Pitt’s Ministry and Reform (1784–1793)

  • Pitt’s India Act 1784 (1784) — Pitt’s East India Act established the Board of Control, creating the dual government of India by Company and Crown. It followed the Fox-North India Bill’s defeat and the King’s dismissal of the coalition. The system lasted until 1858. Read event page
  • Impeachment of Warren Hastings (1788–1795) — Burke’s seven-year impeachment of the former Governor-General of Bengal before the House of Lords. The greatest state trial of the century, Hastings was eventually acquitted, but the trial established that imperial officials were accountable to Parliament. Read event page
  • Regency Crisis (1788–1789) — George III’s first episode of mental illness (porphyria). Pitt’s ministry faced the prospect of a Foxite Regency under the Prince of Wales. The King’s dramatic recovery in February 1789 saved Pitt and damaged Fox. Read event page
  • French Revolution: British Reaction (1789–1793) — The fierce British debate over the French Revolution. Burke’s Reflections against Paine’s Rights of Man; the Revolution Controversy; the London Corresponding Society; the government crackdown — the Treason Trials, Habeas Corpus suspension, and the Two Acts. Read event page

The French Revolutionary Wars (1793–1801)

  • War of the First Coalition (1793–1797) — The first great coalition against Revolutionary France. Britain, Austria, Prussia, Spain, and Holland against the French Republic. From Pitt’s declaration of war, through the Flanders disaster and the fall of Toulon, to the Glorious First of June and the collapse of the coalition. Read event page
  • Glorious First of June (1 June 1794) — The first major naval battle of the French Revolutionary Wars. Admiral Lord Howe’s Channel Fleet defeated the French Atlantic fleet under Villaret-Joyeuse, capturing or destroying seven French ships. Though the French grain convoy escaped, the battle established British naval dominance. Read event page
  • Irish Rebellion of 1798 (May–September 1798) — The United Irishmen’s rising against the Anglican Ascendancy. Wexford, Antrim, and Down rose; French landings at Killala; Vinegar Hill; government brutal suppression. Wolfe Tone captured and died by suicide. The rebellion drove Pitt toward the Act of Union. Read event page
  • Act of Union 1800 (1800, effective 1 January 1801) — The union of Great Britain and Ireland into the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. The Irish Parliament voted itself out of existence after a campaign of systematic bribery; 100 Irish MPs and 32 peers went to Westminster. Pitt resigned when George III vetoed Catholic emancipation. Read event page
