The Anglo-French Rivalry

The rivalry between Great Britain and France was the central geopolitical fact of the 18th century. From the accession of William III to the Peace of Amiens, the two nations were at war for more than half the period, and their competition extended to every quarter of the globe — to Europe, to America, to India, to Africa, and to the distant islands of the Pacific. The outcome of this rivalry determined the shape of the modern world.

The War of the Spanish Succession, 1702–1714

The first great war of the century arose from the dying bequest of Charles II of Spain, who left his vast empire to Philip, Duke of Anjou, the grandson of Louis XIV. The union of France and Spain under the House of Bourbon threatened the balance of Europe, and a Grand Alliance was formed to prevent it.

The military campaigns of the war were dominated by the genius of the Duke of Marlborough, whose victories at Blenheim (1704), Ramillies (1706), Oudenarde (1708), and Malplaquet (1709) shattered the reputation of the French army. Mr. Smollett’s narrative (Smollett History Vol X) celebrates these achievements while lamenting their cost in blood and treasure.

The naval war, as Captain Campbell relates (Campbell Naval History Vol III), was no less decisive. The capture of Gibraltar in 1704 gave Britain command of the entrance to the Mediterranean — a possession of incalculable value for the future. Minorca was taken in 1708. The French navy, which had been the equal of the British at the beginning of the war, was driven from the seas.

The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) (Treaty of Utrecht) established the peace on terms highly advantageous to Britain. Philip was confirmed as King of Spain, but the crowns of France and Spain were never to be united. Britain obtained Gibraltar and Minorca, the Asiento (the monopoly of supplying slaves to the Spanish colonies), and territorial concessions in North America. The balance of power had been preserved, and Britain had emerged as a first-rate power.

The War of the Austrian Succession, 1740–1748

The death of the Emperor Charles VI in 1740, and the challenge to his daughter Maria Theresa’s succession by the Elector of Bavaria and the King of Prussia, provoked a new European war. Britain, in defence of the Pragmatic Sanction and the balance of power, took the side of Austria against France and her allies.

The war was marked by desperate battles and uncertain results. At Dettingen (1743), King George II, the last British sovereign to command his troops in person, defeated the French. At Fontenoy (1745), the Duke of Cumberland suffered a bloody repulse. The Jacobite Rising of 1745, which nearly overthrew the Hanoverian dynasty, was itself a part of the Franco-British conflict — the French had promised support to Charles Edward Stuart (Charles Edward Stuart), and a French invasion was at one time expected.

The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) restored the conquests of all parties and settled nothing. The war had been, in the words of a contemporary observer, a “war for nothing.” But the struggle for empire in America and India continued even in time of peace, fought by the agents and soldiers of the rival East India Companies and the colonial militias.

The Seven Years’ War, 1756–1763: The First Global War

The Seven Years’ War was the most decisive conflict of the century. It was, in a real sense, the first global war — fought in Europe, America, India, Africa, and the oceans that connected them. Its outcome established British supremacy in North America and India, and laid the foundations of the British empire.

Mr. William Pitt the Elder, who directed the British war effort from 1757, understood the global character of the conflict. He concentrated the resources of the nation upon the navy and the colonial expeditions, while subsidising the King of Prussia with British gold to keep the French armies occupied in Europe. The strategy was simple and effective: the navy would blockade the French ports, destroy French commerce, and convey British troops to the French colonies, while Frederick the Great fought the French armies on the continent.

The conquest of Canada was the greatest achievement of the war. General Wolfe’s victory at Quebec (1759), in which both Wolfe and the French commander Montcalm were killed, gave Britain the key to North America. In India, Clive’s victory at Plassey (1757) established British rule in Bengal. At sea, the battles of Lagos and Quiberon Bay (1759) — “the year of victories” — destroyed the French navy and ended any threat of invasion (Battle of Quiberon Bay).

The Peace of Paris 1763 left Britain in possession of Canada, Florida, and the eastern half of the Mississippi valley. France retained only a few fishing stations in North America and a precarious foothold in India. The British empire had reached a height that no European nation had attained since the days of Rome.

The American Revolutionary War, 1778–1783: France’s Revenge

The American Revolution gave France the opportunity for revenge. The French government, still smarting from the humiliation of 1763, lent substantial aid to the American colonists — money, arms, and eventually a fleet and an army. The war, which began as a civil conflict within the British empire, became a war between Britain and France (and, after 1779, with Spain as well).

The French navy, which had been rebuilt since 1763, proved a formidable opponent. The British fleet was overextended, forced to guard the Channel, the Mediterranean, the West Indies, and the coast of America simultaneously. The French admiral de Grasse established naval superiority off the coast of Virginia in 1781, allowing Washington and Rochambeau to trap Lord Cornwallis at Yorktown.

The Treaty of Versailles in 1783 restored to France some of the losses of 1763, though not Canada or India. The United States of America was recognised as an independent nation. The balance of power in Europe and the world was altered, perhaps permanently.

The French Revolutionary Wars, 1793–1802: Ideology and National Mobilisation

The war that broke out between revolutionary France and Great Britain in 1793 was different in character from those that had preceded it. It was not a war of dynasties or of limited objectives, but a war of principle — an ideological struggle between the principles of monarchy, aristocracy, and religion on the one hand, and the principles of popular sovereignty, equality, and the rights of man on the other.

The war required a mobilisation of national resources without precedent in British history. Mr. William Pitt the Younger, as Mr. Belsham records (Belsham Memoirs Vol IV), introduced measures — the income tax, the suspension of Habeas Corpus, the Seditious Meetings Act — that would have been thought tyrannical in peacetime. The navy was expanded to unprecedented size; the army was increased; the national debt rose to a height that the generation of Walpole would have considered the ruin of the nation.

The war itself was a series of desperate and uncertain contests. The victories of Lord Howe on the First of June (1794), of Sir John Jervis at Cape St. Vincent (1797), and of Lord Nelson at the Nile (1798) and Copenhagen (1801) preserved British naval supremacy. The campaigns in the West Indies, which were the scenes of heroic exertions and terrible mortality, secured the richest of the British colonies. The Irish Rebellion of 1798, fomented by French agents, was suppressed with a severity that left a lasting legacy of bitterness (Irish Rebellion of 1798).

Commerce and Conflict

The Anglo-French rivalry was not merely a military but a commercial struggle. The two nations were competitors for the trade of the world — the sugar of the West Indies, the slaves of Africa, the silks and spices of the East, the furs and fish of America. The wars were fought, in large measure, to capture and defend markets.

Mr. Postlethwayt, in his Commercial Interest of Great Britain (Postlethwayt Commercial Interest), argued that the rivalry with France was a permanent condition of commercial existence. “The French are our natural rivals in trade,” he wrote, “and every advantage they gain is a loss to us.” The principle of the balance of trade, by which the prosperity of a nation was measured by the excess of its exports over its imports, made the Anglo-French rivalry a zero-sum contest.

The Naval Dimension

The Ango-French rivalry was, at bottom, a struggle for sea control. The navy was the instrument by which Britain defended her trade, transported her armies, blockaded her enemies, and projected her power across the globe.

Captain Campbell’s Lives of the Admirals (Campbell Lives of the Admirals Vol IV) and his Naval History (Campbell Naval History Vol III) supply the record of the great naval commanders — Hawke, Boscawen, Rodney, Howe, Hood, Jervis, Nelson — who established and maintained British naval supremacy. The system of blockade, by which the British fleet kept the French ports under constant surveillance, was the essential condition of all other naval operations. The convoy system, by which merchant ships were protected by men-of-war, was the means by which British commerce continued to flourish in time of war.

The struggle for sea control was not without its reverses. The French built better ships and better harbours; they had the advantage of interior lines in Europe; they were sometimes able to concentrate superior forces at a decisive point. But the British navy had the advantages of seamanship, experience, and the unbroken tradition of victory.
