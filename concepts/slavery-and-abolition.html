Slavery and the Abolition Movement

The British transatlantic slave trade was, by the middle of the eighteenth century, the largest in the world. British ships carried more enslaved Africans to the Americas than those of any other European nation — an estimated 40,000 per year at the peak of the trade, representing about half the total Atlantic traffic. The wealth generated by slave labour in the West Indian sugar plantations financed British commercial expansion, supported a powerful interest in Parliament, and was intimately connected to the Atlantic System and Colonial Trade that was the foundation of the first British empire. Against this immense structure of commercial interest, a small but determined movement of humanitarian reformers — fired by religious conviction and philosophical principle — waged a campaign that, by the end of the century, had brought the abolition of the slave trade to the verge of parliamentary enactment.

The British Slave Trade

The British slave trade was conducted under the monopoly of the Royal African Company until 1698, after which the trade was opened to independent merchants. The “triangular trade” — British manufactures to Africa, enslaved Africans to the West Indies, West Indian produce to Britain — was the organising pattern. Bristol was the leading slaving port in the early part of the century, but by 1750 Liverpool had overtaken it. The Liverpool slavers, according to Malachy Postlethwayt’s Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce, sent approximately 100 ships annually to the African coast, carrying goods valued at £300,000 to exchange for slaves.

The Middle Passage — the transport of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic — was the most terrible stage of the trade. Enslaved people were packed below decks in conditions of unspeakable squalor, shackled in rows on platforms built to maximise the number of captives per voyage. Mortality rates averaged 10–15 percent per voyage, though on some ships the death rate exceeded 50 percent. The Dolben Act of 1788, which regulated the number of slaves per ton of shipping, was the first parliamentary attempt to mitigate the worst horrors of the passage.

Slavery in the Plantations

On the West Indian plantations, the enslaved population worked under a regime of relentless brutality. The sugar harvest was the most demanding agricultural labour in the Americas: the cane had to be cut and milled within 24 hours, requiring round-the-clock work during the harvest season. The slave codes of the British West Indies, modelled on the Barbados Code of 1661, denied the enslaved virtually all legal rights. They could not own property, give evidence in court, or marry legally. Punishment was at the discretion of the planter or his overseer.

The demographic regime of West Indian slavery was one of continuous importation. The enslaved population did not reproduce itself: the mortality rate exceeded the birth rate, and the sex ratio was heavily male. Barbados was the exception, with a relatively stable creole (island-born) slave population; in Jamaica and the newer colonies, the enslaved population could be maintained only by constant importation from Africa. This fact — that the slave trade was essential to prevent the depopulation of the plantations — became a central argument of the pro-slavery interest.

The Early Critics

The first sustained critique of the British slave trade came not from the humanitarian movement of the late century but from the Quakers and from individual moralists. The Quakers of Pennsylvania and London were the earliest organised body to denounce slavery as incompatible with Christianity. In 1727, the London Yearly Meeting condemned the trade, and by 1761 the Pennsylvania Quakers had excluded slave traders from membership.

The Somerset case of 1772 was a landmark. James Somerset, an enslaved African brought to England by his master, Charles Stewart, absconded and was recaptured. Lord Mansfield, Lord Chief Justice of the King’s Bench, ruled that slavery was “so odious” that it could exist only by positive law, and that since no such law existed in England, Somerset must be freed. The judgment did not abolish slavery in England — its scope was narrower than is sometimes supposed — but it established that the status of an enslaved person did not survive on English soil.

Granville Sharp, the scholar and philanthropist who had championed Somerset’s case, was the leading English abolitionist of the pre-Wilberforce era. His Representation of the Injustice and Dangerous Tendency of Tolerating Slavery (1769) was the first systematic English argument for abolition.

The Abolition Campaign

The abolition movement as a political force dates from the foundation of the Committee for the Abolition of the Slave Trade in 1787. The Committee, dominated by Quakers, brought together the most effective campaigners of the age: William Wilberforce, the independent member of Parliament for Yorkshire, who became the parliamentary leader of the cause; Thomas Clarkson, who spent years travelling the country collecting evidence of the trade’s horrors; and the Quaker Granville Sharp, the veteran of the Somerset case.

Clarkson’s investigations — he collected the iron manacles, the thumbscrews, the branding irons used by the slave ships — supplied the evidence on which the parliamentary campaign was built. His published works, particularly his Essay on the Slavery and Commerce of the Human Species (1786) and his later History of the Abolition of the Slave Trade, provided the factual basis for the abolitionists’ case. The Committee organised a nationwide petitioning campaign that, by 1792, had collected over 500,000 signatures — an extraordinary expression of public opinion in an age before mass democracy.

The Parliamentary Battles

The abolition question entered Parliament in earnest in 1788. The Dolben Act, regulating the conditions of the Middle Passage, passed both Houses with William Pitt the Younger’s support. In 1791, Wilberforce’s first motion for the abolition of the slave trade was defeated by 163 votes to 88. In 1792, a second motion passed the Commons in principle (230 to 85) but was delayed in the Lords, where the planter interest was entrenched.

Belsham, in his Memoirs of the Reign of George III, describes the parliamentary debates as among the most remarkable of the age. Edmund Burke, Charles James Fox, and Pitt himself all spoke in favour of abolition. Pitt’s speech of 2 April 1792 was one of the greatest of his career — a sustained argument that the slave trade was contrary to every principle of justice, humanity, and sound policy. But the Lords, led by the Lord Chancellor, Lord Thurlow, insisted on hearing evidence, and the measure was talked out.

The Economic Arguments

The pro-slavery case rested on the immense economic stakes involved. The West Indian planters, represented by their agents in London and by sympathetic members of Parliament, argued that the abolition of the slave trade would destroy the sugar islands, impoverish the merchants, ruin the shipping interest, and weaken the Royal Navy (which depended on the mercantile marine for its supply of seamen). The value of the West Indian trade was estimated at £4–5 million per annum; the capital invested in the sugar plantations at £30 million; the number of British subjects dependent on the trade at hundreds of thousands.

The abolitionists responded with a fourfold argument. First, the trade was morally indefensible — a crime against humanity that could not be justified by any calculation of commercial advantage. Second, the trade was economically self-defeating: the continual importation of enslaved Africans was less efficient than the creation of a stable, self-reproducing free-labour force. Third, British commerce could survive without the slave trade, as the growth of trade with Asia and with the United States demonstrated. Fourth, the danger of a slave revolt — of which the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was a terrible example — made the continuation of the slave trade a risk to the security of the colonies themselves.

The Wartime Pause

The outbreak of the War of the First Coalition against Revolutionary France in 1793 suspended the abolition campaign. The political atmosphere of the 1790s, dominated by fears of revolutionary conspiracy and the repressive legislation of the Pitt government, was inhospitable to abolition. Wilberforce continued to bring forward his motion year after year, but the tide of war and reaction prevented it from passing. The abolition of the slave trade would not be achieved until 1807, but the moral and political battle had been substantially won in the parliamentary contests of 1788–1792. The cause had been established as a permanent question of British politics.
