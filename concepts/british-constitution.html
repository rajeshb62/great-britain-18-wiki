The British Constitution

The British constitution, in the understanding of the 18th century, was not a written document but a body of laws, customs, precedents, and principles that had been transmitted from antiquity. It was, and was believed to be, the most perfect model of a mixed and balanced government that the world had ever seen — uniting in a single polity the monarchical, the aristocratic, and the democratic elements, and so preserving the liberty of the subject against the encroachments of any one power.

The Revolution Settlement of 1688–1689

The foundations of the 18th-century constitution were laid in the Revolution of 1688–1689. The flight of King James II, the invitation to William of Orange, and the Convention Parliament that declared the throne vacant — these events, as Bishop Gilbert Burnet records in his History of His Own Time, established the constitutional principle that the Crown was held by the consent of Parliament and the nation.

The Bill of Rights of 1689 was the charter of the new constitution. It declared the illegality of the suspending and dispensing powers claimed by the late King; it prohibited the levying of money without the consent of Parliament; it secured the right of petition, the freedom of elections, and the freedom of debate in Parliament; it condemned standing armies in time of peace; and it limited the Crown’s power over the administration of justice.

The Toleration Act of 1689, which granted liberty of worship to Protestant Dissenters, was a second pillar of the Settlement. The principle of religious toleration, though limited to those who accepted the Trinity and rejected transubstantiation, marked a decisive departure from the penal laws that had subjected nonconformists to persecution.

The succession to the Crown was regulated by the Bill of Rights and the Act of Settlement of 1701, which excluded Catholics from the throne and established the Protestant line of succession through the House of Hanover.

The Mixed Constitution: Theory and Practice

The theory of the mixed and balanced constitution was the central political doctrine of the age. The Crown, the Lords, and the Commons were each assigned a distinct function in the constitution, and each was supposed to check the encroachments of the others. The King was the executive magistrate, the fountain of honour, the commander-in-chief of the armed forces. The House of Lords was the hereditary council of the nation, the highest court of judicature, and the defender of the aristocracy against the ambition of the Crown and the turbulence of the Commons. The House of Commons was the representative of the people, the guardian of the purse, and the voice of the nation in the councils of the state.

Mr. David Hume, in his essay That Politics May Be Reduced to a Science, argued that the excellence of the British constitution lay in its contrivance of checks and balances, by which the ambition of each part was made to restrain the ambition of the others. Every man, pursuing his own interest, was led to defend the constitution against those who would subvert it.

Mr. Edmund Burke, in his writings on the constitution, emphasised the principle of prescription — the authority that long possession gave to established institutions. The constitution was not a thing to be remade according to abstract theories, but an inheritance to be preserved and improved by the slow accumulation of experience.

The Crown and Its Prerogatives

The limits of royal power were a central theme of 18th-century constitutional debate. The Crown retained considerable prerogatives: the appointment of ministers, the dissolution of Parliament, the command of the army and navy, the conduct of foreign affairs, the grant of offices and honours. But these powers were exercised, in practice, by ministers who were responsible to Parliament, and the Crown could not govern against the sense of the House of Commons.

The development of cabinet government, by which the King’s ministers acted as a body united by common responsibility, was one of the great constitutional innovations of the century. The principle that the King’s servants must command a majority in the House of Commons, though not formally established, was gradually accepted as a constitutional necessity.

Parliament and the Law

The supremacy of Parliament — the doctrine that the King in Parliament could make or unmake any law whatever — was the central principle of the constitution. No body of law, no ancient custom, no charter of rights could stand against an Act of Parliament. The Revolution Settlement had been enacted by Parliament, and no authority existed that could review or repeal its provisions.

The Common Law, administered by the judges in the courts of Westminster, was the other great pillar of the constitution. The judges, who held their offices during good behaviour rather than at the pleasure of the Crown (as secured by the Act of Settlement), were independent of the executive. The principles of the Common Law — trial by jury, the presumption of innocence, the right to be heard, the protection of property — were the inheritance of every Englishman.

Liberty and the Subject

The liberty of the subject was protected by a number of ancient and modern remedies. The writ of Habeas Corpus, confirmed and extended by the Habeas Corpus Act of 1679, ensured that no man could be detained in prison without being brought before a court and charged with a specific offence. Trial by jury secured the citizen against the arbitrary judgment of the executive.

The liberty of the press, though not formally established by law, was secured in practice by the abolition of the licensing system in 1695. A man might print what he pleased, provided that he was willing to answer for it before a jury if he were prosecuted for seditious or blasphemous libel. The trials of John Wilkes and others established the principle that the jury, not the judge, was the judge of the criminality of the publication.

The freedom of speech in Parliament, secured by the Bill of Rights, allowed Members to debate the measures of the government without fear of prosecution. The publication of parliamentary debates, though long forbidden, became an established practice in the second half of the century.

Constitutional Crises

The 18th century was not without its constitutional crises. The trial of Dr. Henry Sacheverell in 1710, in which the High Church clergyman was prosecuted by the Whig ministry for preaching against the principles of the Revolution, exposed the divisions within the Revolution Settlement itself — between those who emphasised the right of resistance and those who insisted upon the duty of passive obedience.

The Wilkes case of the 1760s and 1770s raised fundamental questions about the rights of electors, the privileges of the House of Commons, and the limits of executive power. The House of Commons had claimed the right to expel a Member and to seat his opponent — a claim that, if sustained, would have made the House the master of its own composition and destroyed the independence of Parliament.

The American controversy, which culminated in the Declaration of Independence, was in its essence a constitutional crisis. The question of the authority of the British Parliament over the colonies — whether it might tax them, legislate for them, and bind them in all cases whatsoever — was a question that the constitution had not resolved, and could not resolve, by any appeal to precedent or principle.

The Empire and the Constitution

The problem of the empire was the problem of the constitution in a new and difficult form. The British constitution had been devised for a compact kingdom in which the King, the Lords, and the Commons represented the various orders of a single society. How could it be extended to colonies separated by three thousand miles of ocean, inhabited by peoples of different races, religions, and legal traditions?

The Stamp Act Crisis of 1765–1766, the Townshend duties, the Boston Tea Party, and the War of American Independence were all, at bottom, constitutional disputes. The British government insisted upon the supremacy of Parliament; the colonists appealed to the rights of Englishmen and the principle of no taxation without representation. The constitution, as it had been inherited from the Revolution, provided no clear resolution of the dispute.
