Radicalism and Popular Politics

The 18th century was not only the age of aristocratic management in politics, but also the age of an emergent popular radicalism — a series of movements that claimed the rights of the people against the corruption of Parliament, the influence of the Crown, and the exclusive domination of the propertied classes. These movements, though suppressed or contained, left a lasting mark upon the political consciousness of the nation.

The Wilkesite Movement, 1763–1774

The radicalism of the 1760s found its first great champion in Mr. John Wilkes, a Member of Parliament of dissolute character but undoubted courage, who made the liberty of the press and the rights of electors the cause of his life. The publication of the North Briton No. 45 in 1763, in which Wilkes attacked the King’s Speech as a tissue of falsehoods, led to his arrest under a general warrant. The question of the legality of general warrants — by which the Secretary of State might arrest unnamed persons and seize their papers — became a constitutional cause of the first magnitude.

The Middlesex election of 1768–1769 raised the stakes. Wilkes, having been expelled from the House of Commons and outlawed, was three times elected by the freeholders of Middlesex, and three times the House voted to disqualify him, seating his defeated opponent, Colonel Luttrell, in his place. The “Middlesex election” became a national scandal. The right of the electors to choose their representatives freely, without the interference of the House of Commons itself, was the constitutional principle at stake.

The Society of Supporters of the Bill of Rights, founded in 1769, was the first mass political association in British history. It demanded annual Parliaments, the exclusion of placemen from the House, the abolition of general warrants, and the publication of parliamentary debates. It collected subscriptions from every part of the kingdom, organised petitions, and maintained a press campaign. Mr. Belsham’s narrative (Belsham Memoirs Vol IV) gives a full account of these proceedings and of the government’s determination to suppress the movement.

The County Association Movement

The failure of the Wilkesite agitation to achieve parliamentary reform led to a new phase of the reform movement in the 1780s. The county associations, led by the Reverend Christopher Wyvill, a Yorkshire clergyman of independent fortune, sought to mobilise the independent country gentlemen and the freeholders of the counties in favour of economical reform and a more equal representation.

The Yorkshire Association, formed in 1779, was the model for similar associations in other counties. Its programme was moderate: it demanded the addition of county Members, the abolition of rotten boroughs, and the reduction of the influence of the Crown. The movement reached its height in 1780, when petitions from every part of the kingdom were presented to Parliament. The House of Commons rejected the reform proposals, and the movement declined.

The Revolution Controversy: Burke vs. Paine

The outbreak of the French Revolution in 1789 transformed the character of English radicalism. The controversy between Mr. Edmund Burke and Mr. Thomas Paine — Burke’s Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) and Paine’s Rights of Man (1791–1792) — was the most important political debate of the age, and it defined the terms of political argument for a generation.

Burke defended the British constitution as the inheritance of ages, the accumulated wisdom of the nation, not to be overthrown in obedience to abstract theories of the rights of man. He denounced the French Revolution as an experiment in destruction, based upon the false philosophy of Rousseau and the Encyclopedists, and warned that it would end in military despotism.

Paine answered with a direct appeal to natural rights. Every man, he argued, was born with rights that no government might violate. The British constitution, with its Crown, its House of Lords, and its rotten boroughs, was an offence against reason and justice. The people had the right to abolish it and to establish a republic upon the principles of liberty and equality. Rights of Man, published in a cheap edition, reached an enormous audience among the common people.

The London Corresponding Society

The London Corresponding Society, founded in 1792 by Thomas Hardy, a shoemaker, was the first mass political organisation of the working classes. Its membership, drawn from the mechanics, artisans, and shopkeepers of the metropolis, paid a penny a week in subscription. Its purpose was to correspond with similar societies throughout the kingdom, to spread the principles of parliamentary reform, and to demand universal suffrage and annual Parliaments.

The Society grew rapidly. By the end of 1792 it claimed thousands of members. Its publications, including the pamphlets of Paine and the writings of the English Jacobins, were distributed through the provincial societies. The government, alarmed by the spread of French revolutionary ideas, determined to destroy the movement.

The Government Reaction

The government’s response to the radical movement was swift and severe. In May 1792, a Royal Proclamation against seditious writings was issued, and prosecutions were commenced against Paine and other radical authors. Paine, having been tried in his absence and convicted of seditious libel, fled to France.

The Treason Trials of 1794 were the government’s most dramatic assault upon the radical movement. Hardy, John Horne Tooke, and John Thelwall were tried for high treason before a special commission. The Attorney General, Sir John Scott (later Lord Eldon), argued that the London Corresponding Society’s activities amounted to a conspiracy to depose the King and establish a republic. The jury, to the surprise of the government and the delight of the radicals, acquitted the prisoners.

The acquittals did not check the government’s determination. The suspension of Habeas Corpus in 1794 allowed the ministry to detain suspected persons without trial. The Two Acts of 1795 — the Treasonable Practices Act and the Seditious Meetings Act — made it a capital crime to incite hatred of the King or the constitution, and prohibited meetings of more than fifty persons without a magistrate’s licence.

Mr. Belsham, who wrote as a reforming Whig, condemned these measures as a violation of the ancient liberties of Englishmen. The government, he argued, had been driven by panic to adopt the very instruments of tyranny that it claimed to be resisting.

The Legacy: Radicalism Forced Underground

By the end of the century, the radical movement had been crushed. The London Corresponding Society was dissolved. The provincial societies were suppressed. The radical press was silenced by prosecution and intimidation. The United Irishmen, who had adopted the principles of the French Revolution and sought the separation of Ireland from Britain, were driven to conspiracy and armed rebellion (Irish Rebellion of 1798).

But the radical tradition was not extinguished. The demand for parliamentary reform, for universal suffrage, for the abolition of slavery, and for the liberty of the press survived in secret societies, in the correspondence of exiles, and in the hearts of those who had read the works of Paine and the writings of the English Jacobins. The reform movement of the 19th century was to build upon foundations laid in the age of Wilkes, Wyvill, and the London Corresponding Society.
