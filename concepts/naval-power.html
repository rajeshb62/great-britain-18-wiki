Naval Power and Maritime Strategy

The Royal Navy was the foundation upon which the entire edifice of British power in the eighteenth century was built. It protected the trade that financed the Fiscal-Military State, blockaded the ports of France and Spain, transported and supplied the armies that fought in America and India, and secured the colonial possessions that were the material basis of British wealth. The Navy’s dockyards, its manning system, its tactical doctrines, and its administrative infrastructure were the largest and most complex industrial organisation in the British Isles — and the most expensive. The cost of the Navy dominated the budgets of every eighteenth-century administration.

The Dockyard System

The Royal Dockyards at Portsmouth, Plymouth, Chatham, Deptford, Woolwich, and Sheerness were among the largest industrial establishments in Europe. They employed thousands of artisans — shipwrights, caulkers, ropemakers, sailmakers, blacksmiths — who built, repaired, and fitted out the ships of the fleet. Campbell, in his Naval History of Great Britain, describes the scale of operations: a first-rate ship of the line required the timber of approximately 2,000 oaks, took three to four years to build, and cost £60,000–£80,000.

Portsmouth was the premier dockyard, the home of the Channel Fleet and the base for the close blockade of Brest. Plymouth (Plymouth Dock, renamed Devonport in 1824) was the western base, essential for the Western Squadron’s operations in the Bay of Biscay. Chatham, on the Medway, was the principal building yard for ships of the line. The Victualling Board supplied the fleet with provisions from its own bakeries, breweries, and slaughterhouses; the Navy Board managed the timber supply, the Ordnance Board the guns.

The greatest administrative challenge of the dockyard system was the supply of timber. The Navy consumed an estimated 100,000 oaks per year at the peak of the eighteenth-century wars. The forests of the Baltic — Poland, Prussia, and Russia — supplied the naval stores (hemp, flax, pitch, tar, masts) that British forests could not provide. The dependence on Baltic supplies was a strategic vulnerability of the first order, a major consideration in British policy toward the northern powers.

Manning the Fleet

The manning of the Navy was the most intractable problem of British naval administration. In peacetime, the Navy employed perhaps 15,000 men; in wartime, this number expanded to 80,000–120,000. The means by which these men were obtained was impressment — the legal power to compel men into naval service, exercised by press gangs operating in port towns and at sea, and by the interception of homeward-bound merchant vessels.

Impressment was hated and feared by the seafaring population. It was also inefficient: pressed men made unreliable sailors, prone to desertion and, in extremis, to mutiny. The Quota Acts of 1795, passed in response to the manning crisis of the French Revolutionary Wars, required each county to supply a specified number of men for the Navy — a system that transferred the burden of impressment from the maritime counties to the whole kingdom.

The bounty system offered an alternative: volunteers who joined the Navy received a cash bounty, typically £5 (rising to £30 during the French wars). But the bounty was never enough to supply the fleet’s needs, and impressment remained the foundation of naval manning throughout the century. Campbell, a former purser in the Navy, wrote with direct experience of the system’s injustices.

The Western Squadron

The most important strategic innovation of the mid-century Navy was the development of the Western Squadron — a permanent cruising force stationed off the coast of Brittany, whose purpose was to maintain a close blockade of the French Atlantic port of Brest. Edward Hawke, who perfected the system during the Seven Years’ War, kept his fleet at sea throughout the winter of 1758–59, a feat of seamanship and organisation that had no precedent in naval history.

The close blockade of Brest achieved two strategic objects. First, it prevented the French Brest fleet from putting to sea without being intercepted — the prelude to Hawke’s great victory at the Battle of Quiberon Bay in November 1759. Second, it protected British trade routes and colonial expeditions by denying the French the freedom of the seas. The Western Squadron system, refined by Richard Howe and John Jervis in the later wars, remained the central strategic doctrine of the Royal Navy until the age of steam.

The Line of Battle and Tactical Innovation

The standard tactical formation of the eighteenth-century Navy was the line of battle — ships arranged in a single line ahead, each ship engaging its opposite number in the enemy line. The line was a defensive and an offensive formation simultaneously: it maximised the firepower that could be brought to bear while minimising the risk of being broken by a superior force.

The line-of-battle tactic had been established by the Fighting Instructions of the Commonwealth and Stuart eras, and it was followed with religious fidelity by most admirals. The great tactical innovators of the century were those who, at decisive moments, departed from the line. George Rooke at Vigo Bay (1702) and George Byng at Cape Passaro (1718) demonstrated the rewards of aggressive pursuit. Edward Hawke at Quiberon Bay abandoned the line entirely, ordering a general chase into a rock-strewn bay in a winter gale. George Rodney at the Battle of the Saintes (1782) broke the French line by passing through it — a manoeuvre that became the signature of Horatio Nelson’s tactical genius.

Prize Money and the Naval Career

The economic incentive system of the Navy was prize money — the proceeds of enemy ships captured in war, distributed among the captors according to a fixed scale. The captain of a successful privateer or a lucky frigate commander could make a fortune from a single cruise. The “Prize Act” of 1708 established the principle that the proceeds of captured enemy property belonged to the captors, subject to the droits of the Admiralty.

Prize money was the engine of the naval career. Young officers of ambition and ability — Nelson, Collingwood, John Jervis — were driven by the desire for prize wealth as much as by professional honour. But the system had its dark side: it encouraged the pursuit of private gain over the public service, and it created bitter disputes over the division of spoil.

The Health of the Fleet

The health of the fleet was a constant preoccupation of naval administrators. Scurvy — the deficiency of vitamin C — destroyed more seamen than enemy action. The voyages of George Anson (1740–44) and John Byron (1764–66) had demonstrated the mortality that scurvy could inflict: Anson’s squadron lost 1,300 men out of 1,900, the great majority to disease. The experiments of Dr James Lind (1754), who demonstrated that citrus juice prevented scurvy, were slow to be adopted by the Admiralty, but by the end of the century lemon juice was a standard issue on long voyages.

The naval hospital system — Haslar at Gosport (1746) and Stonehouse at Plymouth (1762) — provided the first purpose-built military hospitals in Britain. The sick and wounded were treated, invalids pensioned, and the dead buried at government expense. The health reforms of the later century, particularly under the administration of John Jervis as First Lord of the Admiralty (1801–1804), significantly reduced the mortality rate of the fleet.

The Mutinies of 1797

The Spithead and Nore mutinies of 1797 were the greatest crisis of discipline in the history of the Royal Navy. In April 1797, the Channel Fleet at Spithead refused to put to sea, demanding higher pay, better provisions, and shore leave — the “Spithead demands.” The mutineers acted with remarkable discipline and moderation, maintaining the routine of the fleet while refusing to sail. The Admiralty, after a tense standoff, granted most of the demands.

The Nore mutiny, which followed in May, was more dangerous and more radical. Led by Richard Parker, a former naval officer turned mutineer, the mutineers blockaded the Thames and demanded political reforms that went beyond pay and conditions. The government, fearing that the mutiny was part of a revolutionary conspiracy (the Irish Rebellion of 1798 was brewing), refused to negotiate. Parker was hanged, the mutiny collapsed, and the authority of the Admiralty was restored. The mutinies demonstrated both the legitimate grievances of the common seaman and the fragility of discipline in a fleet that had been expanded rapidly to meet the demands of total war.

The Cost

The naval estimates were the largest single item in the British budget. In the decade after the Peace of Paris (1763), the Navy cost approximately £2 million per annum in peacetime; during the French Revolutionary Wars, the cost rose to £8–10 million per annum. The naval debt — the accumulated arrears of naval expenditure — was a major contributor to the growth of the national debt and a constant preoccupation of Chancellors of the Exchequer.
