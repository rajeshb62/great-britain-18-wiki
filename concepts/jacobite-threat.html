The Jacobite Threat and the Highlands

The Jacobite claim to the British throne — the assertion that the lawful sovereign was James Francis Edward Stuart, the son of the deposed King James II, and after him his grandson Charles Edward Stuart — was a living political force in Great Britain for more than half a century after the Revolution of 1688. The Jacobite risings of 1715 and 1745 were the most serious armed challenges to the Hanoverian state, and the ‘Forty-Five, in particular, came within a hair’s breadth of overthrowing the established order.

The Stuart Claim

The foundation of the Jacobite claim was the doctrine of indefeasible hereditary right — the principle that the Crown descends by inheritance according to the rules of primogeniture, and that no Act of Parliament, no decree of a Convention, and no vote of a House of Commons can alter the succession established by God and nature. By this principle, King James II, notwithstanding his flight from the kingdom, remained the lawful sovereign; and his son, James Francis Edward Stuart — the “Old Pretender” to his enemies, “King James III” to his followers — was the rightful heir.

The Jacobite claim was not merely a dynastic one. It was supported by a constellation of political, religious, and social interests. The Catholic gentry of England and Scotland, who had been excluded from the benefits of the Revolution Settlement, looked to a Stuart restoration for the repeal of the penal laws. The Episcopalians of Scotland, who had been deprived of their bishops and their liturgy, saw the Stuarts as the defenders of the true church. The Highland clans, who valued their ancient customs and their independence of the central government, found in the Stuart cause a focus for their loyalty.

The foreign support for the Jacobite cause was a constant anxiety to the British government. The French court, which regarded the Stuarts as useful instruments of French policy, provided money, arms, and occasionally troops for Jacobite enterprises. The courts of Spain and the Papacy likewise lent their support.

The ‘Fifteen: The First Rising

The accession of George I in 1714, the first Hanoverian monarch, was the signal for the first great Jacobite rising. The new King was a German prince who spoke no English, who was suspected of indifference to the Church of England, and whose chief ministers were Whigs of the Revolution party. The Tories, who had governed England in the last years of Queen Anne, were excluded from power, and many looked to the Chevalier as a deliverer.

The Earl of Mar, who had served Queen Anne as Secretary of State for Scotland, raised the standard of the Old Pretender at Braemar in September 1715. The rising spread rapidly through the Highlands and the north-eastern Lowlands. Mar, an indifferent general, assembled a substantial army — perhaps twelve thousand men — but failed to act with decision.

The battle of Sheriffmuir in November 1715 was indecisive; the two wings of each army routed the opposing wing, and the field was left in possession of neither side. But in the south, the Jacobite rising in England — led by Mr. Forster, a Northumberland gentleman of no military ability — was defeated at Preston. The Chevalier, who arrived in Scotland too late to influence events, sailed for France in February 1716, and the rising collapsed.

The government’s response was severe but not vindictive. The leaders were attainted, their estates forfeited, and some were executed. The Disarming Act of 1716 was intended to pacify the Highlands by prohibiting the possession of arms. But the Act was imperfectly enforced, and the spirit of Jacobitism survived.

The Interwar Period: Conspiracy and Management

The years between the ‘Fifteen and the ‘Forty-Five were marked by intermittent Jacobite conspiracies and government counter-measures. The Atterbury Plot of 1721–1722, in which the Bishop of Rochester was implicated, revealed the extent of Jacobite sentiment among the English Tories. The government of Sir Robert Walpole, which had come to power in the crisis of the South Sea Bubble, employed spies, informers, and agents provocateurs to detect and disrupt Jacobite activity.

Walpole’s policy towards the Jacobites was a mixture of severity and indulgence. The leading conspirators were pursued and punished, but the ordinary gentry and the Highland chiefs were, for the most part, left in peace. The government relied upon the influence of the great Whig families in Scotland — the Argyll interest, the Forbes interest — to keep the Highlands quiet. The system of Parliamentary Patronage, which Walpole perfected, extended to Scotland, where the management of the Scottish representation in Parliament was entrusted to Lord Ilay, the Duke of Argyll’s brother.

The ‘Forty-Five: The Rising of Charles Edward Stuart

The rising of 1745 was the most serious Jacobite threat of the century. Charles Edward Stuart — “Bonnie Prince Charlie,” the Young Pretender — landed on the island of Eriskay in the Hebrides in July 1745 with a handful of companions and a French privateer. He had no army, no money, no supplies — only his name, his courage, and the confidence that the Highland clans would rise for the Stuart cause.

They did. The raising of the standard at Glenfinnan in August 1745 brought the Camerons, the Macdonalds, the Stewarts, and other clans to the Prince’s side. The government forces in Scotland, under the command of Sir John Cope, were routed at Prestonpans in September. Edinburgh fell to the Jacobites without a fight. By November, the Prince’s army — perhaps five thousand strong — had crossed the border into England.

The march to Derby was the great crisis of the rising. The Jacobite army advanced through Lancashire and the Midlands, gathering recruits and skirmishing with government forces. In December 1745, the Prince reached Derby, only one hundred and twenty miles from London. The capital was in a panic. King George II had ordered his horses to be kept ready for flight to Hanover. The Bank of England had suspended payment in gold.

But the Jacobite council of war, meeting in Derby, decided to retreat. The reasons were military: the government army under the Duke of Cumberland (Duke of Cumberland) was approaching from the west; another army was assembling in the south; the expected French invasion had not materialised; and the English Jacobites, on whose support the Prince had counted, had not risen. The retreat, though it was conducted with skill, was the beginning of the end.

Culloden and the Aftermath

The decisive battle was fought at Culloden Moor, near Inverness, on 16 April 1746. The Prince’s army, diminished by desertion and starvation, faced the Duke of Cumberland’s well-supplied and well-disciplined force. The battle was short and bloody. The Highland charge, which had broken the government ranks at Prestonpans and Falkirk, was checked by Cumberland’s artillery and disciplined fire. The Jacobite army was shattered; perhaps two thousand men were killed or wounded.

The aftermath of Culloden was a campaign of pacification that combined military terror with systematic destruction. The Duke of Cumberland, who was called “Butcher” by his enemies, ordered the execution of wounded prisoners, the burning of houses and crops, the slaughter of cattle, and the persecution of the Jacobite population. The Highlands, which had been the heart of the rising, were subjected to a military occupation that lasted for years.

The Disarming Acts, which followed the rebellion, were enforced with a rigour that had been absent after 1715. The wearing of Highland dress — the plaid, the kilt, the tartan — was prohibited by statute. The heritable jurisdictions of the Highland chiefs, under which they had exercised criminal and civil authority over their tenants and dependents, were abolished. The clan system, which had been the foundation of Jacobite military power, was systematically dismantled.

The Highlanders Reformed

The suppression of the Jacobite threat was followed by the transformation of the Highlands. The broken clans, deprived of their chiefs’ authority and their traditional way of life, were pressed into the service of the very state they had sought to overthrow.

The Highland regiments — the Black Watch, the Highland Light Infantry, the Cameronians, the Seaforth Highlanders — were raised for the service of the Crown. The same men who had charged at Prestonpans and Culloden were now sent to fight the battles of the Hanoverian king in America, India, and Europe. The martial spirit of the Highlanders, which had been the danger of the state, was turned to its advantage.

The economic transformation of the Highlands, which accelerated in the second half of the century, was itself a consequence of the suppression of Jacobitism. The chiefs, who had been the leaders of armed clans, became landlords, interested in the commercial exploitation of their estates. The clearance of the glens, the introduction of sheep farming, the emigration of the Highland people — these were the longer-term consequences of the destruction of the old order.

Jacobitism in Memory

The Jacobite cause, which had been a serious political and military threat, was gradually transformed into a romantic memory. The figure of Bonnie Prince Charlie, the failure of the ‘Forty-Five, the brutality of Culloden and its aftermath — these became the stuff of folk song and legend, of sentimental attachment to a lost cause.

The romanticisation of Jacobitism began almost immediately after the rising. The Prince, who had lived as a fugitive in the Highlands for five months after Culloden, aided by Flora MacDonald and a network of secret supporters, became a figure of romance. The songs of the ‘Forty-Five — “The Skye Boat Song,” “Will Ye No Come Back Again,” “Charlie Is My Darling” — preserved the memory of the rising in a softened, sentimental form.

By the end of the century, the Jacobite threat was a thing of the past. The political and military danger had passed. But the memory of the ‘Forty-Five, and of the Prince who had come so close to the throne, lingered in the hearts of those who remembered the Stuarts and the old Scotland that had died at Culloden.
