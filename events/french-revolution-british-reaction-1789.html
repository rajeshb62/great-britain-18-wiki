British Reactions to the French Revolution (1789–1793)

The French Revolution of 1789 provoked the most profound ideological crisis in British public life since the Glorious Revolution of 1688. Over the course of four years — from the fall of the Bastille in July 1789 to the outbreak of war between Britain and Revolutionary France in February 1793 — the Revolution transformed the terms of British politics, shattered the old Whig party, generated an unprecedented pamphlet war known as the Revolution Controversy, and forced the government of William Pitt the Younger into a campaign of domestic repression that curtailed the liberties the Revolution was supposed to have vindicated. The debate over the meaning and legitimacy of the French Revolution became, in effect, a debate over the meaning of the British constitution itself.

The Initial Response: Cautious Sympathy and Whig Enthusiasm

The fall of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 was received in Britain with emotions that ranged from cautious approval to outright jubilation. The initial response was overwhelmingly favourable: the Revolution appeared to be the French emulation of Britain’s own Glorious Revolution — the overthrow of arbitrary power by the representatives of the nation, the establishment of a constitutional monarchy, the vindication of the rights of the people. The French, it seemed, were at last following the path the British had pioneered in 1688.

The Whig opposition, led by Charles James Fox, was enthusiastic. Fox famously declared the fall of the Bastille “the greatest event that ever happened in the history of the world, and much the best.” Richard Brinsley Sheridan, Charles Grey, and the young Whig radicals welcomed the Revolution as the triumph of liberty over despotism. The Revolution Society, a club of Dissenters and Whigs established to commemorate the Glorious Revolution, sent congratulations to the French National Assembly and toasted “the cause of liberty in France and throughout the world.” William Wilberforce, though no radical, initially expressed cautious sympathy, as did many of the evangelical reformers and anti-slave-trade activists who saw in the Revolution a sign of providential progress.

But the response was never uniform. The governing circles of William Pitt the Younger’s ministry — Pitt himself, Henry Dundas, Lord Grenville — were more guarded. Pitt famously remarked in the House of Commons in February 1790 that the French Revolution presented “an event, the extent of which no human foresight could appreciate.” He gave no sign of the hostility that would consume his later years. The King, George III, was instinctively suspicious, but the ministry maintained a policy of official neutrality toward the Revolution through 1790 and most of 1791.

Edmund Burke was among the waverers. His initial response was neither the enthusiasm of Fox nor the complacency of Pitt. He had spent the 1780s prosecuting Warren Hastings for the crimes of empire, and he was instinctively sympathetic to any check on arbitrary power. But he was also, by temperament and philosophy, a creature of prescriptive tradition, of slow and organic reform. As the Revolution unfolded — the abolition of aristocratic privileges on 4 August 1789, the confiscation of Church property in November, the Civil Constitution of the Clergy in July 1790 — Burke’s misgivings deepened into horror. He saw not the birth of liberty but the collapse of civilisation: the substitution of abstract metaphysical rights for the inherited liberties of Englishmen, the destruction of the corporate bodies that mediated between the individual and the state, and the descent into a tyranny more absolute than the one the revolutionaries had overthrown.

Burke’s Reflections on the Revolution in France (November 1790)

The event that transformed the British debate was the publication, on 1 November 1790, of Edmund Burke’s Reflections on the Revolution in France — the greatest counter-revolutionary text in the English language and one of the foundational documents of modern conservatism.

The Reflections was written as a letter addressed to a young French correspondent, but its intended audience was the British public. It was Burke’s answer to Richard Price’s sermon to the Revolution Society (November 1789), in which the Unitarian minister had welcomed the Revolution as the harbinger of a universal reign of liberty and had claimed for the British people the right to choose their own governors, cashier them for misconduct, and frame a government for themselves. These were, Burke thundered, the very doctrines that had produced anarchy in the seventeenth century and that would produce anarchy again if they were ever allowed to take root in Britain.

The argument of the Reflections was not merely a political polemic but a complete philosophy of civil society. Burke grounded his defence of the existing order on three foundations: prescription, prejudice, and the partnership of the generations. Prescription — the authority of long-established custom — was for Burke the surest title to political legitimacy. The British constitution was not the product of abstract reason but of centuries of accumulated experience, of trial and error, of “the cold neutrality of implicit and indiscriminate suspicion.” To sweep it away in the name of rational reconstruction was an act of political madness.

Prejudice — the unreflective attachment to inherited institutions and customs — was not a weakness to be overcome but a source of social wisdom. Burke declared himself content to “leave these acute and subtle questions of natural right to the schools of metaphysics.” For practical purposes, the prejudices of the English people — their reverence for the monarchy, the aristocracy, the established Church, the common law — were the bulwarks of their liberty. “We are afraid to put men to live and trade each on his own private stock of reason,” Burke wrote, “because we suspect that this stock in each man is small, and that the individuals would do better to avail themselves of the general bank and capital of nations and of ages.”

Above all, Burke insisted on the sacred character of the social contract. The contract of society was not a mere commercial agreement to be dissolved at pleasure by the current generation. It was a partnership “in all science; a partnership in all art; a partnership in every virtue, and in all perfection” — a partnership not only between the living but between the living, the dead, and the unborn. The revolutionaries, in asserting the absolute sovereignty of the present generation, had broken faith with the past and forfeited the future.

The Reflections was an immediate sensation. It sold 30,000 copies in the first year and went through eleven editions. It was reviewed, excerpted, debated, and denounced across the entire political spectrum. The governing class — the King, the ministry, the bishops, the great landowners — received it as a revelation. George III declared it “a good book — a very good book indeed.” The Whigs were thrown into confusion. Fox, who had been Burke’s closest ally in the Hastings impeachment and the champion of American liberty, found himself on the opposite side of the defining question of the age.

The British Debate: Burke vs. Paine

If Burke’s Reflections was the trumpet of counter-revolution, its answer — Thomas Paine’s Rights of Man — became the bible of British radicalism. Paine was the most formidable antagonist Burke could have encountered: a man whose entire life had been a rebuke to Burke’s philosophy of reverence and prescription. An Englishman turned American revolutionary, Paine had served as George Washington’s propagandist, defended the French Revolution in the National Assembly, and possessed a prose style of crystalline clarity that reached audiences far beyond the reach of Burke’s dense and learned periods.

The first part of the Rights of Man was published in February 1791, dedicated to George Washington. It was a point-by-point demolition of the Reflections. Where Burke defended prescription, Paine asserted the natural and imprescriptible rights of every human being — rights that belonged, as he put it, to “the living generation” and could not be alienated by the dead. The British constitution, for Burke the accumulated wisdom of ages, was for Paine a system of hereditary absurdities that had preserved the abuses of monarchy, aristocracy, and clerical privilege under the pretence of venerating tradition. “It is not because a thing has been done a thousand times,” Paine wrote, “that it is right.”

Paine’s radicalism went far beyond Burke’s conservative anxieties. He argued for the abolition of monarchy, universal manhood suffrage, representative government, and a sweeping programme of social welfare that foreshadowed the modern welfare state: old-age pensions, public education, family allowances, and progressive taxation. The second part of the Rights of Man (February 1792) applied these principles to the concrete conditions of England, demonstrating with devastating clarity that the existing constitution served the interests of the privileged few at the expense of the suffering many. The domestic policy section — in which Paine proposed a graduated income tax to fund poor relief — was the most radical programme of social reform ever proposed by a mainstream political writer in eighteenth-century Britain.

The Rights of Man was even more successful than the Reflections. It sold between 200,000 and 500,000 copies in the first few years — a number that dwarfed every previous record for a political pamphlet. It was read aloud in taverns, debated in clubs, and passed from hand to hand among working men who had never before possessed a political argument of their own. The government became alarmed. Pitt is reported to have said that a man who could sell such numbers of a seditious pamphlet was a more dangerous enemy than the French army. In May 1792, a proclamation was issued against seditious writings, and Paine was indicted for treason. He fled to France in September 1792, narrowly escaping arrest, and was tried and convicted in his absence in December.

The Revolution Controversy

The Burke-Paine exchange ignited the most intensive pamphlet war in British history — what historians call the Revolution Controversy. Between 1789 and 1793, over 300 pamphlets, books, and periodical essays were published on the subject of the French Revolution, making the British debate the most voluminous and intellectually sophisticated in Europe. The controversy engaged the entire range of the British reading public, from the political philosophers and parliamentary orators who had dominated the earlier eighteenth-century public sphere to the provincial artisans, Dissenting ministers, and radical schoolmasters who now found their voices.

The radical side of the controversy was sustained by a generation of writers whose names would define the romantic and reformist temper of the next half-century. William Godwin, the philosophical anarchist, published his Enquiry Concerning Political Justice in 1793, a work of breathtaking intellectual ambition that sought to ground morality and politics on the principles of reason and universal benevolence. Godwin argued that government was at best a necessary evil and at worst an engine of corruption; that the human mind was perfectible; and that the gradual extension of knowledge would eventually render all political institutions unnecessary. The book made Godwin the intellectual godfather of English radicalism — though its subscription price of three guineas placed it far beyond the reach of the audience that Paine had captured.

The response to Political Justice was immense. Samuel Taylor Coleridge, then a radical undergraduate at Cambridge, hailed Godwin as a philosopher whose work would transform the moral world. Coleridge himself, in his early poem “Religious Musings” (1794), celebrated the French Revolution as “the dawn of universal liberty” and imagined the French as the agents of providential deliverance. With Robert Southey, he planned a “Pantisocracy” — a utopian community on the banks of the Susquehanna River in Pennsylvania, where the principles of Godwinian equality could be realised in practice. The Pantisocracy scheme collapsed, but the radical enthusiasm it expressed was widely shared among the young intelligentsia.

The moderate reformers and Dissenters produced their own contribution. Richard Price and Joseph Priestley — the great Unitarian rationalists — had welcomed the Revolution as the fulfilment of the Dissenting tradition’s long struggle against religious and political inequality. Priestley’s house and laboratory in Birmingham were destroyed by a Church-and-King mob in July 1791, on the second anniversary of the fall of the Bastille — a vivid demonstration that the loyalist response was not confined to the pages of pamphlets.

The conservative side of the controversy was equally prolific. Hannah More produced a series of anti-radical tracts, the Cheap Repository Tracts (1795–1798), which sold in millions and preached piety, resignation, and obedience to the poor. Arthur Young, the agricultural writer, turned his pen against the Revolution. The Anti-Jacobin Review, founded in 1798 by George Canning and other Pittite wits, became the leading organ of conservative satire and polemic.

The London Corresponding Society (1792)

The doctrinal debates of the Revolution Controversy were translated into political action by the emergence of popular radical societies. The most important of these was the London Corresponding Society (LCS), founded in January 1792 by Thomas Hardy, a Scottish shoemaker. The LCS was the first working-class political organisation in British history — a society of “tradesmen, mechanics, and shopkeepers” who paid a penny a week to finance the publication and distribution of radical literature, the maintenance of a network of correspondents across the provincial towns, and the petitioning of Parliament for universal manhood suffrage and annual parliaments.

The LCS modelled itself explicitly on the Jacobin clubs of revolutionary Paris. Its structure was democratic — each division of thirty members elected delegates to a general committee — and its programme was drawn directly from the Rights of Man. Hardy corresponded with Corresponding Societies in Sheffield, Manchester, Norwich, Edinburgh, and dozens of smaller towns; by 1795, the movement claimed perhaps 100,000 members across the country.

The government watched the growth of the Corresponding Societies with increasing alarm. The societies were not, in themselves, revolutionary — they petitioned for parliamentary reform, not for the overthrow of the constitution. But in the atmosphere of the Revolution Controversy, the distinction between reform and revolution was easily lost. The LCS’s open admiration for the French experiment, its publication of Paine’s works, its recruitment of working-class members who had never before participated in politics — all of this seemed to Pitt’s government to be the seedbed of a British Jacobinism that might one day bloom into an English Terror.

The Government Crackdown

The shift from neutrality to hostility was gradual but decisive. Between 1792 and 1795, the British government moved through four stages of increasing repression: the Royal Proclamation against Seditious Writings (May 1792), the Treason Trials (1794), the suspension of habeas corpus (1794), and the Two Acts (1795).

The Royal Proclamation against Seditious Writings (May 1792)

On 21 May 1792, George III issued a Royal Proclamation against Seditious Writings and Publications. The proclamation was a direct response to the unprecedented circulation of the Rights of Man and the proliferation of Corresponding Societies. It condemned “diverse wicked and seditious writings” that were “industriously circulated among the lower orders” and called upon all magistrates and law officers to “use their utmost endeavours” to discover the authors, printers, and distributors of such publications and to bring them to justice.

The proclamation had an immediate effect. The prosecution of Paine was initiated almost at once. Booksellers and printers who had sold the Rights of Man were arrested and tried. In Scotland, the campaign was even more severe: the Scottish courts convicted the radical lawyer Thomas Muir and the Unitarian minister Thomas Fyshe Palmer — who had done nothing more than distribute the writings of Paine and other reformers — and sentenced them to fourteen years’ transportation to Australia.

The Treason Trials (1794)

In the spring of 1794, the government struck at the leadership of the radical movement. The leaders of the London Corresponding Society — Thomas Hardy, John Horne Tooke, John Thelwall — were arrested and charged with high treason. The charge was based on an alleged conspiracy to summon a “National Convention” (modelled on the French Convention) that would supersede Parliament and overthrow the constitution.

The trials, held at the Old Bailey in October and November 1794, were the most important state trials of the eighteenth century. The prosecution’s case rested on the government’s claim that the Corresponding Societies’ agitation for reform was a treasonous conspiracy to subvert the constitution by force. The defence, led by the Whig lawyers Thomas Erskine and Vicary Gibbs, argued that the reformers had done nothing more than petition for parliamentary reform — an activity that was not only lawful but protected by the Bill of Rights.

The government’s case collapsed. The jury acquitted Hardy on 5 November 1794 after only a few minutes’ deliberation. Tooke and Thelwall were acquitted in turn. The acquittals were a spectacular victory for the radical movement — but they proved to be a temporary reprieve. The government, humiliated by the failure of the prosecutions, turned to legislation instead.

The Suspension of Habeas Corpus (1794)

Even before the Treason Trials, the government had taken a step of extraordinary constitutional significance. On 16 May 1794, Parliament passed a bill suspending the Habeas Corpus Act — the great bulwark of English liberty against arbitrary imprisonment — for the duration of the session. The suspension empowered the government to detain suspected traitors without charge or trial.

Charles James Fox led the opposition to the bill in the most memorable speeches of his career. He argued that the suspension was a greater threat to the constitution than any conspiracy of radicals. “If this bill passes,” Fox declared, “I tremble for the constitution of this country. The king’s proclamation, the suspension of the habeas corpus — these are the steps of a despotism more dangerous than that of France.”

The bill passed, and was renewed in 1795. Under its authority, dozens of radicals were imprisoned without trial — some for months, some for years. The suspension was a turning-point in the development of the British state: for the first time since the Glorious Revolution, the government had assumed the power to imprison Englishmen at pleasure.

The Two Acts (1795)

The final stage of the crackdown came after an incident that gave the government the pretext it needed. On 29 October 1795, as the King drove in state to open Parliament, a mob surrounded his carriage and a stone (or possibly a bullet) struck the window. The King was unharmed, but the government used the incident to justify the most repressive legislation of the decade.

The Treasonable Practices Act and the Seditious Meetings Act — known together as the “Two Acts” or the “Gagging Acts” — were rushed through Parliament in November and December 1795. The Treasonable Practices Act broadened the definition of treason to include not only acts but spoken and written words that “compassed or imagined” the death of the King or the subversion of the constitution. The Seditious Meetings Act prohibited all public meetings of more than fifty persons unless licensed by a magistrate, gave magistrates the power to arrest speakers, and extended the law of libel.

The Two Acts were the most severe restrictions on civil liberty enacted in Britain during the eighteenth century. They were passed by overwhelming majorities — the Seditious Meetings Act passed the Commons by 214 votes to 37 — but the opposition was fierce. Fox conducted a rearguard action of extraordinary eloquence, denouncing the Acts as “the most tyrannical and disgraceful measures that had ever disgraced the British legislature.” Charles Grey warned that the Acts would convert the British constitution into “a detestable despotism.”

In the event, the Two Acts were enforced only sporadically — never with the thoroughness that their opponents had feared. But their symbolic force was immense. The age of reform was over; the age of repression had begun. The French Revolution, which had begun in 1789 as a promise of universal liberty, had by 1795 become the justification for a system of domestic surveillance and control that the defenders of the Rights of Man could never have imagined.

The Popular Response: Loyalism and the Association Movement

The radicalism of the Corresponding Societies was met by a popular counter-movement that drew on deeper reserves of popular sentiment than the reformers could command. The loyalist associations that sprang up across the country in 1792–1793 — most notably John Reeves’s Association for Preserving Liberty and Property against Republicans and Levellers — mobilised the middle and upper classes in a campaign of patriotic counter-revolution.

John Reeves, a lawyer and magistrate, founded the Association in November 1792. Its programme was simple: to support the government, to suppress seditious publications, and to defend the constitution against the new doctrines of French liberty and English radicalism. The Association published pamphlets, rewarded informers, prosecuted radical booksellers, and organised loyalist demonstrations. Within months, similar associations had been established in every county in England.

The loyalist associations were not merely tools of the government. They represented a genuine popular conservatism — a deep attachment to the monarchy, the Church of England, and the traditional institutions of English life. The Church-and-King mobs that attacked the houses of Dissenting radicals in Birmingham (1791), Manchester, and Nottingham were not government hirelings; they were the expression of a popular Anglicanism that identified religious Dissent with political sedition and regarded the Revolution as an assault on God’s ordained order.

The loyalist campaign was also a battle for control of the public sphere. The Cheap Repository Tracts of Hannah More sold in the millions — vastly exceeding the circulation of the radical press — and preached a conservative theology of submission: the poor were to be patient, industrious, and grateful; the rich were to be charitable, paternal, and vigilant. The Church of England, after decades of somnolence under the Hanoverian bishops, was galvanised into activism. The Bishop of Landaff, Richard Watson, who had been a moderate reformer in the 1780s, turned decisively against the Revolution and preached sermons that were distributed across the country as government propaganda.

The Whig Schism and the Realignment of British Politics

The French Revolution destroyed the old Whig party. For sixty years, the Whigs had defined themselves as the party of liberty — the party that had vindicated the rights of Parliament in the Glorious Revolution and that stood watch against the encroachments of the Crown. The Revolution of 1789 forced every Whig to choose: was the Revolution in France the continuation of the Whig tradition, as Fox insisted, or its repudiation, as Burke believed?

Burke’s break with Fox was the most painful political rupture of the age. The two men had been inseparable allies — on America, on India, on economical reform. When Burke published the Reflections, Fox read it with dismay and told Burke that he had “lost the Whig party.” At the climax of the debate on the Quebec Bill in May 1791, the two men clashed directly. Burke rose to attack the Revolution and the French emissaries who had been fêted by the London radicals; Fox rose in reply, defending the Revolution and denouncing Burke’s “unnatural and monstrous doctrines.” As Fox spoke, Burke lifted a small dagger that he had brought to the House and hurled it to the floor, shouting: “That is what you are to gain by an alliance with France — a dagger!” The House was in an uproar. Fox was in tears. The friendship was over, and with it the unity of the Whig party.

The schism produced three fragments. The Foxite Whigs — a minority of about sixty MPs — continued to defend the Revolution, to oppose the war, and to agitate for parliamentary reform. Fox’s popularity in the country remained enormous; his eloquence in Parliament was undimmed; but he had become the leader of a permanent opposition, never again to taste office. The Portland Whigs — the great Whig magnates under the Duke of Portland — broke with Fox and in 1794 joined Pitt’s ministry, bringing with them the parliamentary weight of the traditional Whig aristocracy. The third fragment was the Pittite Tory party — a coalition that drew strength from the loyalist associations, the country gentry, the Church of England, and the conservative Whigs who had found a home in the government. The old division of Whig and Tory, which had been eclipsed since the accession of George III, was revived and given new ideological content: Toryism became the creed of order, tradition, and resistance to revolution; Whiggism became the creed of reform, liberty, and suspicion of executive power.

Key Figures

  • Edmund Burke (1729–1797) — The author of the Reflections on the Revolution in France and the greatest counter-revolutionary thinker of the age. His break with Fox over the Revolution split the Whig party and redefined the ideological landscape of British politics. See Edmund Burke.

  • Thomas Paine (1737–1809) — The author of the Rights of Man, the most widely circulated radical text of the eighteenth century. His defence of natural right against prescriptive tradition, his denunciation of monarchy, and his programme of social reform made him the hero of the Corresponding Societies and the most wanted man in England. See Thomas Paine.

  • William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) — The Prime Minister who led Britain from neutrality through repression to total war against Revolutionary France. His government’s response to the Revolution — the Proclamation of 1792, the Treason Trials, the suspension of habeas corpus, the Two Acts — defined the terms of British domestic politics for a generation. See William Pitt the Younger.

  • Charles James Fox (1749–1806) — The leader of the Whig opposition who defended the Revolution, opposed the war, and conducted the parliamentary battle against the repressive legislation of the 1790s. His friendship with Burke was destroyed by the Revolution; his political career was consigned to a principled but powerless opposition. See Charles James Fox.

  • William Godwin (1756–1836) — The philosophical anarchist whose Enquiry Concerning Political Justice (1793) was the most intellectually ambitious work to emerge from the radical side of the Revolution Controversy. His doctrines of perfectibility and rational benevolence influenced a generation of English radicals and Romantic writers. See William Godwin.

  • Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1772–1834) — The poet and philosopher who began his career as a radical enthusiast of the Revolution, wrote odes to the fall of the Bastille, planned a utopian Pantisocracy with Robert Southey, and later repudiated his youthful radicalism to become the great conservative sage of the next generation. See Samuel Taylor Coleridge.

  • John Reeves (1752–1829) — The founder of the Association for Preserving Liberty and Property against Republicans and Levellers (1792), the most influential of the loyalist associations that mobilised popular conservatism against the radical movement.

  • Thomas Hardy (1752–1832) — The Scottish shoemaker who founded the London Corresponding Society in 1792, the first working-class political organisation in British history. Arrested and tried for high treason in 1794, he was acquitted to public rejoicing.

  • Hannah More (1745–1833) — The evangelical writer whose Cheap Repository Tracts (1795–1798) sold in millions and became the government’s most effective weapon in the battle for popular opinion. Her works preached piety, resignation, and loyalty to the poor.
