Battle of Plassey (1757)

The Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757) was the decisive engagement of the Anglo-French struggle for India during the Seven Years’ War, fought beside a mango grove on the Bhagirathi River in Bengal. A force of approximately 3,000 men under Robert Clive — comprising 900 European soldiers, 2,100 sepoys, and eight field guns — routed the army of Siraj-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal, which numbered perhaps 50,000 men with 53 cannon. The battle was decided less by force of arms than by the defection of Mir Jafar, the Nawab’s commander-in-chief, whom Clive had secretly suborned. The victory placed the richest province of the Mughal Empire under the effective control of the East India Company, filled the Company’s treasury with Bengal’s gold, and established Clive as the virtual ruler of Bengal. It is one of the hinge-points of modern world history — the moment when a London trading corporation became a territorial sovereign power.

Background

The Seven Years’ War in India

The Seven Years’ War (1756–1763), which pitted Britain against France in a global struggle for empire, had an Indian theatre as intense as any other. The Anglo-French contest in India had begun with the War of the Austrian Succession (1744–1748) and had continued, through native proxies, in the Carnatic Wars of the early 1750s. The French Compagnie des Indes, under the brilliant Joseph François Dupleix, had established a system of indirect rule through allied Indian princes — placing their own candidate on the throne of the Carnatic, dominating the court of Hyderabad, and threatening to reduce the English Company to a subordinate position on the Coromandel Coast.

Robert Clive, then a young captain in the Company’s service, had broken the French ascendancy by the defence of Arcot (1751) and a series of stunning victories that made his reputation. By 1754, when Dupleix was recalled to France, the English Company had recovered its position in southern India. But the struggle had exhausted both companies; the Carnatic was a battlefield, not a source of profit. The real prize, all men understood, was Bengal — the wealthiest province in the Mughal Empire, whose revenues were the envy of every European commercial nation.

The Black Hole of Calcutta (June 1756)

In April 1756, Alivardi Khan, the old Nawab of Bengal who had maintained an uneasy peace with the English Company for sixteen years, died at the age of eighty. He was succeeded by his grandson, Siraj-ud-Daulah, a young man of twenty-four — volatile, suspicious, debauched, and deeply resentful of the privileges and fortifications of the European trading settlements in his dominions. The English at Calcutta, accustomed to the indulgence of the old Nawab, had made no effort to conciliate the new one. They had strengthened the fortifications of Fort William without consulting him; they had given asylum to political refugees from his court; and they had conducted themselves with the arrogance of men who believed themselves beyond the reach of native authority.

Siraj-ud-Daulah’s response was swift. On 4 June 1756, he appeared before Calcutta with an army of perhaps 50,000 men and 400 elephants. The British garrison, commanded by the incompetent Governor Roger Drake and the cowardly Colonel Minchin, offered only a feeble resistance. The Governor and many of the civilians fled to the ships in the river; the fort was abandoned; and Calcutta fell without a proper defence.

The notorious incident that followed — the Black Hole of Calcutta — became the casus belli for the Company’s revenge. According to the account of John Zephaniah Holwell, the surviving senior officer, 146 European prisoners were forced into the punishment cell of the fort — a room eighteen feet square, with two small windows. The heat of a Bengal June night, suffocating and airless, killed all but twenty-three of them by morning. The figure of 123 dead was questioned by later historians, but its power over the British imagination was immense. The Black Hole became a watchword for oriental cruelty and a justification for the destruction of the Nawab’s power.

Clive’s Response

The news of the fall of Calcutta reached Madras in August 1756. The Madras Council, recognising that the loss of Bengal threatened the Company’s entire position in India, resolved on an immediate expedition to recover the settlement. The command was given to Robert Clive — now a lieutenant-colonel in the King’s army — and a naval squadron under Admiral Charles Watson was assigned to the mission. The force was small: 900 European soldiers, 1,500 sepoys, and six ships of the Royal Navy. But it was the best that Madras could spare, and Clive was its commander.

The expedition sailed in October 1756, was delayed by the monsoon, and did not reach the mouth of the Hooghly River until December. On 2 January 1757, Clive recaptured Fort William without a fight — the Nawab’s garrison, which outnumbered the British ten to one, fled at the sight of the approaching column. Calcutta was retaken. Siraj-ud-Daulah, alarmed by the speed of the British recovery, offered a treaty: he would restore the Company’s privileges, pay compensation for the loss of Calcutta, and confirm the Company’s right to fortify the settlement. Clive, whose force was too small to press the advantage, accepted.

But neither side intended to keep the peace. The Nawab immediately opened negotiations with the French at Chandernagore, the French settlement thirty miles upriver from Calcutta, urging them to join him in a combined attack on the British. Clive, learning of these intrigues through his intelligence network, struck first. On 23 March 1757, he stormed Chandernagore in a combined land-and-river operation. The French garrison, deserted by their Bengali allies, surrendered after a brief but bloody resistance. The French presence in Bengal was destroyed at a stroke.

The Conspiracy with Mir Jafar

Siraj-ud-Daulah, humiliated by the loss of Chandernagore and terrified by the prospect of a British advance on his capital at Murshidabad, now began assembling the largest army he could command. But his court was riven with conspiracy. The Nawab had alienated every powerful interest in Bengal: the Hindu financiers from whom he had extorted money; the army officers whose pay he had withheld; his own aunt, Ghaseti Begum, whose wealth he had seized; and, most dangerously, his commander-in-chief, Mir Jafar, a man of sixty years and vast ambition whom the Nawab had publicly insulted and privately suspected.

Clive, informed of the disaffection at Murshidabad by his agent William Watts, resolved on a bold stroke. He would offer the throne of Bengal to Mir Jafar in exchange for his support in the coming confrontation. The negotiations were conducted in conditions of elaborate secrecy. The chief intermediary was Jagat Seth, the greatest banker in India — a man whose wealth gave him influence over every court in the Ganges valley. The treaty was drafted in Calcutta, smuggled to Murshidabad, and signed by Mir Jafar under the seal of a confidential messenger. It promised the Company £1.5 million in compensation, exclusive trading rights, and a perpetual alliance. Mir Jafar, in turn, was promised the Nawabship.

The conspiracy was the decisive strategic stroke of the campaign. Clive, who had 3,000 men against the Nawab’s 50,000, knew that he could not win a straight fight. But a battle in which half the enemy’s army stood idle was a battle that even a small force could win. The treaty with Mir Jafar was the key that unlocked Bengal.

The Opposing Forces

The British Army

Clive’s army was small but of excellent quality. The European contingent consisted of the 39th Regiment of Foot — the first King’s regiment ever sent to India — and two battalions of the Company’s own European infantry. These men were hardened by years of Indian service, accustomed to the climate and to the tactics of native warfare. The sepoys — Indian soldiers trained and disciplined in the European manner — were drawn from the Madras battalions that Clive had led at Arcot. They were the finest native troops in Asia, as loyal to their commander as the best European veterans.

The Army of the Nawab

Siraj-ud-Daulah’s army was one of the most formidable military forces in Asia — on paper. It mustered perhaps 50,000 men, including 30,000 infantry, 18,000 cavalry, and 53 pieces of artillery, many of them heavy cannon served by French gunners under the command of M. de St. Frais. The camp presented a spectacle of Oriental magnificence: scarlet elephants in gold-embroidered housings, silken tents, glinting standards, and cavalry squadrons parading with drawn sabres.

But the army was a hollow instrument. The infantry were irregular levies — matchlockmen and spearmen — with no training in European-style volley fire. The cavalry, though numerous, had no experience of charging formed infantry. And the command structure was fatally compromised by the conspiracy at its heart.

The Battle (23 June 1757)

The March to Plassey

On 12 June 1757, Clive put his army in motion from Chandernagore, advancing up the western bank of the Bhagirathi River towards Murshidabad. The progress was slow — the roads were axle-deep in mud from the early monsoon, and the heat was oppressive. On the evening of 22 June, Clive reached the village of Plassey, or Palashi — a mango grove on the eastern bank of the Bhagirathi, some twenty miles south of Murshidabad. The Nawab’s army was encamped in the open plain beyond the grove, deployed in battle order and awaiting the British advance.

Clive held a council of war that night. The odds were absurd: 3,000 men against 50,000, with the enemy holding a strong defensive position and commanding the field with heavy artillery. The senior officers — including the cautious Major Kilpatrick — advised delay. They should wait for reinforcements from Madras before risking a general action. Clive himself was indecisive, torn between the soldier’s instinct for caution and the statesman’s knowledge that delay meant the collapse of the conspiracy. He spent much of the night in the grove, pacing beneath the mango trees, unable to settle on a course of action.

Dawn found him determined to fight.

The Opening Cannonade

At sunrise on 23 June, Clive advanced from the mango grove and deployed his army behind the high earthen bank of a large tank — a rectangular irrigation reservoir, some six feet deep and surrounded by a thick mud embankment. The tank, which later became known as “Clive’s Tank,” provided excellent cover from the enemy’s fire while allowing the British to return fire over the top.

The Nawab’s army was drawn up in a vast crescent, its centre anchored on a low ridge north of the grove, its wings extending across the plain. The French-manned guns, under St. Frais, opened the action at about eight o’clock in the morning. The heavy cannon — 24-pounders and 18-pounders — swept the mango grove with a devastating fire, crashing through the branches and sending showers of leaves and splinters over the crouching British infantry. Clive’s lighter six-pounders replied, but they were outranged and outmatched.

For three hours, from eight to eleven, the two armies remained locked in an unequal artillery duel. Clive ordered his men to lie flat behind the tank bank, conserving ammunition and shielding them from the worst of the cannonade. The casualties mounted — twenty-two killed and fifty wounded by the end of the action — but the line held. The sepoys, many of whom had never faced so concentrated a fire of heavy artillery, did not waver.

The Turning Point

At about eleven o’clock, the first of the monsoon rains swept across the field — one of those sudden, violent downpours that drench the Bengal delta in a matter of minutes. The Nawab’s gunners, who had taken no precautions against the weather, found their powder soaked and their cartridges useless. The French guns, in particular, fell silent. The British, by contrast, had been prepared: tarpaulins and oiled cloths had been spread over the ammunition boxes, and the cartridges remained dry. The British guns continued to fire.

The effect on the Nawab’s army was immediate and demoralising. The French gunners, their ammunition ruined, abandoned their pieces. The Nawab’s native gunners, less disciplined still, followed suit. The British artillery, which had been outmatched for three hours, now had the field to itself.

Siraj-ud-Daulah, watching from his elephant on the ridge, saw that Mir Jafar’s division — the largest and best-equipped in his army — had not moved. The betrayal was now unmistakable. For a long moment, the Nawab hesitated. Then his nerve broke. He ordered a general retreat.

Clive’s Advance

Clive, who had been lying in the rear of his line suffering from a fever, was roused by the sudden clamour from the enemy camp. He saw the confusion, the units drifting backward, the elephants turning away from the field. Instantly, he gave the order for a general advance.

The British army surged forward from behind the tank, deploying in line on the open plain. The 39th Foot and the Company’s Europeans advanced in perfect order, volleying into the crowded masses of the Nawab’s retreating army. The sepoys pressed forward with the bayonet, their discipline and training converting a rout into a slaughter.

The Nawab’s covering force — the division of Mir Madan, the only commander who remained loyal — made a desperate stand. Mir Madan himself was struck down by a cannonball early in the action; his men, leaderless and exposed to the converging fire of the British guns, broke and fled. The French gunners, trying desperately to rearm their pieces with dry ammunition, were overrun and bayoneted.

Within an hour of Clive’s advance, the battle was over. Siraj-ud-Daulah, abandoning his army, his treasure, and his elephant, fled the field on a camel. The British losses were reported as twenty-two killed and fifty wounded. The Nawab’s losses were perhaps five hundred. The disparity tells the story: it was a battle not of mutual destruction but of betrayal and collapse.

The Question of the Downpour

The story of the monsoon downpour — that the Nawab’s gunners failed to cover their powder while the British, forewarned or forethoughtful, protected theirs — has become the most famous anecdote of the battle. It may be apocryphal. Some accounts (including Belsham’s) do not mention it; others (including Malcolm’s) give it a central role. The likelihood is that the rain was a real event but not the decisive one — that Mir Jafar’s defection, not the weather, was the determining factor.

What is certain is that Clive’s generalship was of a high order. The choice of the tank as a defensive position, the patience during the cannonade, the pre-arrangement of the ammunition cover, the instant recognition of the moment to advance — these were the marks of a commander of real ability. The conspiracy made the victory possible; Clive’s soldiership made it certain.

The Aftermath

The Fall of Siraj-ud-Daulah

Siraj-ud-Daulah fled to Murshidabad, where he attempted to rally his supporters. But the city had already been occupied by Mir Jafar’s partisans. The deposed Nawab disguised himself as a beggar and slipped out of the capital, heading north towards Patna. He was betrayed at Rajmahal by a former servant, captured by Mir Jafar’s men, and brought back to Murshidabad.

His fate was summary. Mir Jafar, knowing that a living claimant to the throne was a permanent danger, ordered his execution. On 2 July 1757, Siraj-ud-Daulah was put to death. His body was paraded through the streets of Murshidabad on an elephant — the traditional demonstration that the old order had passed.

He was twenty-four years old. He had reigned for fourteen months.

The Installation of Mir Jafar

Mir Jafar was proclaimed Nawab of Bengal on 29 June 1757. The ceremony at Murshidabad was a masterpiece of political theatre: the new Nawab, seated on the musnud (throne) of Bengal, received the homage of the nobility while Clive and Watson looked on as the power behind the curtain.

The terms of the treaty were exacted to the letter. The Company received £1.5 million in cash as compensation for the war, plus an additional indemnity for the losses sustained by the European inhabitants of Calcutta. The Company’s servants received personal presents of enormous value: Clive accepted £234,000, Major Kilpatrick £60,000, and the other officers shares proportionate to their rank. The Company’s trading privileges were confirmed and extended. The French were expelled from Bengal forever.

The transfer of wealth was staggering. The Company’s treasury, which had been near-empty on the eve of the battle, was suddenly overflowing. The total value of the treasure extracted from Bengal in the months after Plassey has been estimated at approximately £2.5 million — a sum equivalent to the entire annual revenue of the British government at the time.

Clive as the Master of Bengal

Clive did not become Nawab — that office was left to Mir Jafar in name — but he became the effective ruler of Bengal. The Company’s military forces, now maintained at the Nawab’s expense, ensured that no decision of importance could be taken at Murshidabad without Clive’s approval. The Company’s civil servants, who had been merchants and factors, became tax-collectors and magistrates. The old trading factory system gave way to a regime of territorial sovereignty, thinly disguised as native rule.

The transformation was immediate. Where the Company had formerly been one of several European trading corporations competing for the Nawab’s favour, it was now the paramount power in the province. The Nawab was a pensioner, maintained in state at Murshidabad, but the real business of government was conducted from Calcutta. The revenues of Bengal — the richest prize in Asia — now flowed into the Company’s coffers.

Clive’s own fortune was immense. In addition to his personal share of the Nawab’s presents, he was granted a jagir (estate) yielding £27,000 per annum. He returned to England in 1760 as the richest man in Britain, was created Baron Clive of Plassey in the Peerage of Ireland, and entered Parliament. The conqueror of Bengal was thirty-four years old.

The Strategic Consequences

Plassey had consequences far beyond Bengal. The Company’s new wealth transformed its position in the global struggle against France. The revenues of Bengal financed the campaigns that destroyed French power in the Carnatic — Sir Eyre Coote’s victory at Wandiwash (1760), the capture of Pondicherry (1761), and the final expulsion of the French from India. The Company, which had been a struggling commercial enterprise on the verge of bankruptcy in 1756, was by 1760 the richest corporation in the world.

The battle also established a pattern of British expansion in India that would persist for a century: the manipulation of native succession disputes, the suborning of disaffected commanders, the decisive battle followed by the installation of a puppet ruler, and the extraction of vast indemnities in cash and commercial privileges. Clive’s method — the conspiracy before the battle — was cheaper and surer than the method of conquest by regular warfare, and it became the template for the Company’s expansion over the following decades.

The Question of Judgement

The Battle of Plassey has been subjected to radically different interpretations. To its Victorian admirers — and there were many — it was a feat of British valour against overwhelming odds, the foundation-stone of the British Empire in India, and the beginning of the civilising mission in the East. To its critics, then and since, it was a sordid transaction between European greed and Indian treachery — a battle won by bribery rather than arms, which inaugurated two centuries of colonial exploitation.

Both interpretations contain truth and falsehood. The battle was won by bribery, but it was also won by brave soldiers and a capable commander. The treasure extracted from Bengal enriched Clive and his officers beyond the dreams of avarice, but it also financed the Company’s survival in its hour of greatest peril. The regime that Plassey installed was corrupt and exploitative, but it was also — within a generation — the object of the first sustained parliamentary inquiry into the ethics of imperialism, culminating in the impeachment of Warren Hastings.

Plassey stands, therefore, as an event of irreducible moral ambiguity — a victory that was also a defeat, a crime that was also a foundation, a battle that decided the fate of a subcontinent in a single afternoon by the banks of a muddy river.

Key Figures

British

  • Robert Clive (1725–1774) — The architect of the victory and the founder of British India. A writer (clerk) turned soldier, Clive had already made his reputation at the defence of Arcot (1751). Plassey made him the virtual ruler of Bengal and one of the richest men in Britain. He later secured the Diwani of Bengal (1765), survived a parliamentary inquiry into his conduct, and died by suicide in 1774. See Robert Clive.

  • Admiral Charles Watson (1714–1757) — The naval commander who conveyed Clive’s expedition to Bengal, recaptured Calcutta, and destroyed the French settlement at Chandernagore. Watson’s squadron provided the firepower that secured the Hooghly River and prevented any French naval intervention. He died of fever at Calcutta on 16 August 1757, less than two months after the battle.

  • Major James Kilpatrick (1715–1770) — Clive’s second-in-command, who led the European infantry at Plassey. A veteran of the Carnatic wars, Kilpatrick was the cautious voice in the council of war; once committed to battle, he fought with distinction.

  • Major Sir Eyre Coote (1726–1783) — Then a young captain commanding the sepoy battalions at Plassey. Coote would later surpass his master: he destroyed French power in India at the Battle of Wandiwash (1760) and saved the Company from destruction by Hyder Ali at Porto Novo (1781). See Sir Eyre Coote.

Native

  • Siraj-ud-Daulah (1733–1757) — The last independent Nawab of Bengal. Young, volatile, and ill-advised, he inherited a throne that he could not hold and a war that he could not win. His capture of Calcutta and the Black Hole incident gave the Company its casus belli; his loss at Plassey cost him his throne and his life. His reign of fourteen months was one of the shortest and most consequential in Indian history.

  • Mir Jafar (c. 1690–1765) — The traitor who won Bengal. A general of long experience and vast ambition, Mir Jafar was the key to Clive’s conspiracy. He was installed as Nawab after Plassey but ruled as a puppet of the Company, unable to satisfy its demands for money and forced to cede more and more authority to Calcutta. He was deposed in 1760, briefly restored in 1763, and died in 1765 — a prince in name, a prisoner in fact.

  • Jagat Seth (c. 1700–1760) — The banker whose wealth and influence made the conspiracy possible. The Jagat Seth was the principal financier of the Mughal Empire, the controller of the Bengal Mint, and the most powerful private citizen in northern India. He brokered the alliance between Clive and Mir Jafar, and his support was the decisive factor in the stability of the new regime.

  • Mir Madan (d. 1757) — The only senior commander of the Nawab’s army who remained loyal to his master. He commanded the advance guard and was killed by a British cannonball during the battle. His death, together with Mir Jafar’s defection, sealed the Nawab’s fate.

  • M. de St. Frais — The French officer commanding the Nawab’s artillery. His gunners served the heavy cannon with professional skill during the opening phase of the battle; the sudden monsoon downpour, by soaking their powder, rendered them helpless at the decisive moment.
