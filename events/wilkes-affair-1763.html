The Wilkes Affair (1763–1769)

The Wilkes Affair was the most serious domestic political crisis of the early reign of George III, a protracted constitutional struggle that convulsed British politics from 1763 to 1769 and raised fundamental questions about the liberty of the press, the legality of general warrants, the scope of parliamentary privilege, and the right of electors to choose their representatives. At its centre was John Wilkes — MP for Aylesbury, journalist, libertine, and demagogue — a man whose personal dissoluteness never diminished his hold on the popular imagination or the potency of the causes he championed.

The affair unfolded in two distinct phases. The first (1763–1764) turned on Wilkes’s arrest and prosecution for seditious libel after the publication of The North Briton No. 45, and established landmark legal precedents against general warrants. The second (1768–1769) — the Middlesex election crisis — escalated the quarrel from a personal prosecution into a constitutional confrontation between the House of Commons and the electors of the largest county in England, and gave birth to the mass politics of extra-parliamentary agitation that would define British radicalism for the next half-century.

The North Briton No. 45

John Wilkes entered Parliament as MP for Aylesbury in 1757 as a follower of William Pitt the Elder, but he made his name not as a debater but as a journalist. In June 1762 he founded The North Briton, a weekly newspaper designed to attack the ministry of the Earl of Bute (John Stuart, 3rd Earl of Bute) and the peace negotiations that would end the Seven Years’ War. The paper was scurrilous, personal, and wildly popular — it gave voice to a London radicalism that the governing classes had long dismissed as beneath notice, and its success rested on Wilkes’s genius for invective and his willingness to name names.

The crisis came on 23 April 1763. King George III had delivered the speech from the throne at the close of the parliamentary session, in which he praised the recently concluded Peace of Paris as “honourable to my crown and beneficial to my people.” On the same day, Wilkes published The North Briton No. 45, in which he denounced the speech as containing “the most abandoned instigations of falsehood.” He accused the King’s ministers — and by implication the King himself — of having deceived Parliament and the nation with a peace that sacrificed the true interests of Britain.

No. 45 was not Wilkes’s most brilliant piece of writing, nor his most scurrilous. But the timing and the target were explosive. The Bute ministry had already resigned (Bute had stepped down on 8 April 1763, replaced by George Grenville), but Wilkes’s attack seemed to strike at the Crown itself. The new Grenville ministry, stung by the insult and eager to demonstrate its authority, determined to crush him.

The General Warrant

On 29 April 1763, the Earl of Halifax (George Montagu-Dunk, 2nd Earl of Halifax), as one of the two Secretaries of State, issued a general warrant — a warrant that named no specific individual but ordered the arrest of “the authors, printers, and publishers” of The North Briton No. 45. Under this warrant, the Secretary of State’s messengers arrested forty-nine people over the course of several days — compositors, pressmen, news vendors — before they finally reached Wilkes himself. His house was entered, his personal papers were seized, and he was committed to the Tower of London.

The use of general warrants was not unprecedented, but it had always been controversial. Such warrants had been employed against Jacobite printers in earlier decades, and the Secretaries of State had long claimed the right to issue them in cases of seditious libel. But no court had ever definitively ruled on their legality. The sheer sweep of the arrests — forty-nine people for a single anonymous article — shocked public opinion and turned the case from a prosecution of a libeller into a test of the limits of executive power.

The Legal Battle: Chief Justice Pratt’s Judgment

Wilkes was brought before the Court of Common Pleas on a writ of habeas corpus. His counsel argued two points: first, that the general warrant was illegal, as it did not name the person to be arrested; second, that as a Member of Parliament, Wilkes was protected by parliamentary privilege from arrest for libel during a session of Parliament.

On 6 May 1763, Lord Chief Justice Charles Pratt (later created Lord Camden) delivered his judgment. It was a landmark of English constitutional law. Pratt ruled that Wilkes was entitled to privilege of Parliament and must be discharged from custody. More importantly, he declared that general warrants — warrants that did not name the person to be arrested — were “unconstitutional, illegal, and absolutely void.” He ordered the Secretary of State’s messengers to be prosecuted for trespass.

The judgment was a sensation. The City of London voted the freedom of the City to Chief Justice Pratt, presented to him in a golden box; his portrait was placed in Guildhall. Belsham records that Pratt “became the idol of the nation.” For the first time, the principle that a man could not be arrested without a warrant naming him was established as a matter of law — a victory for English liberty that Wilkes, whatever his personal defects, had made possible.

The Expulsion and Flight

The ministry, however, did not surrender. While Pratt’s judgment freed Wilkes from the Tower, it did not prevent Parliament from taking its own action. The Commons was a jealous guardian of its privileges, and the ministry had a comfortable majority. On 24 November 1763, after a protracted debate, the House of Commons voted by a great majority “that THE NORTH BRITON, No. 45, is a false, scandalous, and seditious libel, manifestly tending to alienate the affections of the people.” On the same day, the Commons resolved that John Wilkes was guilty of writing and publishing the libel, and ordered his expulsion from the House.

Wilkes had already fled to France — not only to escape the parliamentary prosecution but because a second charge had been prepared against him. During the original search of his papers, the ministry had seized a copy of an obscene and blasphemous poem, the Essay on Woman, which Wilkes had printed privately for his amusement among a small circle of friends. The poem, which parodied Pope’s Essay on Man, was of no political significance, but its publication gave the ministry a weapon against him that Pratts judgment could not touch.

Wilkes was tried in absentia in the Court of King’s Bench, found guilty of publishing both the North Briton No. 45 and the Essay on Woman, and outlawed. He remained in France for four years, moving restlessly through the literary and political salons of Paris, corresponding with his English supporters, and waiting for his moment.

The Return and the Middlesex Elections

In February 1768, after four years of exile, Wilkes returned to England. The political mood had shifted. The Grenville ministry had fallen in 1765; the Rockingham ministry that followed had repealed the Stamp Act but lasted barely a year; the Chatham ministry (with Pitt now Earl of Chatham in the Lords) was in disarray, with the Duke of Grafton effectively managing the government. The great popular currents that had swirled around the North Briton had not dissipated — they had only deepened.

Wilkes presented himself to the Court of King’s Bench to be discharged from his outlawry. The court confirmed his conviction but refused to hear his appeal against outlawry on a technicality, and he was sentenced to twenty-two months’ imprisonment. He was carried to the King’s Bench Prison in St. George’s Fields, Southwark.

His imprisonment became the occasion for the mass politics that defined the second phase of his career. Crowds gathered outside the prison, chanting “Wilkes and Liberty!” — the slogan that became the cry of the London mob and the emblem of the movement. On 10 May 1768, a crowd estimated at fifteen thousand assembled in St. George’s Fields. Soldiers of the Scottish regiment stationed nearby — an inflammatory choice in itself — were called out. They fired into the crowd, killing several people. The Massacre of St. George’s Fields, as it became known, turned a political crisis into a popular insurgency.

In the general election of March 1768, while still a prisoner and an outlaw in the eyes of the law, Wilkes had stood for Parliament. He was defeated in the City of London but then stood for the County of Middlesex — the most populous and politically conscious constituency in the kingdom, encompassing the northern and western suburbs of London. He was elected by a overwhelming majority.

The Crisis of 1769

Parliament refused to seat him. On 3 February 1769, the House of Commons voted to expel him for the libels of 1763. The freeholders of Middlesex immediately re-elected him. The House expelled him again. The freeholders returned him a second time. When the House expelled him a fourth time and declared Wilkes incapable of being elected to the present Parliament, Middlesex returned him yet again.

At this point, the ministry under the Duke of Grafton determined on a drastic course. Rather than allow the cycle of expulsion and re-election to continue indefinitely, the House declared on 17 March 1769 that Wilkes’s opponent, Henry Luttrell, was the duly elected member for Middlesex — despite Luttrell having received fewer than 300 votes to Wilkes’s 1,143.

This was the decisive moment. The question was no longer whether John Wilkes was a fit person to sit in Parliament — a question on which reasonable people could disagree — but whether the House of Commons could nullify the votes of a free constituency and install a candidate whom the electors had rejected. The principle at stake was the right of election itself.

Popular Support: Wilkes and Liberty

The Middlesex election crisis transformed Wilkes from a journalist and libertine into a popular hero, the symbol of resistance against an overbearing executive. The cry of “Wilkes and Liberty!” swept through London and the provinces. Effigies of Lord Bute and the Earl of Halifax were burned. Wilkes’s prison in the King’s Bench became a place of pilgrimage. Subscriptions poured in from across the country to pay his debts and finance his campaigns.

The Society of the Supporters of the Bill of Rights was founded in February 1769, initially to defray Wilkes’s enormous legal expenses — estimated at £20,000 — but quickly expanding into the first organised extra-parliamentary pressure group in British politics. The Society published accounts of its expenditures, circulated petitions, organised public meetings, and agitated for parliamentary reform. It was the prototype of the radical clubs and associations that would transform British politics over the following half-century.

The crisis also produced a torrent of pamphlets and essays. Edmund Burke, who had entered Parliament in 1765 as secretary to the Marquess of Rockingham, published his Thoughts on the Cause of the Present Discontents in 1770 — a work that, while not directly about Wilkes, analysed the same constitutional disease: the growth of the influence of the Crown and the corruption of Parliament by the “king’s friends.” Lord Chatham (as Pitt had become) supported Wilkes’s cause in the Lords, though his health was failing and his interventions were intermittent.

The city of London — the great commercial and financial heart of the kingdom — stood solidly behind Wilkes. The Common Council voted him its thanks, and the Livery of London made him their champion. The freedom of the City was voted to him. London’s support gave the Wilkesite movement a base of wealth and institutional power that no amount of ministerial pressure could break.

The Aftermath and the Constitutional Crisis

The Middlesex election crisis was unresolved. The Grafton ministry had triumphed in the short term: Luttrell sat in the Commons, and the principle of virtual representation — that the Commons could judge the qualifications of its own members — was upheld by the ministerial majority. But the cost was immense. The crisis had exposed the unreformed electoral system to public scrutiny as never before. The case of the Middlesex electors — 1,143 voters whose choice had been set aside by an oligarchical House — became the rallying cry of every reform movement for the next half-century.

The constitutional question was fundamental: could the House of Commons, by a simple majority vote, exclude a member duly elected by a free constituency? If it could, then the Commons was not a representative body but a self-perpetuating oligarchy, accountable to no one but itself. The Wilkesites argued that the House’s claim to be “the judge of its own elections” — a claim traditionally used to protect the Commons against the Crown — had been perverted into a weapon against the electors themselves.

The crisis also had a profound effect on the American colonies. American newspapers followed the Wilkes affair with intense interest. The struggle against general warrants and the cry of “Wilkes and Liberty” resonated powerfully in Boston and New York, where comparable issues of executive power and the rights of Englishmen were being contested. The Middlesex election seemed, from an American perspective, a warning of what parliamentary supremacy might mean — a House of Commons that could nullify the votes of Englishmen at home could surely do the same to colonists abroad. When the American crisis escalated in the 1770s, Wilkes — by then Lord Mayor of London — became one of the colonists’ most prominent British allies, presenting their petitions to the King and denouncing the Coercive Acts.

The Resolution

The crisis did not come to a formal conclusion until 1774, five years after the expulsion. In the general election of that year, Wilkes — now serving as Lord Mayor of London, to which dignity he had been elected in 1774 — was again returned unopposed for Middlesex. This time, with a new Parliament and a changed political mood, the House allowed him to take his seat. He had been a member in exile for six years; he was now, at last, a member in possession.

The resolutions that had declared Luttrell elected and Wilkes incapable of sitting were eventually expunged from the Journals of the House of Commons in 1782 — a posthumous vindication of the principle for which the Middlesex electors had fought.

Key Figures

John Wilkes (1725–1797)

The central figure of the affair — MP, journalist, libertine, and radical demagogue. Wilkes was not a systematic thinker nor a principled reformer; he was a wit, a rake, and a man of immense personal courage whose battles against authority established precedents that became foundations of English liberty. His ugliness was legendary, his charm irresistible, and his resilience extraordinary.

George III (1738–1820)

The King whose Speech provoked Wilkes’s attack, and whose prerogative was indirectly challenged by the crisis. George III gave his full support to the ministries that prosecuted Wilkes and expelled him from the Commons. The crisis deepened the King’s conviction that the press was licentious and that the popular agitation against his government was the work of faction and sedition.

The Earl of Bute (John Stuart, 3rd Earl of Bute, 1713–1792)

The prime minister whose ministry was the target of Wilkes’s attacks in The North Briton. Bute had resigned before the arrest, but his shadow hung over the affair. The popular hatred of “the Scots favourite” — the King’s former tutor, a Scot, and a man of inexperience — gave Wilkes’s journalism much of its venom and appeal.

The Earl of Halifax (George Montagu-Dunk, 2nd Earl of Halifax, 1716–1771)

The Secretary of State who issued the general warrant against Wilkes. Halifax was a hard-liner on questions of executive authority and colonial policy, and his willingness to use the full power of the state against Wilkes turned the case into a test of the limits of that power.

Charles Pratt, Lord Camden (1714–1794)

Lord Chief Justice of the Common Pleas, later Lord Chancellor. Pratt’s judgment in May 1763 — declaring general warrants illegal and parliamentary privilege applicable to libel — was the most important judicial victory of the affair and a landmark of English constitutional law. He was raised to the peerage as Lord Camden in 1766 and became Lord Chancellor in the Chatham ministry.

William Pitt the Elder (1708–1778)

Wilkes’s early political patron, who had brought him into Parliament for Aylesbury. By 1763 Pitt had resigned from office and was in the political wilderness; he spoke in defence of Wilkes’s cause but was constrained by his own complicated relationship with the King and the ministry. He returned to office in 1766 as Earl of Chatham, by which time the Wilkes affair had moved into its second phase.

Edmund Burke (1729–1797)

The great Whig philosopher-politician, who entered Parliament in 1765 and became the Rockingham Whigs’ most brilliant spokesman. Burke’s Thoughts on the Cause of the Present Discontents (1770), written in the wake of the Wilkes crisis, analysed the constitutional disease that the affair had exposed: the growth of the influence of the Crown and the corruption of the representative system. Burke also defended the principle that the House of Commons should respect the choice of electors, though he distrusted Wilkes personally as a demagogue.

The Duke of Grafton (Augustus FitzRoy, 3rd Duke of Grafton, 1735–1811)

Prime Minister during the Middlesex election crisis (1768–1770). Grafton, a grandson of the second duke and a protege of Chatham, led the ministry that expelled Wilkes and declared Luttrell elected. His government never recovered from the damage, and he resigned in January 1770, to be replaced by Lord North — a change that did not, however, alter the ministry’s Wilkes policy.

Henry Luttrell (1737–1821)

The defeated candidate whom the House of Commons declared elected for Middlesex. A soldier and placeman, Luttrell’s name became a byword for parliamentary corruption. He was awarded £500 for his “trouble” by the Treasury, sat in Parliament for the remainder of the term, and lived under a cloud of popular contempt for the rest of his life.
