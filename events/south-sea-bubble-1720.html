South Sea Bubble (1720)

The South Sea Bubble of 1720 was the greatest financial catastrophe in eighteenth-century Britain — a speculative mania that gripped the entire nation, from the King’s court to the humblest tradesman, and ended in ruin for thousands. It began as a bold scheme to reduce the national debt, degenerated into an orgy of bribery, fraud, and corruption reaching into the highest offices of state, and concluded with the collapse of public credit, a parliamentary inquiry that exposed systemic rot, and the political extinction of the King’s first ministry. Out of the wreckage emerged Robert Walpole, whose masterful handling of the crisis made him indispensable and launched the longest premiership in British history.

The affair takes its name from the South Sea Company, a trading corporation whose stock rose from £128 in January 1720 to £1,050 in June, before crashing to £150 by September of the same year. It was the English counterpart to John Law’s Mississippi Scheme in France, and together they formed the twin pillars of the great European speculative bubble of 1719–1720.

Origins: The South Sea Company (1711–1719)

Foundation under Harley

The South Sea Company was incorporated in 1711 by Robert Harley, Earl of Oxford, as a centrepiece of his Tory ministry’s financial policy. The country had emerged from the War of the Spanish Succession burdened with a national debt of some £10 million — much of it in short-term, high-interest obligations that the government could not readily service. Harley’s plan was to convert these floating debts (some £9.47 million of “unprovided-for” debts, as Campbell records) into perpetual annuities at 6%, secured against the customs revenues, and to create a joint-stock company that would undertake this conversion in exchange for a monopoly of trade to the South Seas — the Spanish Americas.

The commercial logic was thin but seductive. The Bristol merchants who had sent the privateers Duke and Duchess under Captain Woodes Rogers and Captain Stephen Courtney to the South Seas in 1708 — with the famous navigator William Dampier as pilot — had returned in 1711 with rich prizes, “the wealth of the South Seas came to make a great noise” (Campbell). Harley’s ministry seized on this to argue that the trade could be made as lucrative as the East India trade. To fix the idea in the public mind, rumours were circulated in Holland that great profits were to be made, and several treatises were published in 1711–1712 advocating the scheme.

The charter of the new company vested in it the sole trade to the South Seas — a grant of enormous potential scope, encompassing the entire Pacific coast of the Americas. The debts were liquidated, the annuities granted, and the company incorporated “for carrying on a trade to the South Seas” (Campbell).

The Asiento (1713)

The company’s commercial prospects received a dramatic boost from the Treaty of Utrecht (1713). By the Asiento de Negros — the exclusive contract to supply the Spanish West Indies with enslaved Africans — Britain secured a thirty-year monopoly on one of the most profitable trades in the Atlantic world. The contract was vested in the South Sea Company by the King’s order, giving it the right not only to supply the Spanish colonies with 144,000 slaves over the term but also to send one “annual ship” of 500 tons to the trade fairs of Spanish America.

This gave the company a real, if limited, commercial foundation. But the annual ship was a pittance compared to the scale of the company’s capital, and the slave trade itself — though profitable — could never generate returns sufficient to justify the valuations that would soon attach to the stock. The company was, from the start, a financial engine disguised as a trading corporation.

The South Sea Scheme (1720)

After the collapse of the Jacobite rising of 1715 and the consolidation of Whig power under the Sunderland-Stanhope ministry, the national debt had grown still larger. In 1719, John Law’s Mississippi Company in France had successfully taken over much of the French national debt, sparking a speculative fever in Paris. The directors of the South Sea Company — led by the projector John Blunt, a man of “moderate talents” according to Smollett, but possessed of remarkable audacity — proposed to do for Britain what Law had done for France.

The plan, which became law as the South Sea Act in April 1720, was breathtaking in its scope. The company would take over the entire national debt — some £31 million — by exchanging its own stock for government annuities. In return, the government would reduce its annual interest payments from ~£1.5 million to ~£600,000, saving the Exchequer heavily. The company’s profit would come from the difference between the interest it received from the government and the dividends it paid to its stockholders — a profit that would be vastly multiplied if the stock could be driven to ever-higher prices.

The bill passed both Houses of Parliament with astonishing speed. Smollett, Belsham, and Campbell all record that the passage was secured by bribery on a massive scale. The directors created a fictitious stock of £574,000 — “fictitious stock” that had never been subscribed for but was distributed as gifts to those who could advance the bill.

The Mania (January–June 1720)

The Stock Rises

When the South Sea Act passed, the stock stood at about £128. It rose steadily through the spring. But a far more dramatic surge came when Blunt and the directors began to spread rumours calculated to inflame the imagination of the public. The most potent was the report — baseless — that Gibraltar and Port Mahon would be exchanged for places in Peru, by which means “the English trade to the South Sea would be protected and enlarged” (Smollett).

“This rumour, diffused by his emissaries, acted like a contagion. In five days the directors opened their books for a subscription of one million, at the rate of three hundred pounds for every hundred pounds capital. Persons of all ranks crowded to the house in such a manner, that the first subscription exceeded two millions of original stock. In a few days this stock advanced to three hundred and forty pounds; and the subscriptions were sold for double the price of the first payment.” — Smollett, Complete History of England, Vol. X

By June 1720, the stock had reached £1,050. The nation was seized by “a kind of national delirium” (Smollett). Exchange Alley was crowded with statesmen, clergymen, churchmen and dissenters, Whigs and Tories, physicians, lawyers, tradesmen, and multitudes of women. All other professions and employments were utterly neglected.

The Flood of Bubble Companies

The mania was not confined to South Sea stock. Imitation companies — “Bubbles” — sprang up by the score, “under the countenance of the prime nobility” (Smollett). The Prince of Wales (the future George II) allowed himself to be constituted governor of the Welch Copper Company; the Duke of Chandos headed the York-Building Company; the Duke of Bridgewater formed a third for building houses in London and Westminster. About a hundred such schemes were projected and put into execution, “to the ruin of many thousands.”

The sums proposed to be raised by these expedients amounted to three hundred millions sterling — exceeding, as Smollett notes with evident horror, the value of all the land in England. One obscure projector, who refused to explain his scheme, simply took subscriptions of two guineas each, promising to disclose the project in a month. In one forenoon he received a thousand subscriptions; by evening he had left the kingdom.

The King, before departing for Hanover, issued a proclamation against these unlawful projects. The Lords Justices dismissed all petitions for charters, and the Prince of Wales renounced the company of which he had been elected governor. But the damage was done.

Bribery and Corruption

The most corrupt aspect of the mania was the systematic bribery of the political class. The committee of secrecy later discovered that, before any subscription could be made, a fictitious stock of £574,000 had been disposed of by the directors to facilitate the passing of the bill. Great part of this was distributed among:

  • Charles Spencer, 3rd Earl of Sunderland — First Lord of the Treasury and the King’s chief minister in England
  • James Craggs the elder — Postmaster-General
  • The Duchess of Kendal — the King’s German mistress (Melusine von der Schulenburg)
  • The Countess of Platen and her two nieces — the King’s half-sister and her circle
  • James Craggs the younger — Secretary of State
  • John Aislabie — Chancellor of the Exchequer

The directors also engaged in a massive fraud against the company itself, buying its own stock at artificially high prices on the pretence of supporting the market, while secretly selling their own holdings — “a notorious fraud and breach of trust” in the words of the House of Lords’ resolutions.

The Crash (September–December 1720)

Collapse of the Stock

The infatuation prevailed until 8 September 1720, when the stock began to fall. Then, as Smollett describes it, “some of the adventurers awake from their delirium.” The number of sellers daily increased. By 29 September, the stock had sunk to £150. Several eminent goldsmiths and bankers who had lent great sums upon the stock were obliged to stop payment and abscond.

“The ebb of this portentous tide was so violent, that it bore down everything in its way; and an infinite number of families was overwhelmed with ruin. Public credit sustained a terrible shock: the nation was thrown into a dangerous ferment; and nothing was heard but the ravings of grief, disappointment, and despair.” — Smollett, Complete History of England, Vol. X

The Bank of England and the Threat to National Credit

Some principal members of the ministry, deeply concerned in the fraudulent transactions, employed all their influence with the Bank of England to support the credit of the South Sea Company. The Bank reluctantly agreed to subscribe into the stock (valued at 400%) the sum of £3.5 million, which the South Sea Company was to repay at Lady Day and Michaelmas of the following year. Books were opened at the Bank to take in subscriptions for the support of public credit, and considerable sums came in.

By this expedient the stock was raised at first, and those who contrived it seized the opportunity to sell. But the bankruptcy of the goldsmiths and the Sword-Blade Company (the South Sea’s own banker) from the fall of the stock occasioned such a run upon the Bank that “the money was paid away faster than it could be received from the subscription” (Smollett). The stock fell again. The directors of the Bank, finding themselves in danger of being involved in the company’s ruin, renounced the agreement.

All expedients having failed, and the clamours of the people daily increasing, expresses were dispatched to George I at Hanover, pressing him to return. He shortened his stay in Germany and arrived in England on 11 November 1720.

The Parliamentary Inquiry (December 1720 – August 1721)

The Commons Acts

Parliament assembled on 8 December 1720. The King expressed his concern for “the unhappy turn of affairs, which had so deeply affected the public credit at home” and earnestly desired the Commons to consider the most effectual methods to restore credit. The lower house was too much interested in the calamity to postpone consideration.

The directors were ordered to produce an account of all their proceedings. Sir Joseph Jekyll moved for a select committee to examine the particulars. Robert Walpole, then Paymaster of the Forces, argued that such a method would protract the inquiry while public credit lay in a “bleeding condition.” He told the house he had formed a scheme for restoring public credit, but before communicating it, desired to know whether the subscriptions of the public debts, money subscriptions, and other contracts with the South Sea Company should remain in their present state. After a warm debate, the question was carried in the affirmative.

Walpole then produced his scheme: to ingraft nine millions of South Sea stock into the Bank of England, and a like sum into the East India Company, on certain conditions. The house voted that proposals should be received from these corporations, and a bill was passed into law.

A second bill restrained the sub-governor, deputy-governor, directors, treasurer, and other officers of the South Sea Company from quitting the kingdom, and provided for discovering their estates.

The Committee of Secrecy

A committee of secrecy was chosen by ballot to examine all books, papers, and proceedings relating to the execution of the South Sea Act. Their work produced devastating revelations. In the words of the committee’s report, they had discovered “a train of the deepest villainy and fraud that hell ever contrived to ruin a nation” (Smollett).

The persons of Sir George Caswell, Sir John Blunt, Sir John Lambert, Sir John Fellows, and Mr. Grigsby were taken into custody. Sir Theodore Janssen, Mr. Sawbridge, Sir Robert Chaplain, and Mr. Eyles were expelled from the House and apprehended. John Aislabie resigned his employment as Chancellor of the Exchequer and Lord of the Treasury. Orders were given to remove all directors of the South Sea Company from the places they possessed under the government.

The Lords and the Exposure of Political Corruption

The Lords, in the course of their examination, discovered that large portions of South Sea stock had been given to several persons in the administration and House of Commons for promoting the passage of the South Sea Act. The House immediately resolved that “this practice was a notorious and most dangerous species of corruption.”

Earl Stanhope distinguished himself by his vehemence. He declared that “the estates of the criminals, whether directors or not directors, ought to be confiscated to repair the public losses.” He was seconded by Lord Carteret, and even by the Earl of Sunderland himself — a man whose own hands were deeply stained. The Duke of Wharton declared he would give up his best friend if found guilty.

But the strain of the inquiry told on Stanhope. In a violent debate in the Lords, the Duke of Wharton made a speech that seemed to reflect on the ministry. Stanhope, “conceiving this reflection was aimed at him, was seized with a transport of anger. He undertook to vindicate the ministry; and spoke with such vehemence as produced a violent head-ach, which obliged him to retire.” He underwent proper evacuations and seemed to recover, but the next day — 5 February 1721 — he became lethargic, was seized with a suffocation, and “instantly expired” (Smollett).

A few days later (16 February), Secretary of State James Craggs the younger died of smallpox. The elder James Craggs died of lethargy before he could undergo the censure of the House. The House resolved, nevertheless, that he was “a notorious accomplice with Robert Knight, and some of the directors, in carrying on their scandalous practices,” and ordered that all his estate from 1 December 1720 be applied to the relief of the sufferers.

The Fate of the Directors

The committee of secrecy found that before any subscription could be made, the fictitious stock of £574,000 had been distributed. Among the recipients was the Earl of Sunderland, for whom £50,000 in stock had been taken by the cashier Knight. The House engaged in a violent dispute over Sunderland’s guilt. The whole strength of the ministry was mustered in his defence. The majority declared him innocent — “the nation in general was of another opinion” (Smollett). He resigned his place of First Commissioner in the Treasury, which was bestowed upon Robert Walpole.

Against John Aislabie, the evidence was overwhelming. The Commons resolved that he had “promoted the destructive execution of the South Sea scheme, with a view to his own exorbitant profit, and combined with the directors in their pernicious practices, to the ruin of public credit.” He was expelled from the House and committed to the Tower of London.

The directors delivered inventories of their estates, which were confiscated by Act of Parliament towards making good the damages sustained by the company, after a certain allowance was deducted for each according to his conduct and circumstances.

Robert Knight’s Flight

Knight, the cashier of the South Sea Company, who had been entrusted with the secrets of the whole affair, fled the kingdom. A proclamation was issued for his apprehension. He was seized at Tirlemont by the vigilance of Mr. Gandot, secretary to the British resident at Brussels, and confined in the citadel of Antwerp. The British government applied to the court of Vienna for his extradition, but the States of Brabant insisted on their privilege that no person apprehended for any crime in Brabant should be tried in any other country. Before the dispute could be resolved, Knight escaped from the citadel — and with him, the most damaging testimony against the inner circle of the ministry.

Walpole’s Scheme for the Restoration of Credit

Robert Walpole emerged as the dominant figure of the crisis. He had not been on the board of the South Sea Company and had publicly warned against the scheme during the mania — though it was later known that he had profited from stock dealings. More importantly, he had the necessary distance from the scandal and the financial acumen to devise a workable restoration plan.

His scheme — the “ingraftment” — was enacted into law in the summer of 1721. The total capital stock at the end of 1720 amounted to about £37.8 million. The stock allotted to all the proprietors did not exceed £24.5 million; the remaining capital belonged to the company in its corporate capacity. Walpole’s bill provided that:

  1. £7 million should be paid to the public out of the company’s profits
  2. Certain additions were made to the proprietors’ stock out of the company’s own stock
  3. A particular distribution of stock amounting to £2.2 million was made
  4. Upon remitting £5 million of the £7 million to the public, £2 million of the capital was annihilated
  5. After these distributions, the remaining capital was divided among all the proprietors — a dividend of £33 6s 8d per cent
  6. The company lost some £8.9 million in the process
  7. All debtors to the company on loans advanced on stock (over £11 million) were discharged upon paying 10%
  8. On this article, the company’s loss exceeded £6.9 million

The proprietors loudly complained of being deprived of £2 million, and the parliament eventually revived that sum. But the essential object was achieved: the South Sea Company was placed in a condition to fulfil its engagements with the public, the ferment of the people subsided, and “the credit of the nation was restored” (Smollett).

Aftermath and Significance

The Fall of the Sunderland Ministry

The South Sea Bubble destroyed the Whig ministry that had governed Britain since the accession of George I. The principal casualties were:

  • Earl Stanhope — dead of apoplexy provoked by the stress of the parliamentary inquiry (February 1721)
  • James Craggs the younger — dead of smallpox (February 1721)
  • James Craggs the elder — dead; his estate confiscated
  • John Aislabie — expelled from the Commons and imprisoned in the Tower
  • Charles Spencer, 3rd Earl of Sunderland — acquitted by a narrow majority in the Commons, but politically destroyed; forced to resign the Treasury
  • Sir John Blunt — taken into custody; fortune forfeited
  • The directors of the South Sea Company — stripped of their estates and forbidden to hold office in the Company, the Bank of England, or the East India Company

The Rise of Walpole

The great beneficiary of the catastrophe was Robert Walpole. He had been out of office when the South Sea Act was passed, having resigned from the ministry in 1717 during the Whig schism. He had been brought back into the ministry as Paymaster-General in June 1720 — too late to influence the South Sea Act but early enough to avoid contamination. When the crisis broke, he was the only senior figure with the financial competence, the parliamentary skills, and the personal integrity (or at least the appearance of it) to restore confidence.

The King, who deeply regretted the death of Stanhope, appointed Lord Townshend — Walpole’s brother-in-law — as Secretary of State in Stanhope’s place. Walpole himself became First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer in April 1721. He would remain the dominant figure in British politics for the next twenty-one years.

The irony was profound. The South Sea Bubble — the greatest financial scandal of the century — made Walpole indispensable. His restoration scheme demonstrated his mastery of finance and his capacity for practical, workable solutions. His refusal to pursue Sunderland to destruction displayed his political magnanimity (or calculation). His distance from the corrupt inner circle of the Company gave him the moral authority to clean up the mess.

The Restoration of Public Credit

By the end of 1721, the immediate crisis was over. The stock of the South Sea Company was reduced to manageable proportions. The Bank of England and the East India Company had absorbed the ingrafted stock. The directors had been punished, their estates confiscated, and the public’s anger — at least the most dangerous edge of it — had been satisfied.

The long-term consequences were more ambiguous. The Bubble discredited the very idea of joint-stock companies and public debt conversion schemes for a generation. The Bubble Act of 1720, which restricted the formation of joint-stock companies without a royal charter, was passed in the aftermath and remained law until 1825. But it also entrenched the Whig oligarchy. The crisis had demonstrated that the financial system — the Bank, the East India Company, the funded debt — was too important to be left to speculators and projectors. It needed management by men of substance, of property, of parliamentary weight. It needed Robert Walpole.

“Mr. Walpole, now paymaster of the forces, observed, that such a method would protract the inquiry, while the public credit lay in a bleeding condition. He told the house he had formed a scheme for restoring public credit.” — Smollett, Complete History of England, Vol. X

Key Figures

Sir John Blunt (1665–1733)

The projector of the South Sea Scheme. A former scrivener turned financier, Blunt was the driving force behind the Company’s operations. Smollett describes him as a man of “moderate talents” who nevertheless “was able to impose upon the whole nation, and make tools of the other directors, to serve his own purposes.” He was responsible for the fictitious stock distributions, the bribery of politicians, and the manipulation of the stock price. Arrested, his estate confiscated, he died in obscurity.

Charles Spencer, 3rd Earl of Sunderland (1675–1722)

First Lord of the Treasury and the King’s chief minister in England during George I’s frequent absences in Hanover. Sunderland was deeply implicated in the South Sea corruption — the committee of secrecy found that £50,000 in stock had been taken for his use by the cashier Knight. He was acquitted in the Commons by a narrow majority, but his reputation was destroyed. He resigned the Treasury to Robert Walpole in April 1721 and died the following year.

John Aislabie (1670–1742)

Chancellor of the Exchequer and a leading figure in the passage of the South Sea Act. Aislabie was the highest-ranking minister to be directly convicted. He was expelled from the House of Commons, committed to the Tower of London, and his estate forfeited. He retired to his Yorkshire estate, where he devoted himself to landscape gardening at Studley Royal.

Robert Walpole (1676–1745)

Paymaster of the Forces at the time of the crisis; First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer from April 1721. Walpole’s role in the South Sea affair was masterfully ambiguous: he had profited from stock dealings but had not been a director nor involved in the passage of the Act. His ingraftment scheme restored public credit, his parliamentary management secured the necessary legislation, and his refusal to pursue Sunderland to a fatal conclusion displayed both magnanimity and calculation. The crisis made him the indispensable man of the age.

James Craggs the Elder (1657–1721)

Postmaster-General and a principal conduit for the distribution of fictitious stock. He died of lethargy in February 1721 before the House could censure him, but the Commons resolved that he was “a notorious accomplice” with Knight and the directors, and ordered his entire estate from December 1720 to be applied to the relief of the sufferers.

James Craggs the Younger (1686–1721)

Secretary of State, son of the above. One of the most promising younger Whig politicians, Craggs died of smallpox on 16 February 1721 — cutting short a career that might have rivalled Walpole’s. His involvement in the South Sea corruption was less deep than his father’s, but the committee’s discoveries would likely have destroyed him politically had he lived.

George I (1660–1727)

King of Great Britain and Ireland from 1714. George was in Hanover for much of the crisis, leaving the ministry under Sunderland’s direction. The King’s German mistresses — particularly the Duchess of Kendal and the Countess of Platen — were among the chief recipients of South Sea bribes, a fact that tainted the court but which the inquiry did not press. George returned in November 1720, opened Parliament in December, and accepted the political destruction of his first ministry. His appointment of Walpole in 1721 was the most important decision of his reign.

Legacy

The South Sea Bubble stands as the archetypal financial mania in English history — the original cautionary tale of speculative excess, systemic corruption, and the entanglement of high finance and high politics. It destroyed one ministry and created another. It ruined thousands of families and laid the foundations of the Walpole system that governed Britain for two decades.

The Bubble left a permanent mark on British law and institutions. The Bubble Act 1720 (6 Geo. I, c. 18) restricted the formation of joint-stock companies and was not repealed until 1825. The scandal entrenched the practice of parliamentary inquiries into financial misconduct. And the disaster — unfolding simultaneously with John Law’s Mississippi Scheme in France — demonstrated to a generation of European statesmen and investors the catastrophic risks of debt conversion schemes that depended on rising stock prices.

In the longer arc of British history, the South Sea Bubble was the crucible in which the modern fiscal-military state was tested and strengthened. The Bank of England, the East India Company, and the funded national debt all survived the crisis — indeed, emerged from it more robust, having demonstrated their capacity to withstand the worst that speculation could inflict. The system proved more resilient than the men who had tried to game it.
