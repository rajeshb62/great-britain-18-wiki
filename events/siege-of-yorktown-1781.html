Siege of Yorktown (28 Sept – 19 Oct 1781)

The Siege of Yorktown (28 September – 19 October 1781) was the culminating military engagement of the American Revolutionary War in North America. After years of bloody stalemate in the northern colonies, the British high command adopted a southern strategy that initially met with dazzling success — the capture of Savannah (1778), Charleston (1780), and the crushing defeat of General Gates at Camden — only for Lieutenant General Charles, Earl Cornwallis to pursue an ultimately disastrous campaign that ended with the surrender of his entire army to a combined Franco-American force at Yorktown, Virginia. The news of the catastrophe reached London in November 1781, prompting Lord North’s legendary cry — “Oh God! It is all over!” — and precipitating the collapse of his ministry in March 1782.

Background: The Southern Strategy

Through the first three years of the war, British arms had failed to deliver a decisive blow in the northern theatre. The Saratoga Campaign of 1777 had ended in the catastrophic surrender of General John Burgoyne’s army at Saratoga, bringing France into the war on the American side. The British evacuation of Philadelphia in 1778 and the subsequent stalemate around New York convinced the ministry of George III and his chief minister Lord North that a new approach was needed.

The so-called southern strategy rested on the premise that the southern colonies — the Carolinas, Georgia, and Virginia — contained a larger proportion of loyalist sentiment than New England, and that a British military presence there would rally the King’s friends to restore royal government. In December 1778, a British expedition captured Savannah, Georgia, and by early 1780 the theatre had become the focus of the war.

The Fall of Charleston and Camden

In May 1780, after a six-week siege, a British army under Sir Henry Clinton and Lord Cornwallis compelled the surrender of Charleston, South Carolina — the worst American defeat of the war. Some 5,500 Continental troops and militia became prisoners of war, and General Benjamin Lincoln, the American commander, was subjected to the humiliation of marching out between the lines with colours cased. In August 1780, Cornwallis shattered a newly-formed American army under General Horatio Gates at Camden, killing or capturing over 2,000 men and driving the remnants into North Carolina.

Belsham records that these victories “excited the most sanguine expectations in England” that the southern colonies could be reduced to obedience. The ministry, intoxicated by success, anticipated a rapid end to the rebellion.

The Tide Turns in the Carolinas

But the British triumph at Camden proved to be the high-water mark of the southern campaign. American resistance, far from being crushed, re-emerged under new commanders. General Nathanael Greene, appointed to command the Southern Department after Camden, pursued a Fabian strategy of avoiding pitched battles while wearing down the British through attrition. At Cowpens (17 January 1781), Colonel Daniel Morgan’s force annihilated a British detachment under the dashing but impetuous Colonel Banastre Tarleton. Cornwallis, stung by the defeat, embarked on a relentless pursuit of Morgan and Greene across the rivers of North Carolina, a chase that Belsham describes as “bold and hazardous” and in which Greene had predicted that Cornwallis might “precipitate himself into some capital misfortune.”

The climax came at Guilford Court House (15 March 1781), where Cornwallis drove Greene from the field after a hard-fought engagement. But the victory was Pyrrhic: the British lost some 600 veteran troops, a quarter of their effective force, and Cornwallis found himself unable to sustain his army in the interior. “This was a victory,” Belsham observes, “very different from that of Camden, and dearly purchased on the part of the English.”

Compelled to march to Wilmington, North Carolina, to refit and resupply, Cornwallis now made the fateful decision that would lead to Yorktown: instead of following Greene back into South Carolina to protect the British posts there, he resolved to march north into Virginia, believing that the Chesapeake colonies were the decisive theatre.

Cornwallis’s Position: The Road to Yorktown

Lord Cornwallis entered Virginia in May 1781, joining forces with a British detachment under General William Phillips at Petersburg. The combined army, now some 7,000 strong, was formidable on paper, but the strategic situation was already deteriorating. The Marquis de Lafayette, commanding the American forces in Virginia, skillfully avoided battle while shadowing the British movements, earning Cornwallis’s contemptuous remark — “The boy cannot escape me!” — which Lafayette would later recall with satisfaction.

Throughout the summer of 1781, Cornwallis marched his army across Virginia — from Petersburg to Richmond, from Richmond to Williamsburg — in a series of operations that achieved little of lasting strategic value. He dispatched Tarleton on a daring raid that nearly captured Governor Thomas Jefferson and the Virginia Assembly at Charlottesville, but these were pinpricks against an enemy that refused to be drawn into a decisive battle.

In June, Cornwallis received dispatches from Sir Henry Clinton, the British commander-in-chief in New York, expressing alarm that New York itself might be threatened by the combined Franco-American army under George Washington and the Comte de Rochambeau. Clinton ordered the return of the troops he had detached to Virginia. Cornwallis, protesting but ultimately compliant, withdrew his army to the coast to facilitate embarkation.

After briefly considering Portsmouth, Cornwallis selected Yorktown, a small port on the York River near the Chesapeake Bay, as his base. The position, as Belsham notes, was unfavourable: Yorktown sat “nearly at the extremity of a narrow peninsula, enclosed between York River to the north and James River to the south,” and was easily invested by an enemy commanding the navigation of both rivers. Cornwallis himself had “so unfavourable an opinion of the port he occupied, that nothing but assurances of support would have induced him to attempt its defence.” But he trusted in the promise of the navy and the army in New York to relieve him.

The French-American Plan

While Cornwallis dug in at Yorktown, a grand strategic convergence was taking shape. The key to the plan was the French navy.

Washington and Rochambeau had spent the spring and early summer of 1781 in conference at Wethersfield, Connecticut, debating their next move. Washington’s instinct was to attack New York, the principal British base in North America. Rochambeau was sceptical. The question hung in the balance until a letter arrived from the Comte de Grasse, commanding a powerful French fleet of twenty-four ships of the line in the West Indies. De Grasse announced that his destination was unalterably fixed to the Chesapeake — he could operate there only until mid-October, when the hurricane season would compel his return to the Caribbean.

“We have no alternative,” Washington and Rochambeau replied. They would march south.

The plan required extraordinary secrecy and coordination. Washington maintained the appearance of preparing to besiege New York as late as 24 August, keeping Sir Henry Clinton guessing. Then, on that date, the allied army suddenly decamped, crossed the North River, and began a swift march through New Jersey and Pennsylvania. They passed through Philadelphia on 30 August, where the Continental Congress received the electrifying but still uncertain news. The fleet of the Comte de Grasse entered the Chesapeake Bay nearly simultaneously.

The Battle of the Capes (5 September 1781)

The strategic hinge of the campaign was a naval battle that would decide whether Cornwallis could be relieved. On 5 September 1781, a British fleet of nineteen ships of the line under Admiral Thomas Graves appeared off the Virginia Capes. De Grasse, expecting a reinforcement squadron from Rhode Island under the Comte de Barras, put to sea to meet them. A warm engagement followed, in which, as Belsham records, “the English appear not to have obtained the advantage.”

The outcome was decisive. De Grasse, joined by Barras’s squadron a few days later, was left “undisputed master of the Chesapeake.” The British fleet, having failed to break the French blockade, withdrew to New York. From this moment, relief of Cornwallis was “wholly impracticable.”

The Siege: 28 September — 19 October 1781

The allied army — some 9,000 Americans and 8,000 French — arrived before Yorktown in late September. Washington, Rochambeau, and the Comte de Grasse had trapped Cornwallis in a vice: the French fleet sealed the bay, while the Franco-American army closed the landward approaches.

The siege works were opened with classical eighteenth-century precision. The allies constructed a first parallel — a line of entrenchments — about two thousand yards from the British works, and began a heavy bombardment. Cornwallis, seeing his outer works untenable, pulled his troops back into the inner fortifications.

By 14 October, despite “the well directed and incessant fire of the enemy,” the besiegers had advanced far in their second parallel. Two British redoubts at a distance of two hundred yards from the British lines were causing particular trouble, enfilading the allied approaches. It was decided to storm them simultaneously — one by the French, the other by the Americans.

The assault was carried out “sword in hand with resistless impetuosity.” The Americans, Belsham notes, advanced with the cry “Remember New London!” — a reference to the destruction of that Connecticut port by the turncoat Benedict Arnold a month earlier. But when the British in the redoubts begged for quarter, the Americans granted it: “they could not tell how to put men to death while begging on their knees for quarter.”

By this time, the allied batteries mounted one hundred pieces of heavy ordnance. The British works, “enfiladed in almost every part, and nearly demolished, could scarcely mount a single gun.”

The Surrender

In desperation, Cornwallis attempted to transport his garrison across the York River to Gloucester Point on the opposite shore, where secondary British works were still held. A violent storm struck after the embarkation had actually commenced, “totally frustrating” the design.

Lord Cornwallis was left with no choice. On 17 October — the fourth anniversary of Burgoyne’s surrender at Saratoga — he dispatched a flag of truce. Negotiations followed, and on 19 October the articles of capitulation were signed.

The terms were hard. The honor of marching out with colours flying, which had been refused to General Lincoln at Charleston, was now refused to Cornwallis. General Benjamin Lincoln — the very officer who had surrendered at Charleston eighteen months before — was appointed to receive the submission of the army of Yorktown, “precisely in the same way his own had been conducted.” On 19 October, the British and Hessian troops, some 7,000 strong, marched out between the allied lines to the tune of a popular march called “The World Turned Upside Down.” Belsham records Cornwallis himself as pleading indisposition and sending his sword by a subordinate.

“The joy of the Americans on the capture of a second royal army was unbounded.” The Continental Congress ordered a marble column erected at Yorktown and appointed a solemn thanksgiving throughout all the states.

News Reaches London

The news of the catastrophe crossed the Atlantic in November 1781. The account that reached London was worse than anything the ministry had dared to fear: an entire British army — the second to have been lost in the war — had laid down its arms.

The famous exclamation attributed to Lord North — “Oh God! It is all over!” — captures the visceral shock with which the intelligence was received. North is said to have recoiled as if struck a physical blow. He had been the King’s minister for twelve years, the longest-serving prime minister of the reign, and had presided over the American war from its outbreak. The pillars of his political position — the confidence of the Crown and the support of Parliament — were both now crumbling.

Belsham records that the King’s Speech at the opening of the new session of Parliament on 27 November 1781 acknowledged “the events of it had been very unfortunate to his army in Virginia, having ended in the total loss of his forces in that province.” But George III, characteristically, insisted on the justice of his cause and called for “a vigorous, animated, and united exertion” to continue the war.

The Fall of the North Ministry: November 1781 — March 1782

The parliamentary session that opened on 27 November 1781 became the battleground on which the North ministry would be destroyed.

In the debates that followed, the Opposition — led by Charles James Fox, Edmund Burke, and the younger William Pitt — launched a sustained assault on the ministry’s conduct of the war. Burke, in a famous speech on army estimates, asked whether the American Secretary wished “to dispatch a third army to America, that General Washington might a third time receive them as prisoners of war.” The ministry survived the first divisions, but the majorities were shrinking: on a censorious motion concerning Lord Sandwich, the First Lord of the Admiralty, 217 members of the House of Commons voted him guilty of “shameful mismanagement of the naval affairs of Great Britain.”

The decisive blow fell in February and March 1782. On 22 February, General Henry Conway moved an address to the King imploring him to end offensive war in America. The ministry defeated it by a single vote — 193 to 192 — a “fearful majority of ONE voice only.” Fox immediately gave notice that the question would be revived. On 27 February, Conway moved again, and this time a ministerial attempt to substitute a bill for a truce was defeated 234 to 215. The original motion was then carried without a division, and on 1 March the House of Commons presented an address to George III, who responded that he would “take such measures as should appear to him most conducive to the restoration of harmony.”

This was not deemed sufficient. On 4 March, Conway moved a second address and a resolution declaring that the House would “consider as enemies to his Majesty and the country all those who should advise a prosecution of offensive war on the continent of North America.”

On 8 March, Lord John Cavendish moved a series of resolutions whose import was “that the chief cause of all the national misfortunes is want of foresight and ability in his Majesty’s Ministers.” Lord North’s government survived by only ten votes — 226 to 216. Sir John Rous, once a zealous ministerialist, moved that “the House could place no further confidence in the Ministers.” The motion was defeated 236 to 227, but with such a majority “victory was defeat.”

On 19 March 1782, the crisis reached its end. The Earl of Surrey was about to move a resolution of similar import when Lord North rose and addressed the Speaker. He declared, Belsham records, “that His Majesty had come to a determination to make an entire change of administration.” North thanked the House for its indulgence and declared himself ready to answer to his country for his conduct. The twelve-year ministry of Lord North — “this famous, or, to use a term more characteristically appropriate, this infamous Administration, so long the bane and curse of the British empire” — was “suddenly and totally dissolved, to the inexpressible joy of all ranks and orders of people.”

The Rockingham ministry was formed in its place, with the Marquis of Rockingham as First Lord of the Treasury and Charles James Fox and Edmund Burke in office. Peace negotiations with the American commissioners opened in Paris, culminating in the provisional articles of peace signed on 30 November 1782 and the definitive Treaty of Paris 1783, by which the thirteen colonies were recognised as free and independent states.

Key Figures

Lord Cornwallis (Charles, Earl Cornwallis): The British commander whose southern campaign, brilliant in its early stages, ended in disaster at Yorktown. Belsham portrays him as “generous, disinterested, and humane” as an individual, but fatally compromised by his adherence to a ruthless policy of war-making and by his subordination to orders from a distant commander-in-chief in New York.

George Washington: The American commander-in-chief, whose patient strategy of avoiding the destruction of his army while waiting for French assistance was vindicated at Yorktown. His decision to march south from New York was the decisive strategic move of the campaign.

Comte de Rochambeau: The French general who commanded the expeditionary force and coordinated with Washington. His loyalty to the plan and willingness to subordinate French strategic interests to the American theatre were essential.

Comte de Grasse: The French admiral whose fleet blockaded the Chesapeake and defeated the British naval relief attempt. His decision to sail north from the West Indies and his insistence on the Chesapeake as his destination determined the shape of the campaign.

Lord North: The British prime minister who had directed the war against America since 1775 and who fell from power in the aftermath of Yorktown. His reputation has been contested: Belsham treats him with a mixture of contempt for his policies and grudging respect for his parliamentary skill.

George III: The King, whose unyielding support for the American war and whose influence over the ministry through the “interior cabinet” (as the Duke of Richmond called it) was held by the Opposition to be the root cause of Britain’s misfortunes.

Aftermath and Consequences

The Siege of Yorktown effectively ended major combat operations in the American Revolutionary War. Although peace would not be formally concluded until the Treaty of Paris 1783 — and although fighting continued at sea and in other theatres — no further large-scale military operations took place in North America.

The principal consequences were:

  1. American Independence: The military victory at Yorktown made American sovereignty a fact that even the most intransigent British ministers could no longer deny. The provisional articles recognising American independence were signed on 30 November 1782.

  2. The Fall of the North Ministry: The political earthquake in London brought to power a ministry committed to peace, triggering a chain of events — the Rockingham ministry, the Shelburne ministry, and ultimately the Fox-North coalition of 1783 — that would reshape British politics for a generation.

  3. The Loyalist Tragedy: The terms of the peace treaty left the American Loyalists — those colonists who had remained faithful to the Crown — exposed to confiscation and persecution. The Congressional recommendation that their estates be restored proved, as Belsham observes, “nugatory and useless.” Thousands migrated to Nova Scotia, the Bahamas, and other British possessions.

  4. British Constitutional Development: The collapse of the North ministry and the subsequent political crisis stretched the boundaries of the eighteenth-century constitution. The King’s right to choose his ministers was constrained by the unmistakable verdict of the House of Commons; the principle that a ministry must command a parliamentary majority was established beyond dispute.

Contemporary Accounts

William Belsham, writing in the 1790s, presents the Yorktown campaign as the just conclusion of a war that was from the beginning unjust, impolitic, and ruinous. His narrative, while written from a distinctly Whiggish and critical perspective, draws on parliamentary debates, military dispatches, and published memoirs. He is scrupulous in recording the arguments of both sides — the ministry’s stubborn insistence on the justice of the cause, the Opposition’s denunciation of its folly — but his moral judgment is never in doubt: the war was a calamity brought on by the obstinacy of the King and the incompetence of his ministers, and Yorktown was its fitting epitaph.

Belsham’s account of the surrender itself is notably restrained, avoiding the triumphalism of American narratives while refusing to disguise the magnitude of the British defeat. He dwells on the pathos of the situation — the “eager hopes and sanguine expectations” that preceded the “final issue” — and on the irony that Cornwallis was compelled to surrender “precisely in the same way” as that in which he had compelled Lincoln at Charleston.
