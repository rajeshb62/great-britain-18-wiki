Battle of Quiberon Bay (20 November 1759)

The Battle of Quiberon Bay (20 November 1759) was the most complete naval victory of the 18th century — a triumph of aggressive seamanship in which Admiral Sir Edward Hawke pursued the French Atlantic fleet into a winter gale and destroyed it among the treacherous shoals of the Brittany coast. The battle extinguished the French threat of invasion, shattered the naval power of France for the remainder of the Seven Years’ War, and crowned what became known as the Annus Mirabilis — the “year of wonders” that also saw British victories at Quebec, Minden, and Lagos.

Background

The Invasion Threat

By 1759, the war had turned decisively against France. William Pitt the Elder, the British Secretary of State, had orchestrated a global strategy that struck at French colonial possessions, trade routes, and naval power. In 1758, Edward Boscawen and General Amherst had captured Louisbourg, opening the St. Lawrence; in India, Robert Clive had secured Bengal at Plassey; in Germany, the army of the Duke of Brunswick was holding the French at bay.

Desperate to retrieve her fortunes, France resolved upon the boldest stroke of the war — a direct invasion of Britain. The plan called for the assembly of a large fleet at Brest, which would escort an army of invasion across the Channel. The Duke d’Aiguillon assembled forty battalions on the coast of Brittany, ready to embark. The Duc de Belleisle, the French minister of war, drew up the detailed plans: the invasion force would land in Scotland or Ireland, strike at the heart of British power, and compel a favourable peace. The scheme was, in the words of Campbell’s Naval History, “the conception of rage, founded on ignorance, and incapable of accomplishment” — but it had to be taken seriously.

The French Fleet

The command of the Brest fleet was given to the Marquis de Conflans, a seasoned if uninspired admiral, who had under his command twenty-one ships of the line — the largest French naval force assembled in a decade. His flagship was the Soleil Royal of eighty guns, the finest ship in the French navy. With him sailed the Tonnant, Formidable, and Orient, each of eighty guns, supported by a powerful body of seventy-fours and sixty-fours.

Hawke’s Blockade of Brest

To thwart this design, Pitt placed his faith in the Western Squadron — the fleet assigned to watch the French Atlantic ports — and gave its command to Sir Edward Hawke. Hawke’s orders were simple: blockade Brest so tightly that Conflans could not put to sea. The service was one of extraordinary hardship. The squadron was required to maintain its station through the autumn gales of the Bay of Biscay, keeping the sea in all weathers, repairing at sea, and resupplying by rotation.

Hawke’s mind possessed a remarkable constancy that made him equal to this grim duty. He was bold without restless irritability; the firmness of his character enabled him to endure the cheerless monotony of the blockade with as much serenity as he enjoyed the heat of battle. His fleet, numbering approximately twenty-three ships of the line, included the Royal George of 100 guns (his flagship), the Union, Namur, and Duke of ninety guns, and a powerful squadron commanded by officers of the highest quality — among them Commodore Lord Howe in the Magnanime, Captain John Campbell in the Dorsetshire, and Captain *Sir John Bentley in the Warspite.

The Pursuit

The Storm

Throughout the autumn of 1759, Hawke maintained his station off Brest with unrelenting vigilance. But on the 12th of November, a violent gale — gathering into an irresistible tempest — drove the British fleet from its station. Hawke was compelled to take shelter in Torbay on the Devon coast.

Conflans Escapes

Conflans, seeing the coast clear, immediately put to sea from Brest on the 14th of November with his entire fleet. His intention was to rendezvous with transports and invasion forces in Quiberon Bay, then proceed north-about around Ireland to cover the landing of d’Aiguillon’s army in Scotland.

But on the very same day that Conflans sailed from Brest, Hawke sailed from Torbay. The two greatest fleets yet employed in the war were now at sea, converging on the same waters.

The Chase

On the 16th of November, Hawke was off Ushant when several English transports returning from Quiberon Bay passed through his fleet and reported that they had seen the French squadron on the preceding day, standing to the south-east, about twenty-three leagues from Belle-Île. The intelligence was received by the whole British fleet with acclamations, and every ship spread every sail to catch the gale.

Hawke directed his course for Quiberon Bay on the coast of Brittany — his conjecture that this would be Conflans’ rendezvous proved correct. Fortune, however, at first opposed him: a strong gale from the eastward drove the British fleet far to the west. But the weather moderated, and Hawke pressed on through the night of the 19th.

The Battle (20 November 1759)

The Discovery

At half an hour after eight o’clock on the morning of 20 November 1759, the Maidstone frigate — which, with the Coventry, had orders to keep ahead of the squadron — let fly her top-gallant sails, the signal for discovering a fleet. About nine o’clock, Lord Howe in the Magnanime made signal that the ships sighted were enemies.

The French were bearing to the northward between the island of Belle-Île and the mainland of France. Conflans, perceiving the British approach, at first seemed to offer battle. His courage, however, soon cooled — he changed his plan and stood right before the wind toward the shore, hoping to decoy the English among the rocks where his pilots’ local knowledge would give him the advantage.

”Make Downright Work with Them”

Hawke immediately told his officers that he did not intend to trouble himself with forming lines — he would attack the French in the old way and “make downright work with them.” He threw out a signal for seven of his ships nearest the enemy to chase, in order to detain the French while the rest of the squadron formed into a line of battle a-head as they chased, so that no time might be lost in the pursuit.

At this moment the scene was one of sublime terror. A dreadful storm darkened the face of the heavens. The sea rolled in tremendous waves which dashed themselves into foam on treacherous rocks and shallows unknown to the British pilots. Into this maelstrom Hawke drove his fleet.

As the British ships neared the French, Conflans’ nerve failed him. What had begun as a ploy — drawing the English onto the shoals — became a desperate flight. The French admiral had intended to play a cunning game, but he did not play it with the address that his situation required. Fluctuating between a resolution to fight and an inclination to fly, he allowed the British to come up with him, then crowded sail when it was too late to escape.

The Action

It was two o’clock in the afternoon before the British headmost ships could get up with the French rear. The Warspite and Dorsetshire began the engagement, pouring broadsides into the rearmost French ships as the British van sailed past toward the enemy’s centre.

At half an hour after two, the British van opened a general fire on the French rear. The Formidable, commanded by the French Rear-Admiral du Verger, a man of great courage and noble determination, behaved in the most heroic manner. Broadside after broadside was poured into her by the British as they sailed successively past toward the van of the enemy; and she returned their fire with a promptitude that excited the admiration of friends and foes alike. The Formidable did not strike her colours until after four o’clock, and only when every officer on board had been killed.

”Lay Me Alongside the Soleil Royal”

Meanwhile, the Royal George, with Hawke aboard, was approaching the Soleil Royal, which bore the flag of Conflans. Intent only on her prey, she passed on without heeding the shot of other ships. The sea was dashing over her bows, and as she came rapidly nearer, she appeared as if actuated by the furiousness of rage.

Her pilot, seeing the breakers foaming on every side, told the admiral that he could not go farther without the most imminent danger from the shoals. Hawke replied with the most famous words of his career:

“You have done your duty in pointing out the danger — but lay me alongside of the Soleil Royal.”

The pilot bowed in obedience and gave the necessary orders.

The Superbe, a French ship of seventy guns, perceiving what was intended by the movements of the English admiral, generously interposed between her commander and the Royal George, receiving the fatal broadside intended for Conflans. The thunder of the explosion was succeeded by a wild shriek from all on board; the British sailors gave a shout of triumph, which was instantly checked, for the smoke clearing away revealed only the masts of the Superbe — with her colours still flying — above the water. In a moment they were covered by a roll of the sea and seen no more. The Thésée, another seventy-four, suffered the same fate — foundering when a high sea entered her lower-deck ports as she returned the British fire.

The Soleil Royal was spared from immediate destruction only by this sacrifice; she escaped toward the shore, where she was later run aground and burned by her own crew to prevent capture.

The Breakdown of the French Fleet

About four in the afternoon, the Formidable — which had maintained the whole battle with heroic determination — struck her colours, but not before every officer had been killed. The Héros, a seventy-four, also struck. The Juste ran aground and was wrecked. Darkness falling, the remnants of the French fleet scattered in confusion.

Seven ships of the line — the Orient, Glorieux, Robuste, Magnifique, Inflexible, Dragon, and Solitaire — hove their guns overboard and, in a desperate manoeuvre, ran into the shallow estuary of the river Vilaine (Villaix). Here they were effectively trapped for months, unable to escape without the help of a spring tide. About as many more, in a shattered condition, escaped to other ports — chiefly Rochefort. Of the twenty-one ships of the line with which Conflans had sailed from Brest, only a handful ever put to sea again as effective fighting units.

Hawke Anchors in the Storm

The wind blowing strong inshore, Hawke made the signal for anchoring to the westward of the small island of Dumet. Here the fleet remained during the night, riding out the tempest in a very dangerous berth. As the storm continued to increase, the darkness was occasionally broken by the flashes of cannon and the howl of the wind; the roar of the breakers was augmented by the sound of guns of distress from ships driven onto the shoals.

When morning appeared, the full extent of the destruction became visible. The Soleil Royal had been run ashore and was burning. The British had lost two ships — the Essex and the Resolution — which had run onto a sandbank called Le Four and were irrecoverably lost, though most of their crews and some stores were saved.

Casualties

The British loss was astoundingly light for a battle of such magnitude: no more than one lieutenant and thirty-nine seamen and marines killed, and two hundred and two wounded. Two ships of the line — the Essex and Resolution — were lost on the Le Four shoal, but most of their crews were saved.

The French loss was catastrophic. Four of their finest ships of the line were destroyed: the Superbe, Thésée, and Juste sunk or wrecked; the Formidable captured; the Héros taken and later destroyed; the Soleil Royal burned. Seven ships were trapped in the Vilaine. The remainder were scattered and shattered. The loss of life was prodigious — all the officers of the Formidable had been killed before she struck, and the total French dead was estimated in the thousands.

Aftermath

The Destruction of French Naval Power

The battle of Quiberon Bay destroyed the French Atlantic fleet as a fighting force for the remainder of the war. Of twenty-one ships of the line, only a shattered handful remained operational. The seven ships that had taken refuge in the Vilaine were blockaded there by a British squadron under Commodore John Boys and did not escape for many months — and then only in a much-reduced condition. The invasion of Britain was no longer possible.

Thurot’s Diversion

A secondary French expedition under François Thurot — five frigates with 1,270 soldiers — had sailed from Dunkirk during the same storm that drove Hawke from Brest. Thurot evaded the British blockade, reached the coast of Ireland, and landed at Carrickfergus on 21 February 1760. But upon learning of the defeat at Quiberon Bay, and with British forces converging on him, he re-embarked and sailed for France. On 28 February, off the Isle of Man, he was overtaken by three English frigates under Captain John Elliot and defeated. Thurot was killed in the action, his ships captured. This was the last gasp of the great invasion armament.

Hawke’s Triumph

Hawke returned to England in January 1760. He was received by George II in that free and hearty manner characteristic of the old king, who advanced to meet him as he entered the room and thanked him aloud for his service. The king settled £2,000 per annum on Hawke for life and the lives of his two sons. The House of Commons voted its unanimous thanks, and the Speaker addressed Hawke in terms of the highest praise — “Your expedition was for the nearest and most affecting concern to us, the immediate defence of his majesty’s kingdoms.”

The celebrated Voltaire himself, no friend to the British, acknowledged the significance of the victory, observing that there were natural circumstances which gave an inherent superiority to the English marine in all ages over that of France.

The Annus Mirabilis

Quiberon Bay was the crowning victory of 1759 — a year of such extraordinary British success that it was celebrated as the Annus Mirabilis, or “year of wonders.” The catalogue of triumphs was unmatched in British history:

  • Capture of Quebec (13 September 1759): General James Wolfe defeated the Marquis de Montcalm on the Plains of Abraham, capturing the capital of French Canada. Both commanders fell.
  • Battle of Minden (1 August 1759): The Anglo-German army under Prince Ferdinand of Brunswick defeated a superior French army, saving Hanover and humiliating the French marshals Contades and Broglie.
  • Battle of Lagos (18–19 August 1759): Admiral Edward Boscawen destroyed a French Mediterranean squadron under de la Clue off the coast of Portugal, preventing the Toulon fleet from joining Conflans at Brest.
  • Battle of Quiberon Bay (20 November 1759): Hawke’s pursuit and destruction of Conflans’ fleet ended the invasion threat and broke French naval power.

William Pitt the Elder, the architect of the global strategy that produced these victories, was at the height of his power. Britain, which had entered the war with disaster at Minorca in 1756 and the execution of Admiral John Byng, had been transformed into the world’s foremost naval and colonial power.

Key Figures

  • Edward Hawke, 1st Baron Hawke (1705–1781) — The victor of Quiberon Bay. His relentless blockade of Brest and his daring pursuit into a winter gale represent the highest standard of British naval command. His reply to his pilot — “You have done your duty in pointing out the danger, but lay me alongside the Soleil Royal” — became legendary.
  • Admiral the Marquis de Conflans (c. 1690–1777) — The French commander. His initial courage in offering battle gave way to irresolution, and his attempt to decoy the British onto the shoals failed through indecision and loss of nerve. He escaped the battle but his reputation did not survive it.
  • Rear-Admiral du Verger — Commander of the French Formidable, who fought with extraordinary resolution. He and every officer on his ship were killed before she struck her colours.
  • William Pitt the Elder (1708–1778) — British Secretary of State, the architect of the strategy that contained France globally and gave Hawke the command and resources to blockade Brest.
  • George II (1683–1760) — The reigning monarch, who personally thanked Hawke and settled a pension on him. George II died on 25 October 1760, less than a year after the victory.
  • Richard Howe, 1st Earl Howe (1726–1799) — Then Commodore, commanding the Magnanime and serving under Hawke. Howe’s signal confirmed the French fleet’s identity. He would later win the Glorious First of June.
  • Duke d’Aiguillon — The French army commander on the Brittany coast, who had prepared forty battalions for the invasion. He corresponded with Hawke after the battle over the fate of the Héros prisoners, and received one of the most sardonic ripostes in naval history.
  • Sir Charles Saunders — The admiral who had commanded the fleet at Quebec. Learning of Hawke’s pursuit, he immediately put to sea from his Quebec expedition to share the danger and honour, arriving too late — but his resolution was alone equal to a victory.

Hawke’s Letter to d’Aiguillon

After the battle, the Héros prize slipped her cables in the night and ran ashore. Hawke demanded her officers and crew as prisoners. The Duke d’Aiguillon refused and wrote to Hawke complaining of the “irregularity” of his proceedings. Hawke’s reply, preserved in Campbell’s memoirs, is a masterpiece of sardonic humour:

“I cannot persuade myself your grace was serious, when you termed my enterprise irregular: it was merriment, and I shall not hereafter be surprised, if in the same gaieté de cœur I should be accused of acting irregularly in attacking M. de Conflans (after a chase of twenty leagues in the open sea) within your islands, and on your coast, and setting fire to the Soleil Royal, &c.”

And he concluded with a defiant reminder of the stakes:

“I came out near eight months ago, only furnished with orders to decide the fate of the two nations with Monsieur Conflans in the open sea; but when we met, as he did not choose to stay for me, he has thereby changed the nature of my military operations.”

Strategic Significance

Quiberon Bay was more than a battle — it was the decisive naval engagement of the Seven Years’ War. Before 1759, the French fleet had been a serious threat to British security; after Quiberon Bay, it ceased to exist as a force capable of challenging British naval supremacy. The blockade of Brest — a strategic innovation that Hawke perfected — became the template for British naval warfare for the next century. The doctrine that the Royal Navy’s proper place was off the enemy’s coast, not protecting its own, was established at Quiberon Bay.

The victory ensured that British expeditions could range the globe — to Quebec, to the West Indies, to India, to the coasts of Africa — without fear of interruption. The foundations of the British Empire, which would reach its zenith in the following century, were laid in the storm-tossed waters of Quiberon Bay.
