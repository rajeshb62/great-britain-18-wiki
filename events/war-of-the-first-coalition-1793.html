War of the First Coalition (1793–1797)

The War of the First Coalition (1793–1797) was the opening phase of the French Revolutionary Wars, in which the monarchies of Europe — led by Pitt’s Britain, Austria, and Prussia — combined to contain and destroy the revolutionary government of France. It was the longest and most expensive war Britain had ever fought, and it ended in the complete military triumph of the French Republic. By 1797, the coalition had collapsed piecemeal, leaving only Britain and Austria still in arms. The war transformed British politics, demolished the fiscal system that Pitt had so carefully constructed, and set the stage for the global struggle against Napoleonic France that would dominate the next two decades.

The Road to War

The French Revolution and the European Reaction

The French Revolution of 1789 had initially been welcomed by much of British public opinion. Charles James Fox and the Whig opposition hailed the fall of the Bastille as the dawn of a new era of liberty; even Pitt, cautious and fiscal-minded, declared in 1792 that the situation in France offered an opportunity for “uninterrupted peace” and predicted “fifteen years of tranquillity.” But the revolution radicalised rapidly. On 21 September 1792, the French National Convention declared France a republic. In November, it issued the Edict of Fraternity (the Decree of 19 November 1792), which promised “fraternity and assistance to all peoples wishing to recover their liberty” — a direct threat to every throne in Europe. The Convention invited the subject nations of the continent to revolt, declared that France would treat as enemies any powers that remained neutral, and ordered its generals to carry the revolution beyond France’s borders.

On 21 January 1793, the Convention executed Louis XVI by the guillotine. The event sent a shock through every court in Europe. In London, the king’s ministers recalled their ambassador; the French ambassador, M. Chauvelin, was ordered to depart the kingdom. But the point of no return had already been reached.

The Scheldt Crisis and the Invasion of the Austrian Netherlands

The immediate casus belli was the French invasion of the Austrian Netherlands (modern-day Belgium). In November 1792, French armies under General Charles Dumouriez crushed the Austrian forces at the Battle of Jemappes and overran the entire province. The French Convention, in a flagrant violation of the Treaty of Westphalia (1648), decreed the opening of the Scheldt River to international navigation. The Scheldt had been closed by treaty for over a century; its opening struck directly at the commercial interests of the Dutch Republic and Great Britain, for whom the closure of the Scheldt was a fundamental guarantee of Dutch independence and British strategic security.

Britain had guaranteed the independence of the Dutch Republic by treaty. The French occupation of the Netherlands — and the French refusal to give any security that the province would be evacuated at the conclusion of war — made the question one of national honour and vital interest. Pitt, who had worked so hard in the 1780s to restore British finances and avoid continental entanglements, found himself forced into a war he did not want but could not avoid.

The Parliamentary Debate (February 1793)

On the day that France declared war — 1 February 1793 — the British Parliament was already debating the crisis. Pitt rose to deliver what Belsham describes as “an eloquent harangue, evidently calculated to prepare the minds of his auditors for war.” He denounced the execution of the French king as a crime that “the present age must be for ever contaminated with the guilt and ignominy of having witnessed.” He catalogued French aggression: the annexation of Savoy, the Edict of Fraternity, the invasion of the Netherlands, the opening of the Scheldt. The French, he argued, had demonstrated that “the wild and destructive ambition of the present rulers in France was limited only by their power.” He moved an address of thanks to the king, preparing the nation for war.

Charles James Fox rose in opposition, in what Belsham records as the most powerful speech of the debate. Fox “allowed that the trial and execution of the French monarch were acts of the grossest inhumanity and injustice,” but argued that “crimes perpetrated in one independent state were not cognisable in another.” He identified three grounds on which the ministry based its case for war — the opening of the Scheldt, the Edict of Fraternity, and the danger to the European balance of power — and demolished each in turn. The Dutch had made no request for British assistance. The Edict of Fraternity had been accompanied by an explanation that took away its sting. As for the balance of power: “We had seen the entire conquest of Poland, and the invasion of France, with such marked indifference, that it would be difficult now to take it up with the grace of sincerity.”

Edmund Burke, who had broken with Fox over the French Revolution and whose Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) had made him the intellectual champion of European counter-revolution, spoke in terms of apocalyptic urgency. He “pronounced a vehement Philippic, affording a melancholy contrast to the speeches of his better days.” He denounced the opposition as men who “could neither vindicate the principles nor deny the power of France, yet impeded the measures taken to secure us against that power.” For Burke, the war was not a diplomatic dispute but a crusade: “No man had a more lively sense of the evils of war than himself. A war with France, in present circumstances, must be terrible; but peace much more so.”

France Declares War (1 February 1793)

Before the British Parliament could vote, France acted. On 1 February 1793, the French National Convention declared war on Great Britain and the Dutch Republic. The British declaration of war followed immediately. The long struggle had begun.

The First Coalition

The coalition that assembled against France in the spring of 1793 was the most formidable alliance Europe had seen since the Seven Years’ War. Its members were:

  • Great Britain — the paymaster, providing financial subsidies to every continental ally, and the naval arm, commanding the seas
  • Austria — the principal military power on the continent, with armies in the Netherlands and on the Rhine
  • Prussia — a reluctant and unreliable ally, whose king was more interested in the partition of Poland than in the war in the west
  • Spain — a Bourbon monarchy that feared the contagion of revolution
  • Holland (the Dutch Republic) — the Stadtholder’s government, bound to Britain by treaty
  • Sardinia-Piedmont — the Italian kingdom that faced French expansion across the Alps
  • Portugal — a minor but consistent ally
  • The Kingdom of Naples and various Italian states

The coalition was united by nothing but fear. Austria and Prussia were traditional rivals. Britain and Spain were still smarting from the wounds of the American war. The alliance had no common strategic objective beyond the defeat of France, and no agreed plan for the settlement of Europe after a victory. It was, as one historian put it, a coalition of monarchies against a revolution, not a coalition of states with a shared interest.

The Military Struggle (1793–1794)

The Flanders Campaign

The main theatre of operations in 1793 was the Austrian Netherlands and northern France, where a combined British, Austrian, and allied army under the Prince of Coburg and the Duke of York — the second son of George III — aimed to invade France. The Duke of York was appointed commander of the British and auxiliary troops on the continent.

The campaign opened with remarkable success. In the spring and summer of 1793, the allies captured the French border fortresses of Valenciennes, Condé, Mentz (Mainz), and Le Quesnoy in rapid succession. The French armies, demoralised by the defection of General Charles Dumouriez to the Austrians after the Battle of Neerwinden (18 March 1793), seemed on the verge of collapse. The court of London, Belsham writes, was filled with “elation” — it was widely believed that the war would be over in a single campaign.

But the coalition’s triumphs were squandered by disunity and strategic disagreement. The Austrians, under Coburg, were more interested in recovering the Netherlands for themselves than in invading France. The British, under the Duke of York, favoured an advance on Paris. The result was a “fatal separation”: Coburg turned south to besiege the fortress of Maubeuge, while York, with 37,000 men, besieged the port of Dunkirk — a decision of catastrophic folly.

The siege of Dunkirk was a disaster. The French, galvanised by the revolutionary levée en masse that had raised hundreds of thousands of conscripts, concentrated their forces against York’s isolated army. On 8 September 1793, the French under General Houchard attacked at the Battle of Hondschoote and inflicted a severe defeat. The Duke of York was forced to raise the siege and retreat, losing his artillery and baggage. Belsham records the defeat in a single terse sentence: “Duke of York totally defeated at Dunkirk.”

The campaign of 1794 was worse. The French, now under the direction of the Committee of Public Safety and the revolutionary zeal of the Reign of Terror, had reorganised their armies and found brilliant young commanders. The Battle of Fleurus (26 June 1794) was the decisive engagement: the French under General Jourdan defeated the Austrian army under Coburg, and the Austrian Netherlands were lost for good. The Duke of York’s army, now reduced to a fraction of its original strength, retreated northwards through Holland, harried by the French and the winter. By the spring of 1795, the “shattered remains of the Duke of York’s army” — Belsham’s phrase — embarked for England. The Flanders campaign had cost Britain £6 million and the lives of tens of thousands of soldiers, and had achieved nothing.

Toulon (August–December 1793)

The one British success of 1793 came in the Mediterranean. In August, the French royalist and federalist inhabitants of Toulon, in revolt against the Jacobin Convention, surrendered their city and its naval arsenal to a British fleet under Admiral Lord Hood. The port was the most important naval base in the Mediterranean; its capture gave Britain control of the French Mediterranean fleet — thirty ships of the line — and a strategic foothold in southern France.

The occupation of Toulon was brief and glorious. A young artillery officer named Napoleon Bonaparte — then unknown outside the army — was given command of the siege artillery. He arrayed his guns on the heights commanding the harbour, and on 17 December 1793, after a ferocious bombardment, the British and Spanish garrison evacuated the town. The British fleet under Hood sailed out of the harbour under fire, carrying thousands of royalist refugees. Among the British officers present was a young captain named Horatio Nelson, serving with the naval brigade. It was the first meeting of the two men who would dominate the coming epoch.

Toulon was the pattern of the war: a brilliant opportunity thrown away by the incompetence of the coalition; a French recovery made possible by the revolutionary energy that the allied monarchies could not match.

The Naval War: The Glorious First of June (1794)

If the land war was a catalogue of disaster, the naval war brought one of the most celebrated British victories of the age. In the spring of 1794, a French grain convoy of over 130 ships was expected from America, vital to feed a France starving under the Terror and the revolutionary governance. The French Atlantic fleet put to sea under Admiral Villaret-Joyeuse to cover the convoy’s passage. The British Channel Fleet, under Admiral Lord Howe — “Black Dick” to the sailors — intercepted them on the morning of 1 June 1794, about 400 miles west of the French coast.

The Glorious First of June was a battle of extraordinary ferocity, fought in the classic style of the line of battle. Howe, in his flagship the Queen Charlotte, broke the French line — a daring tactical innovation — and engaged at close quarters. Six French ships of the line were captured; one was sunk. The British loss was 290 killed and 858 wounded; the French lost over 3,000 killed and wounded plus the captured crews.

Yet the strategic result was ambiguous. The French grain convoy, the real object of the campaign, had escaped — the British frigates had missed it in the fog, and it reached Brest safely. The French fleet, though battered, was not destroyed; within weeks it was ready for sea again. The Channel remained contested. But the victory gave Britain its first great naval triumph of the war, and Howe was rewarded with the thanks of Parliament and — after years of neglect by the Admiralty — overdue recognition. The battle established a moral ascendancy that the Royal Navy would never lose.

The Financial Strain

The war was an economic catastrophe for Britain. Pitt had spent the 1780s restoring the national finances after the disaster of the American war. His Sinking Fund — a device by which surplus revenue was set aside to redeem the national debt — was the centrepiece of his fiscal policy, and he had boasted that under his management the debt would be paid off within a generation.

The war destroyed that dream. In 1793 alone, Pitt borrowed £6 million for the war effort. In 1794, another £11 million. By 1795, the national debt had more than doubled from its pre-war level. The Sinking Fund was suspended in 1795 — formally abandoned, though Pitt continued to use the mechanism as a fiscal fiction. The “extravagant loan of the minister” became a regular theme of the opposition’s attacks.

Belsham records that commercial credit collapsed in the first months of the war: “An unparalleled number of bankruptcies in London” followed the declaration of war, and it required the government’s intervention — the issuance of exchequer bills to support commercial credit — to prevent a general financial crash. The Bank of England itself came under severe pressure, and in 1797 it would be forced to suspend cash payments — the famous Bank Restriction Act.

Pitt’s response to the financial crisis was to raise taxes to levels unprecedented in British history. He introduced the first income tax in British history in 1799, but even before that, in the period of the First Coalition, he had imposed heavy new duties on imports, raised the land tax, and borrowed at ruinous rates of interest. The cost of the war in the first four years alone was estimated at over £50 million — more than the entire cost of the Seven Years’ War.

The Collapse of the Coalition (1795–1797)

The First Coalition did not break in battle; it dissolved by mutual exhaustion and diplomatic betrayal.

Prussia, the first to falter, signed the separate Peace of Basel with France on 5 April 1795. The Prussian king, Frederick William II, had never been enthusiastic about the war; his eyes were fixed on Poland, where the third and final partition was in progress. France recognised Prussia’s Polish acquisitions; Prussia recognised French conquests on the left bank of the Rhine. The price of Prussian withdrawal was the abandonment of all the German princes whose territories lay west of the river.

Spain, the second to defect, signed its own Peace of Basel on 22 July 1795. The Spanish Bourbon monarchy, after two years of defeat and exhaustion, abandoned the coalition and — in 1796 — became an ally of France, bound by the Treaty of San Ildefonso. The defection of Spain was a strategic catastrophe for Britain: it opened the Mediterranean to the combined French and Spanish fleets, forced the British evacuation of Corsica, and made the sea route to India and the East Indies vulnerable to enemy attack.

Holland, or rather the Batavian Republic that the French had established after their conquest of the Netherlands in the winter of 1794–95, had been forced out of the coalition and into an alliance with France. The Dutch fleet, which had been Britain’s ally, was now a hostile force in the North Sea.

By the summer of 1796, the First Coalition had shrunk to two members: Great Britain and Austria. Britain had the navy and the money but no army capable of engaging the French on the continent. Austria had the army but was reeling under the blows of a young general named Napoleon Bonaparte, who had taken command of the French Army of Italy in March 1796 and was, in the space of a year, destroying the Austrian position in Italy in a campaign of unparalleled brilliance — Montenotte, Lodi, Castiglione, Arcole, Rivoli.

The Invasion Threat and Hoche’s Irish Expedition (1796–1797)

The collapse of the coalition brought the war directly to the shores of Britain and Ireland. With France triumphant on the continent, the French Directory resolved to strike at the heart of British power by an invasion of the British Isles.

The principal threat was directed at Ireland. Lazare Hoche, one of the ablest of the French revolutionary generals, assembled a fleet at Brest in December 1796: fifteen ships of the line, some forty transports, and 14,000 veteran troops. The plan was to land in Bantry Bay, in the south-west of Ireland, where the French hoped to unite with the United Irishmen under Lord Edward Fitzgerald and Wolfe Tone — the revolutionary society preparing an insurrection against British rule.

The expedition sailed on 15 December 1796. It was scattered by the worst winter storms in living memory. Hoche’s flagship became separated from the fleet; the ships that reached Bantry Bay — under Admiral Bouvet — found themselves unable to land troops in the mountainous seas. After a week of indecision, the fleet turned back for France. Not a single French soldier set foot on Irish soil.

The failed expedition was a narrow escape, but it was not an end to the danger. The French would try again in 1798 — and this time, in the summer of that year, they would land a small force at Killala Bay, too late to save the Irish Rebellion from defeat. The threat of invasion hung over Britain until the Peace of Amiens in 1802, and — after a brief interval — returned with the great invasion camps of 1803–1805.

The End of the First Coalition and the Peace Offensive (1797)

By 1797, Britain stood alone. Austria, after Bonaparte’s relentless Italian campaign and the preliminary Peace of Leoben (April 1797), sued for peace, confirmed by the Treaty of Campo Formio (October 1797). The First Coalition was dead.

Britain’s position in 1797 was the most perilous it had faced since the American war. The navy was shattered by the mutinies at Spithead and the Nore — the greatest breakdown of discipline in the history of the Royal Navy. The Bank of England had suspended cash payments. The national debt was staggering. The Opposition, led by Fox and Grey, was demanding peace. Pitt himself, in February 1797, authorised secret peace feelers through Lord Malmesbury — the first of several abortive attempts to negotiate with the Directory. The negotiations at Lille broke down over irreconcilable differences: the Directory demanded the return of the French colonies that Britain had captured, and Britain demanded the evacuation of Belgium and the Rhineland.

The war would go on. The Second Coalition — Britain, Russia, Austria, and Naples — was already being assembled. But the character of the war had changed. It was no longer a war against revolutionary principles; it was a war of national survival against a military power that threatened the very existence of the British state.

Significance

The War of the First Coalition was the formative experience of Pitt’s second ministry and the crucible in which the fiscal-military state of the Napoleonic Wars was forged. It taught the British government four hard lessons that would shape strategy for the next twenty years:

First, Britain could not fight a continental war with a continental army. The Duke of York’s Flanders campaign demonstrated that British troops, however brave, were no match for the mass armies of revolutionary France when commanded by allied incompetence and coalition politics. Britain’s strength was naval and financial.

Second, the coalition system was fragile. The allies had no common strategic objective, no command structure, and no shared commitment to the war. Prussia and Spain peeled off the moment it suited them.

Third, revolutionary France could mobilise resources that the old regime could not match. The levée en masse, the Committee of Public Safety, the brutal efficiency of the Terror — the French Republic was capable of concentrating national power in a way that the monarchies of the old regime could not replicate.

Fourth, the war would be decided at sea and in the empire, not in Flanders. The Glorious First of June, the capture of the Cape of Good Hope (1795), the capture of Ceylon from the Dutch, the conquest of Trinidad, the successful defence of the West Indian colonies — these were the achievements that sustained Britain’s will to fight. And in the Mediterranean, a new generation of naval commanders — Horatio Nelson, Sir John Jervis, Adam Duncan — was learning the craft that would culminate in the Nile (1798), Copenhagen (1801), and Trafalgar (1805).

Key Figures

  • William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) — Prime Minister. Architect of the war strategy, manager of the coalition’s finances, and the man who reluctantly led Britain into the longest war of his generation. His fiscal system was shattered by the war he was forced to fight.

  • Charles James Fox (1749–1806) — Leader of the Whig opposition. The most eloquent critic of the war, who argued from the first that it was unnecessary, provoked by British policy, and doomed to fail. Political isolation after his break with Burke; his consistent advocacy of peace was vindicated only by later history.

  • Edmund Burke (1729–1797) — The apostate Whig who broke with Fox over the French Revolution. His Reflections and his parliamentary speeches provided the intellectual justification for the war as a crusade against the principles of revolution. His death in 1797 deprived the war party of its most powerful voice.

  • Prince Frederick, Duke of York (1763–1827) — Second son of George III, appointed commander of the British expeditionary force in Flanders. His campaign was a catastrophic failure. The nursery rhyme “The Grand Old Duke of York” is thought to be a satirical memorial of his futile marches.

  • Admiral Lord Howe (1726–1799) — “Black Dick,” victor of the Glorious First of June. The most respected British naval commander of his generation.

  • Henry Dundas (1742–1811) — Pitt’s secretary of state for war (from 1794). The manager of the war effort, the organiser of the colonial expeditions, and the patron of Scottish patronage in the British government.

  • Horatio Nelson (1758–1805) — A young captain at the siege of Toulon, the blockade of Genoa, and the Battle of Cape St. Vincent (1797). The First Coalition was the apprenticeship of the man who would become the greatest naval commander in British history.

  • Lazare Hoche (1768–1797) — The French general who led the attempted invasion of Ireland (December 1796). His death in 1797 deprived France of one of its most capable commanders.

  • Wolfe Tone (1763–1798) — The Irish revolutionary who accompanied Hoche’s expedition as a French officer. The leader of the United Irishmen, whose alliance with France gave the British government its greatest fear: insurrection in Ireland combined with invasion from France.
