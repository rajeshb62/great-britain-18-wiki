Battle of Culloden (1746)

The Battle of Culloden (16 April 1746) was the final, decisive engagement of the Forty-Five Jacobite Rising, fought on Culloden Moor (also known as Drummossie Moor) near Inverness. It was the last pitched battle fought on British soil and the catastrophic end of the last serious attempt to restore the Stuart dynasty to the British throne. The battle lasted less than an hour and resulted in the total destruction of the Jacobite army, the flight of Charles Edward Stuart, and a campaign of brutal pacification that earned the victor, William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland, the enduring epithet “the Butcher.”

Background

The Retreat from Derby

The Jacobite army under Charles Edward Stuart had reached its high-water mark on 4 December 1745, when it occupied Derby, only 120 miles from London. The capital was in panic; the government of the Duke of Newcastle had lost control of events; and the French invasion force at Dunkirk awaited only the signal to cross. Yet at the council of war held on the night of 5 December, Lord George Murray and the other senior Jacobite commanders prevailed upon the Prince to order a retreat. Two government armies — one under Cumberland advancing from the Midlands, another under General Wade threatening from the north — were converging to trap the Jacobites. No English recruits had risen, and no French landing had materialised (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4556–4569).

The retreat from Derby was a remarkable feat of military discipline, conducted through the worst of the winter weather, but it was the turning point of the rising. From that moment, the Jacobite cause passed irreversibly from the brink of triumph into a slow, grinding collapse. The army reached Scotland and won a further victory at the Battle of Falkirk Muir (17 January 1746), but the victory was hollow: the Highland clans were quarrelling among themselves, discipline was disintegrating, and the army was shrinking through desertion and hunger.

Cumberland Takes Command

The Duke of Cumberland, having been recalled from the continent where he had commanded the British army at the Battle of Fontenoy (1745), arrived in Edinburgh in January 1746 with substantial reinforcements and assumed command of the government forces. He at once moved north, relieving Stirling Castle, which the Jacobites had besieged, and driving the rebel army before him. The Jacobites abandoned the siege and retreated northward across the River Forth, then through the passes of Perthshire and Aberdeenshire, with Cumberland in methodical pursuit. By February, Cumberland had secured Stirling and Perth and advanced to Aberdeen, where he established his headquarters and drilled his army through the late winter months, preparing them specifically to counter the Highland charge (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4556–4570).

The March to Culloden

In the beginning of April 1746, Cumberland began his march from Aberdeen. On 12 April, he crossed the deep and rapid River Spey in full view of the Jacobite advanced posts, who offered no opposition — a sign of the demoralisation and physical exhaustion of the rebel army. The Jacobites, underfed and undersupplied, had been living on short rations for weeks; many of the Highland clansmen had drifted home to see to their families and crops, and those who remained were a fraction of the force that had marched to Derby four months earlier (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4569–4573).

At Nairn, Cumberland received intelligence that Charles Edward Stuart had advanced from Inverness to Culloden Moor, determined to offer battle. The Prince, against the advice of Lord George Murray, was resolved to fight.

The Opposing Forces

The Jacobite Army

By 16 April 1746, Charles Edward Stuart’s army had been reduced by desertion and detachment to approximately 5,000 men, though some estimates place the effective fighting strength as low as 3,500–4,000. The army was a coalition of Scottish clan regiments — Camerons, Stewarts of Appin, MacDonalds, MacPhersons, MacLachlans, MacLeans, Chisholms, Frasers, and many others — together with a small number of Irish and French regular troops from the Irish Brigade of the French army, and a lowland Scottish regiment, the Royal Ecossais. The army had approximately 12 field guns, mostly light and poorly served.

The Jacobite army was desperately short of provisions. On the night of 15 April, the Prince ordered a night attack on Cumberland’s camp at Nairn, hoping to catch the government troops in the midst of their celebrations of Cumberland’s 25th birthday. The march was mismanaged — the Highlanders stumbled through the darkness in confusion, lost their way, and at two in the morning, with dawn approaching and the element of surprise lost, Murray ordered the army back to Culloden, exhausted, hungry, and demoralised.

The Government Army

The Duke of Cumberland commanded approximately 9,000 men of the best troops in the British army, many of them veterans of the War of the Austrian Succession. The army was composed of regiments of the British line, with supporting artillery and cavalry:

Cumberland’s artillery consisted of ten 3-pounder battalion guns and several heavier pieces, served by experienced gunners from the Royal Artillery. In total, the government force outnumbered the Jacobites by nearly two to one and possessed an overwhelming superiority in artillery and cavalry.

The Battlefield

The ground chosen by Charles Edward Stuart and his adjutant-general, the Irish officer John O’Sullivan, has been almost universally condemned by military historians. The battlefield was Culloden Moor (or Drummossie Moor), a flat, open tract of boggy heathland about six miles east of Inverness, bounded on one side by a wall and on the other by waterlogged ground.

The site was entirely unsuitable for the Highland style of fighting. The Jacobite army’s greatest tactical weapon was the Highland charge — a shock assault by clansmen armed with broadswords and Lochaber axes who would advance at a run after firing a single volley, break through the enemy line in the few seconds before the defenders could reload, and throw the opposing formation into hand-to-hand chaos. This tactic had succeeded brilliantly at Prestonpans (September 1745) and at Falkirk Muir (January 1746). But it required firm, dry ground for a rapid advance, and it required an enemy unprepared for its ferocity.

At Culloden, the ground was soft and waterlogged in the centre, and the Jacobite front line would have to advance across 300 to 400 yards of open, boggy moorland under sustained artillery and musket fire before they could close with the government line. Lord George Murray, who had opposed the choice of ground, warned the Prince that the position was “not proper for Highlanders.” His advice was overruled.

The Battle

The Cannonade

The battle began at approximately one o’clock in the afternoon of 16 April 1746. The Jacobite army, drawn up in thirteen divisions (Belsham’s account), stood exposed on the open moor while Cumberland’s artillery opened a devastating fire. The government gunners, using grapeshot and roundshot, methodically raked the Jacobite ranks for nearly twenty minutes before the Highland line was ordered to advance. The Jacobite artillery replied but was outmatched in weight of metal, skill of service, and volume of fire.

The Highland Charge

Charles Edward Stuart, watching from a slight elevation behind his line, gave the order to advance. The Highland regiments began their charge — but the ground was against them from the first step. The waterlogged bog in the centre of the moor broke the cohesion of the Jacobite line. The right wing, under Lord George Murray, advanced more rapidly than the left, which was slowed by a wall and the softer ground. The clansmen on the right, the Atholl Brigade and the Camerons, charged with their traditional fury and reached the government line — but in a ragged, disordered formation rather than the compact mass that had broken Cope at Prestonpans.

Cumberland, having learned from the disasters of Prestonpans and Falkirk, had trained his troops to meet the Highland charge with a specific tactic. The front rank was ordered to fire by platoons — a disciplined, rolling volley that kept up a continuous fire — rather than a single, simultaneous discharge. Those men whose bayonets were directed at an incoming Highlander were to thrust at the man to their right rather than straight ahead, so that each soldier covered the open side of his neighbour, a technique designed to negate the famous skill of the Highlanders in parrying bayonets with their targets (small shields) before delivering the sword stroke (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4581–4593).

The effect was murderous. The Highland charge, deprived of its momentum by the boggy ground and shattered by the platoon fire, faltered and broke. The Camerons of Lochiel, leading the assault on the left of the government front line, took terrible losses from Barrell’s and Wolfe’s regiments. Lochiel himself was wounded in both ankles as he led the charge. The Atholl Brigade suffered similar devastation.

The Collapse of the Jacobite Right

The right wing of the Jacobite army, composed of the best Highland troops, was destroyed within the first twenty minutes of the infantry engagement. Murray’s Atholl men, Lochiel’s Camerons, and the Stewarts of Appin were cut down by the disciplined fire of the government regiments, and the survivors who reached the bayonet line found no breach — only a wall of levelled steel. The boggy ground in the centre had delayed the second rank of the charge just long enough for the first wave to be annihilated before support could arrive.

The MacDonald Refusal and the Left Wing

On the Jacobite left, the regiments of Clan Donald — the MacDonalds of Clanranald, Glengarry, and Keppoch — had been placed on the left of the line, contrary to the ancient tradition of the McDonalds that they should hold the right wing (the place of honour). The MacDonalds, nursing this grievance and seeing their kinsmen on the right being destroyed, advanced slowly and reluctantly. When they finally charged, they were already demoralised, and the government fire broke them before they could close.

The left wing’s collapse exposed the Irish and French regulars in the second line, who put up a stubborn resistance. The Irish Picquets of the French service and the Royal Ecossais held their ground and exchanged volleys with the government line for a considerable time, covering the retreat of what remained of the Highland force. But they were vastly outnumbered and ultimately overwhelmed.

The Rout

Within thirty minutes of the first infantry engagement, the Jacobite army had disintegrated. Cumberland ordered his cavalry — Hawley’s dragoons — to pursue the fleeing Highlanders. The government horse swept across the battlefield, cutting down fugitives without mercy (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4593–4598). Orders had been issued to give no quarter, and they were obeyed with grim thoroughness.

The entire battle, from the opening artillery fire to the final rout, lasted less than an hour. The Jacobite army had ceased to exist as a fighting force.

Casualties

The disparity in casualties was staggering. Approximately 1,200 to 1,500 Jacobites lay dead on the moor, with a further 500 to 1,000 wounded. Many of the wounded were killed where they lay by the pursuing government troops. The government forces lost approximately 50 killed and about 260 wounded — a ratio that reflects the one-sided nature of the engagement.

The Butcher of Cumberland

The brutality that followed the battle earned William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland the lasting enmity of the Scottish Highlands and the nickname “Butcher Cumberland.” In his own dispatch to London, Cumberland reported that “the rebels have lost above a thousand men killed,” but subsequent accounts — including that of William Belsham — recorded that orders were issued to give no quarter, and that “unnecessary and wanton barbarities were committed on the persons and families of the rebels long after the cessation of resistance” (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4597–4605).

Belsham, though no friend to the Jacobite cause, wrote that Cumberland “sullied the glory of his victory by displaying a savage thirst of revenge, and a courage untinctured with the feelings of humanity.” He added, however, a qualification — that Cumberland’s conduct on no other occasion was tainted with this imputation, and that the severity may have been prompted either by the “rage which is so frequent and horrid a concomitant and characteristic of civil discord,” or by a genuine belief that “these severities were necessary for the prevention of further resistance on the part of the rebels” (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4606–4614).

The pursuit after the battle was particularly savage. Government troops scoured the countryside, burning houses, driving off cattle, and killing anyone suspected of having fought with the Jacobites. Prisoners were summarily executed on the battlefield and in the days that followed. Wounded Jacobites found on the moor were bayoneted where they lay. The savagery of the repression confirmed in the Highland mind the image of Cumberland as a tyrant and became a lasting grievance transmitted through Gaelic oral tradition.

The Flight of Charles Edward Stuart

Charles Edward Stuart escaped from the field of battle with great difficulty. He was led away by a small party of retainers as the government dragoons swept across the moor, and began the five-month flight that became the most romanticised episode of the entire rising. He wandered as a fugitive through the woods and lakes and mountains of the Western Highlands and the Hebrides, passing through a series of extraordinary adventures “to be paralleled only by those of King Charles II after the Battle of Worcester” (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4615–4629).

A price of £30,000 was set on his head — an enormous sum, equivalent to a lifetime’s income for a Highland gentleman — yet not a single Highlander betrayed him. He was hidden by Flora MacDonald, who famously smuggled him across the sea to Skye disguised as an Irish spinning-maid named Betty Burke. After five months of concealment and narrow escapes, he embarked on 20 September 1746 from Loch nan Uamh — the very spot where he had landed fourteen months before — on a French ship that carried him to Morlaix in Brittany.

He never set foot on British soil again.

Aftermath

The Pacification of the Highlands

Cumberland’s victory at Culloden was followed by a systematic campaign to destroy the capacity of the Highlands to support another rising. The government pursued what amounted to a policy of military occupation and cultural suppression:

  • The Disarming Act (1746) — prohibited the possession of weapons by Highlanders and made the wearing of Highland dress (the tartan kilt and plaid) a criminal offence, punishable by transportation for six months for a first offence and seven years for a second.
  • The Heritable Jurisdictions Act (1747) — abolished the feudal courts and legal authority of the Highland chiefs, breaking the legal foundation of the clan system.
  • Forfeiture of Estates — the lands of Jacobite chiefs were confiscated by the Crown, and many were purchased by Lowland or English landowners.
  • Military Occupation — fortresses were built or strengthened at Fort George, Fort Augustus, and Fort William, while a network of military roads (following the earlier pattern of General Wade’s roads after the 1715 rising) was extended to permit the rapid movement of troops into the interior.
  • The Episcopal Church Suppressed — the Scottish Episcopal Church, with which many Jacobites were associated, was subjected to penal legislation.

These measures, sustained over decades, destroyed the Highland clan system as a military and political force and laid the administrative foundations for the Highland Clearances of the later 18th and 19th centuries.

The Trials and Executions

The executions that followed the suppression of the rising were, in Belsham’s judgement, “much more numerous than the necessity of the case required” (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4635–4638). Three Jacobite peers were tried for high treason and beheaded on Tower Hill: the Lords Balmerino and Kilmarnock, who had commanded Jacobite regiments, and Lord Lovat, the aged, twice-traitorous chief of the Frasers, who had fought for the government in 1715 and for the Jacobites in 1745, and who — at over eighty years of age — was the last man to be executed by decapitation in England. The Earl of Derwentwater was also executed, being arraigned on the sentence passed against him in 1716. Only the Earl of Cromarty, who had surrendered and whose wife famously threw herself at the king’s feet, received a pardon (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4638–4644).

The End of the Jacobite Threat

Thus ended, in Belsham’s words, “the last effort of the Stuart family to re-ascend that throne which they had forfeited by folly the most egregious, and attempts the most flagitious and criminal” (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4632–4635). The Forty-Five was the last Jacobite rising; Culloden was its death-blow. Never again would a Stuart claimant lead an army on British soil, never again would the Highland charge terrify a government army, and never again would the question of the British succession be decided in the field of battle.

The Duke of Cumberland, on his return to London, was received as a conquering hero. Both Houses of Parliament presented addresses of congratulation to the King and of thanks to the Duke. The House of Commons added £25,000 per annum to Cumberland’s revenues, and he was extolled “as equal to the most illustrious of its heroes” (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4645–4659). The idolatry did not survive the exposure of the brutality that had accompanied the victory, and Cumberland’s historical reputation has been dominated by the epithet “Butcher” — a word that his admirers at the time would never have applied to the victor of Culloden.

Key Figures

Government

  • William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland (1721–1765) — The third and youngest son of George II, Captain-General of the British army. Commanded the government forces at Culloden. His victories against the Jacobites made him briefly the most popular man in Britain, but the brutality of the subsequent pacification earned him the enduring nickname “the Butcher of Cumberland.” The £25,000 annual addition to his income from Parliament was a measure of the gratitude — and the anxiety — of the Hanoverian regime.
  • Lieutenant-General Henry Hawley (c. 1685–1759) — A professional soldier of harsh reputation, Hawley had commanded the government forces at Falkirk Muir, where he had been defeated by the Jacobites. He commanded the second line at Culloden and led the cavalry pursuit that slaughtered the fleeing Highlanders.
  • Major-General John Huske (c. 1692–1761) — An able and experienced officer who commanded the left wing of the front line. His regiments bore the brunt of the Highland charge and held firm.
  • Colonel James Wolfe (1727–1759) — Then a young brigadier serving on Hawley’s staff. Wolfe was present at Culloden as an aide-de-camp and participated in the pursuit. He would later achieve immortal fame at the Battle of Quebec (1759). His involvement in the post-Culloden suppression was cited by his later detractors.

Jacobite

  • Charles Edward Stuart (1720–1788) — “Bonnie Prince Charlie,” the Young Pretender. His charisma and determination had launched the rising and carried it to Derby, but his poor judgement at Culloden — the choice of ground, the rejection of Murray’s advice, the mismanagement of the night attack on Nairn — destroyed his army and his cause. He escaped to France and died a drunken, broken exile in Rome.
  • Lord George Murray (1694–1760) — The finest military commander on the Jacobite side, a veteran of the 1715 rising and the War of the Austrian Succession. Murray had been the architect of the Jacobite army’s success at Prestonpans and the commander of the retreat from Derby. He argued vehemently against fighting at Culloden and, after the defeat, led the rearguard action that allowed the Prince to escape. Murray was subsequently attainted for high treason and spent the remainder of his life in exile on the Continent.
  • John O’Sullivan (c. 1700–c. 1760) — An Irish-born soldier in French service who served as Charles Edward Stuart’s adjutant-general and quartermaster-general. O’Sullivan has been heavily criticised by historians for his role in selecting the battlefield at Culloden Moor, a flat, boggy site that neutralised the Highland charge. His tactical arrangements for the battle were also poor, and much of the blame for the Jacobite defeat has been laid at his feet.
  • Donald Cameron of Lochiel (c. 1695–1748) — “Gentle Lochiel,” the chief of Clan Cameron and one of the most powerful and respected of the Jacobite chiefs. He raised over 700 Camerons for the rising and led the clan charge at Culloden, where he was shot through both ankles. He was carried from the field and later escaped to France, dying there in 1748.
  • Simon Fraser, 11th Lord Lovat (c. 1667–1747) — The aged and notoriously duplicitous chief of the Frasers. Lovat had served the government in the 1715 but threw in his lot with the Jacobites in 1745. He raised the Frasers for the Prince but did not command them in person at Culloden, remaining at his castle. His son led the clan regiment. After the rising, Lovat was captured, tried for high treason, and beheaded on Tower Hill on 9 April 1747 — the last man in England to suffer death by decapitation.
  • James Drummond, 3rd Duke of Perth (1713–1746) — A Scottish peer who commanded the Jacobite centre at Culloden. He was a capable officer who had led the Jacobite left at Prestonpans. Perth escaped the field and died aboard a French ship bound for France in May 1746.
