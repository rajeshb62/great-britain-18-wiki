Septennial Act (1716)

The Septennial Act (1716) — formally 1 Geo. I, st. 2, c. 38 — was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that extended the maximum duration of a Parliament from three to seven years. It was passed on 7 May 1716 by the Whig ministry of George I, led by Charles Townshend and James Stanhope, with the vigorous support of Robert Walpole. The Act remains one of the most consequential and controversial constitutional measures of the eighteenth century: it entrenched Whig oligarchy, reduced electoral contests, and became the cornerstone of Walpole’s system of political management for two decades (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2076–2542).

The Triennial Act (1694)

The Septennial Act repealed the Triennial Act 1694 (6 & 7 Will. & Mar., c. 2), which had been one of the key achievements of the post-Revolution settlement. That statute, passed under William III following the Glorious Revolution, declared that Parliament should be summoned at least once every three years and that no Parliament should continue longer than three years from the date of its first meeting. Its preamble recited that “by the ancient laws and statutes of this realm frequent parliaments ought to be held” and that “frequent NEW parliaments tend very much to the happy union and good agreement of the king and his people” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2476–2480).

The Triennial Act was widely regarded as a fundamental guarantee of English liberty. It ensured that the House of Commons, the elected element of the constitution, could not be perpetuated beyond its natural term by a ministry that had lost the confidence of the electorate. In the two decades of its operation, ten successive parliaments had been elected under its provisions, through two long and bloody wars and intense political faction.

Jacobite Context

The immediate precipitant of the Septennial Act was the Jacobite Rising of 1715, which had convulsed Britain within a year of the Hanoverian accession. The rebellion, led by the Earl of Mar in Scotland and supported by English Jacobites under Thomas Forster and the Earl of Derwentwater, was suppressed by February 1716 — but its psychological impact on the Whig ministry was profound.

The Whig leadership, who had impeached the former Tory ministers Robert Harley and Bolingbroke and driven the latter into Jacobite exile, had every reason to fear the verdict of a new election. The country remained deeply divided: Tory sentiment was strong in the counties, the landed gentry resented the new dynasty’s foreign entourage, and the Jacobite party, though militarily defeated, had not been extinguished. A general election in 1716 or early 1717 — the existing Parliament, elected in January 1715, was approaching the end of its three-year term — might well return a Tory majority, or at least a House of Commons hostile to the Whig programme of continental alliances, standing armies, and Hanoverian favour.

The preamble of the Septennial Bill itself acknowledged this context, declaring that “the Popish and Jacobite faction is so powerful as to threaten destruction to the present establishment” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2131–2133). The argument from security was the ministry’s principal justification: at a juncture so critical, with rebellion barely crushed and French and Swedish intrigues ongoing, a dissolution could be fatal. As Lord Carteret, the bill’s ablest defender in the Lords, put it, “this bill would fix the king and the present happy establishment on a lasting foundation” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2188–2190).

The Debate: Stability Versus Constitutional Violation

The bill, which originated in the House of Lords, provoked one of the most searching constitutional debates of the eighteenth century. It was opposed with extraordinary ability on both constitutional and prudential grounds.

In the House of Lords

The opposition in the Lords was led by the Earl of Nottingham (Daniel Finch), a former Tory who had broken with the Whigs over the intemperance of the administration’s prosecutions of the Jacobite lords. Nottingham argued that frequent parliaments were “of the essence of the English constitution, sanctioned by the practice of ages”; that the members of the lower house were “delegated by the body of the nation for a certain term of years, at the expiration of which they were no longer the representatives of the people”; and that by lengthening their own authority at pleasure, Parliament “deprived the people of the only remedy which the wisdom of our ancestors had provided against the ignorance and corruption of those who might be tempted to betray the trust reposed in them” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2107–2139).

Nottingham further warned that a long parliament would encourage every species of corruption: “the value of a seat would bear a determinate proportion to the legal duration of parliaments, and the purchase would rise accordingly”; that it would “both enhance the temptations, and multiply the opportunities, of a vicious ministry, to undermine the integrity and independency of parliaments”; and that the reasons urged for prolonging this particular parliament would apply with equal or greater force at the end of seven years, leading potentially to perpetual parliaments (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2140–2160).

Lord Carteret, then a rising Whig statesman, defended the bill with equal force. He acknowledged that frequent parliaments were a great security of the people’s rights, but insisted that the bill was directed against frequent elections, not frequent meetings. The Jacobite danger was real: “of all rebellions, this last was the most monstrous and unnatural — for all others were carried on under pretences of liberty, whereas the last was a rebellion for slavery” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2172–2179). On the question of commitment, the Lords voted 96 for, 61 against. Thirty peers — headed by the Duke of Somerset and the Duke of Shrewsbury — entered a formal protest (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2191–2195).

In the House of Commons

When the bill was transmitted to the Commons, opposition was “still more vehement and formidable” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2197–2198). Lord Guernsey (Heneage Finch) moved to reject the bill without reading it — a motion defeated, but a measure of the temper of the house.

The most celebrated Commons speech against the bill was delivered by William Shippen, the leader of the Jacobite Tories in the Commons and one of the finest parliamentary orators of the age. Shippen argued on multiple grounds. The claim that a new election might be dangerous was, he said, an insult to the king: “How is it that in the infancy of his reign, he hath deprived himself of the love and affection of a people who so lately received him with every expression of joy?” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2237–2239). The argument from foreign alliances was contemptible — to alter the constitution merely to gratify allies was to humble the nation’s dignity: “What emotions of indignation must not the insolence of this demand excite — especially if it happen to be urged by a state which owes its very being to England?” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2261–2265).

But Shippen’s most powerful argument was constitutional. The present parliament had been elected under the Triennial Act; its trust was a triennial trust. To extend it was to cease to be trustees of the people and to become their own electors: “I cannot discover by what rule of reason or law we, who are only representatives, can enlarge, to our own advantage, the authority delegated to us — or that by virtue of such delegated authority we can destroy the fundamental rights of our constitution” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2303–2309). The Triennial Act was a concession made to the people by William III “in the midst of his difficulties,” and to repeal it in the very commencement of the new reign was an act of astonishing ingratitude and impolicy (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2328–2342). He concluded with a memorable image: “A standing parliament… has been said to resemble a standing pool, the waters of which grow, for want of a fresh and free current, offensive and fetid. But the present parliament may more justly be compared to a torrent, which, in its furious and foaming course, desolates the land” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2361–2370).

The debate was concluded by Sir Robert Raymond, later Lord Raymond and Chief Justice of England, who delivered a comprehensive reply. He enumerated the arguments for the bill — the expense of elections, the divisions and animosities they excited, the advantages to enemies from domestic feuds, and the encouragement to allies — and refuted each in turn. Annual parliaments, not septennial, were the true constitutional remedy for electoral corruption. The expense argument proved too much: if men would bribe for a three-year seat, they would bribe proportionally more for seven. As for the allies: “Is it not to proclaim to the world that the king dare not call a new parliament? that he dare not trust the people in a new choice?” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2456–2458).

On the question of commitment, the Commons voted 284 for, 162 against — a majority of 122. Final passage was carried by 264 votes against 121, a “triumphant majority” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2510–2526). The bill received the royal assent on 7 May 1716.

Self-Extension Clause

A particularly contentious feature of the Act was that it not only fixed the maximum duration of future parliaments at seven years, but also extended the life of the existing parliament — which had been elected for three years under the Triennial Act — by an additional four years. As Belsham puts it with characteristic pungency: “being elected by the nation for three years, they elected themselves for four years more” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 2086–2087). This self-extension gave the measure an appearance of naked self-interest that its defenders never wholly refuted.

Consequences

Entrenchment of Whig Oligarchy

The immediate consequence of the Septennial Act was to secure the Whig ministry in power for the foreseeable future. The Parliament of 1715 continued until 1722 (when it was dissolved by the death of George I’s first Parliament), and the Whig-dominated electoral system that operated under the new seven-year cycle produced Commons after Commons that were amenable to Whig management. The Act effectively dismantled the electoral threat to Whig supremacy for a generation.

Reduction of Electoral Contests

One of the Act’s most remarked-upon effects was a dramatic reduction in the frequency and intensity of electoral contests. Under the Triennial Act, elections were a near-constant feature of political life, generating expense, local faction, and unpredictable shifts in the parliamentary balance. The longer interval dampened electoral activity: the cost of fighting elections increased (as Raymond had predicted), but the intervals were long enough that local interests consolidated rather than competed. This suited the Whig oligarchy, whose control of borough patronage and government placements could be exercised more effectively over a seven-year horizon.

Walpole’s Cornerstone

The Act became an essential element of Robert Walpole’s system of political management during his long ministry (1721–1742). A seven-year Parliament gave him the stability to build his patronage network, distribute places and pensions systematically, and maintain discipline over his followers without the constant disruption of electoral campaigns. His opponents — the “Patriot” opposition of William Pitt the Elder, Bolingbroke (after his return from exile), and the dissident Whigs — railed against the Septennial Act as the bulwark of “Robin’s” corruption, but were unable to muster sufficient force to repeal it. Belsham records a later “ineffectual attempt to repeal the septennial act” during the later years of the reign (Belsham, vol. II, line 479), suggesting that opposition to the measure never entirely subsided but never reached critical mass.

Endurance Until 1911

The Septennial Act remained in force for nearly two centuries. It was ultimately superseded by the Parliament Act 1911, which reduced the maximum duration of a Parliament to five years. During that long currency, the Act was defended on the same pragmatic grounds on which it had been passed: that frequent elections were disruptive, expensive, and destabilising, and that the mass of ordinary business could not be conducted under the shadow of a triennial dissolution. Critics, from John Wilkes in the 1760s to the Chartists in the 1840s, continued to argue that the Act had been a violation of constitutional principle and a self-interested power-grab by the Whig oligarchy — but the Septennial regime outlasted every attempt to restore the Triennial principle.

Constitutional Significance

The Septennial Act raised a question that troubled eighteenth-century constitutional thought: could Parliament limit its own duration? The Act’s opponents argued that the Triennial Act was a fundamental law, a compact between the sovereign and the people that could not be unilaterally altered by the representatives. Shippen and Raymond contended that a legislative body elected under one set of terms had no moral or legal authority to extend those terms without a fresh mandate from the electorate. The Act’s defenders, by contrast, relied on the doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty — the principle that the King-in-Parliament was the supreme legislative authority and could not be bound by its predecessors. This doctrine prevailed, and the Septennial Act became a precedent for the view that Parliament’s powers over the constitution were unlimited. The Act thus marks a pivotal moment in the development of the modern British constitution: the victory of absolute parliamentary sovereignty over the older notion of a fundamental or ancient constitution.

Key Figures

The Whig Ministry (in favour)

  • Charles Townshend, 2nd Viscount Townshend — Secretary of State for the Northern Department and a leading figure in the Whig ministry that introduced the bill. Townshend was Walpole’s brother-in-law and political ally at this stage, though they would later fall out.
  • James Stanhope, 1st Earl Stanhope — Secretary of State for the Southern Department, a soldier-diplomat who was the ministry’s driving force in foreign affairs and a staunch supporter of the Septennial measure.
  • Robert Walpole — First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer (from 1715), whose parliamentary management was essential in steering the bill through the Commons. Walpole’s later long ministry would depend heavily on the Act’s provisions.
  • Lord Carteret — The bill’s most effective defender in the Lords, who argued that septennial parliaments would fix the Hanoverian establishment on a lasting foundation.

Opposition

  • Earl of Nottingham (Daniel Finch) — Former Tory secretary of state who led the opposition in the Lords with a powerful constitutional critique of the bill as violating the essence of the English constitution.
  • William Shippen — Jacobite Tory MP who delivered the most celebrated Commons speech against the bill, arguing that the House could not extend its own delegated authority.
  • Sir Robert Raymond — Future Chief Justice of England whose comprehensive speech refuted each argument for the bill point by point and called for annual, not septennial, parliaments as the constitutional remedy.
  • Charles Seymour, 6th Duke of Somerset — Whig grandee who nevertheless joined the protest against the bill in the Lords.
  • Charles Talbot, 1st Duke of Shrewsbury — Lord Chamberlain and former Lord Treasurer who also protested the bill despite his Whig credentials.
