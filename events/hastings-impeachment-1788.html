Impeachment of Warren Hastings (1788–1795)

The Impeachment of Warren Hastings (1788–1795) was the longest, most spectacular, and most consequential political trial of eighteenth-century Britain. For 145 days spread over seven years, the former Governor-General of Bengal stood before the House of Lords in Westminster Hall, charged with high crimes and misdemeanours committed during his thirteen-year administration of the East India Company’s Indian possessions. The prosecution, led by Edmund Burke, was the first sustained parliamentary attempt to hold an imperial administrator accountable for the conduct of colonial government. Though Hastings was ultimately acquitted in 1795, the trial established the principle that the agents of the British Empire were answerable to Parliament for their actions, and it raised a generation of public consciousness about the nature and morality of British rule in India.

Background: Hastings’s Governorship of Bengal (1772–1785)

Warren Hastings arrived in India in 1750 as a junior clerk in the service of the East India Company and rose, through a combination of talent, ambition, and survival instinct, to become the first Governor-General of Bengal in 1773. The Company he served had been transformed by Robert Clive from a trading corporation into a territorial power; Hastings inherited an empire in crisis. The Company’s treasury was empty, its servants were corrupt, and its Indian neighbours — the Maratha Confederacy, the Sultanate of Mysore under Hyder Ali, and the Nizam of Hyderabad — were pressing at its borders.

Hastings’s administration was characterised by aggressive expansion, administrative reform, and financial improvisation. He reorganised the revenue collection of Bengal, established a system of civil and criminal courts, patronised Oriental scholarship (founding the Asiatic Society of Bengal with Sir William Jones in 1784), and fought a series of expensive and controversial wars. His defenders called him the “Saviour of India”; his critics saw him as a despot who had corrupted every principle of justice and humanity.

The Rohilla War (1774)

The first great controversy of Hastings’s administration was the Rohilla War of 1774. The Rohillas were Afghan settlers who had established an independent state in the territory between Oudh and the Maratha dominions. The Nawab of Oudh, Shuja-ud-Daula, coveted their country. Hastings agreed to lend him Company troops in exchange for a payment of forty lakhs of rupees. The Rohillas were defeated and their territory annexed to Oudh; the Company collected its fee. Burke later denounced this as a mercenary war of aggression — a cynical transaction in which the Company had sold its military power to a native prince for the destruction of a defenceless people.

The Benares Affair (1781)

In 1781, Hastings faced rebellion in Benares, the holy city of the Ganges. The Raja, Chait Singh, a vassal of the Company under the Vizier of Oudh, had fallen behind in his tribute. Hastings travelled to Benares to demand payment and, when Chait Singh resisted, ordered his arrest. The arrest provoked an insurrection: Chait Singh’s troops attacked the British party, Hastings barely escaped with his life, and a punitive military campaign was required to restore order. Hastings confiscated the Raja’s estates and imposed a heavy fine. Burke’s prosecution would treat the Benares affair as a case of extortion by a rapacious governor who had provoked a rebellion to justify plunder.

The Begums of Oudh (1782)

The most sensational charge concerned the treatment of the Begums of Oudh — the mother and grandmother of the Nawab of Oudh, Asaf-ud-Daula. The Begums possessed vast treasure, inherited from the late Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula. Hastings, needing funds for the war against Mysore and suspicious of the Begums’s loyalty, demanded that Asaf-ud-Daula compel them to surrender their wealth. When the Begums resisted, their eunuch servants were imprisoned and starved until the treasure was yielded. The sum extracted was enormous — perhaps £1 million. Richard Brinsley Sheridan’s speech on the Begums charge at the trial was described by contemporaries as the finest oration ever delivered in the English Parliament.

The Campaign Against Hastings

Edmund Burke’s Crusade

The driving force behind the impeachment was Edmund Burke. Burke had been interested in Indian affairs since the early 1770s, when he served on a select committee investigating the Company’s abuses. He studied the evidence with extraordinary thoroughness — reading reports, examining witnesses, and mastering the details of Indian revenue systems, treaty obligations, and dynastic politics. The more he learned, the more he became convinced that Hastings was not merely a corrupt administrator but a tyrant who had “destroyed the whole framework of the Indian constitution” and ground the people of India under a “system of despotism and depredation.”

Burke’s campaign gathered momentum in the early 1780s. In 1781, he secured the appointment of a secret committee to investigate Indian affairs. In 1783, he introduced Charles James Fox’s East India Bill, which would have transferred the Company’s political authority to parliamentary commissioners — a bill that was defeated in the Lords after the King’s personal intervention, bringing down the Fox-North Coalition. But the evidence of misgovernment continued to accumulate, and Burke pressed relentlessly for action against Hastings personally.

The ‘Unjust Judge’ Accusation

A key element in Burke’s indictment was the case of Nandakumar (Nuncomar), a wealthy Bengali Brahmin who had accused Hastings of accepting bribes in 1775. Hastings’s enemy on the Supreme Council, Philip Francis, had taken up the accusation — but before it could be pursued, Nandakumar was himself arrested, tried for forgery before the Supreme Court under Sir Elijah Impey, convicted, and hanged on 5 August 1775. Burke charged that this was a judicial murder — that Hastings had suborned the Chief Justice to destroy his accuser, and that the “pure ermine of justice was stained and soiled with the basest contamination.” The Nandakumar case became the foundation of the most serious articles of impeachment.

The Parliamentary Campaign (1784–1787)

In the session of 1784, Burke moved for an inquiry into the affairs of India, but was blocked by William Pitt the Younger’s ministry. Henry Dundas, Pitt’s Indian minister, who had been Burke’s collaborator on the earlier select committees, had by now drawn back. Burke, speaking in July 1784, described the sufferings of the Indian people in terms that moved even his opponents: “The miserable objects it exhibited — nations extirpated, provinces desolated, cities and countries overwhelmed in one mass of destruction — constantly dwelt on his imagination. The cries of the native Indians were never out of his ears; an impression of horror had seized on his mind, which deprived him of sleep, and night and day preyed upon his peace.”

In 1786, Burke at last succeeded. On 4 April 1786, he laid before the House of Commons twenty-two charges against Hastings, drawn from the massive evidence accumulated over five years. The charges covered the Rohilla War, the Benares confiscations, the plunder of the Begums of Oudh, the Nandakumar execution, corrupt contracts, oppressive revenue settlements, and the general abuse of power. On 10 May 1787, after a year of committee work and debate, the Commons voted to impeach Hastings, and Burke, in the name of the House of Commons and of all the commons of Great Britain, repaired to the bar of the House of Lords to present the impeachment.

The Trial (1788–1795)

The Opening in Westminster Hall

The trial opened on 13 February 1788 in Westminster Hall. The ancient hall, the largest secular building in the kingdom, had witnessed the coronation banquets of the Hanoverian kings and the trial of Charles I. For the Hastings impeachment, it was fitted up with great magnificence — galleries for the peeresses and the public, a throne for the Lord Chancellor, a bar for the managers of the Commons, and a special enclosure for the prisoner.

The spectacle was unprecedented. The King, the Queen, the Prince of Wales, and the entire peerage attended. The court was the House of Lords itself, sitting in full judicial capacity. The prosecution was conducted by the greatest orators of the age: Burke, Fox, Sheridan, and the rising young Charles Grey. The defendant was the former Governor-General of Bengal, a man of fifty-five, composed and dignified, who conducted his own defence.

Burke’s Opening Speech

Burke’s opening speech lasted four days — 15, 16, 18, and 19 February 1788. It was the most sustained rhetorical performance of his career, a moral indictment of imperialism that still resounds across two centuries. He described Hastings as a “captain-general of iniquity” who had “exercised power without law, and dominion without principle.” He painted a picture of India before Hastings’s arrival — a land of ancient civilisation, settled government, and contented people — and of India after — desolated by war, plundered by extortion, and degraded by oppression.

Belsham, who was no admirer of Burke, describes the speech as “filled with vehement invective, with much rhetorical exaggeration, and with matter wholly extraneous to the subject of the impeachment.” But the effect on the audience was immense. The speech was extolled by Burke’s admirers as “a more than Ciceronian effort of eloquence.” The diarists of the age recorded that women wept, that peers sat transfixed, and that the accumulated weight of moral outrage was like a physical presence in the hall.

Sheridan’s speech on the Begums of Oudh — which lasted five days — was the dramatic highlight of the trial. He described the imprisonment of the Begums’ eunuch servants, the confiscation of the treasure, and the cruelty of Hastings’s methods with such emotional power that half the House was in tears. Pitt himself, who had been sceptical of the impeachment, is said to have moved for an adjournment so that the house could recover from the impact of Sheridan’s oratory.

The Famous ‘Dagger’ Scene

One of the most famous moments of the trial — though it belongs to the House of Commons debates rather than the Lords trial — was the “dagger scene.” During the 1793 debates on the war with revolutionary France, Burke, who had by then broken with Fox over the French Revolution, dramatised his point by dramatically drawing a dagger and hurling it across the floor of the House. The gesture was so violent and unexpected that it caused a sensation. While this scene is conventionally associated with Burke’s later political passions rather than the Hastings trial itself, it illustrates the same rhetorical intensity that characterised his prosecution of Hastings — a willingness to shock, to gesture, to perform the outrage that his words described.

More directly relevant to the trial was Burke’s penchant for physical props and vivid imagery: he described the sword of government being “converted to an assassin’s dagger” and the “pure ermine of justice stained and soiled” in Hastings’s India. The language of the dagger ran throughout the prosecution’s case.

The Length of the Trial

The trial dragged on for seven years — from February 1788 to April 1795. There were 145 days of actual proceedings, stretched over 18 sessions of Parliament. The delays were caused by the complexity of the evidence, the political distractions of the French Revolution (which from 1789 absorbed the energies of everyone involved), the illnesses of the principals (Burke was often too ill to attend), and the tactical procrastination of Hastings’s defence.

The length itself became a political issue. In 1794, during the debates on the Habeas Corpus suspension and the treason trials, opponents of the government remarked ironically that while the House was voting impeachments, the advanced guard of the enemy might meet them in the lobby. The trial’s protraction gradually drained the public’s interest and the prosecution’s momentum.

The Verdict: Acquittal (1795)

On 23 April 1795, the House of Lords delivered its verdict. The Lords voted on each of the articles separately. On every count, the verdict was not guilty. The most important votes were:

The precise numbers were not recorded, but it was known that a substantial majority of the Lords voted for acquittal on every charge. The Lord Chancellor, Lord Thurlow, who had opposed the impeachment from the start, pronounced the judgment with evident satisfaction.

Hastings was free. But the cost of his freedom was immense. He claimed to have spent £70,000 on his defence — a sum that left him in what he called “genteel poverty” for the rest of his life. He was not merely acquitted; he was ruined.

Significance

Accountability for Imperial Officials

The impeachment of Warren Hastings established a principle of incalculable importance: that the servants of the British Empire could be called to account before Parliament for their conduct in office. No previous colonial administrator had ever been subjected to such scrutiny. The trial created a precedent that imperial governance was answerable to the same standards of justice that governed the constitution at home. That Hastings was acquitted mattered less, in the long run, than the fact that the trial had taken place at all.

The Public Debate on Empire

The trial brought the realities of British rule in India into the public consciousness of Britain for the first time. The detailed evidence of Indian civilisation, Indian legal traditions, and Indian suffering that was presented at the trial — much of it gathered by Burke himself — educated a generation of Britons about the subcontinent they were conquering. The trial gave rise to a literature of imperial criticism, including Burke’s own published speeches, which remain among the most powerful indictments of colonial exploitation ever written.

The Constitutional Meaning

The impeachment was also a constitutional event of the first order. It was an assertion of the authority of Parliament — and particularly of the House of Commons — over the executive government and over the chartered corporations that exercised sovereignty abroad. It demonstrated that the Commons could impeach a servant of the Crown even against the wishes of the ministry (for Pitt’s government, though it eventually supported parts of the prosecution, had never been enthusiastic). It revived the ancient procedure of impeachment — dormant since the trial of Henry Sacheverell in 1710 — and demonstrated its continuing relevance as a check on executive power.

The Critique of Burke’s Methods

The acquittal owed much to the political context and to flaws in the prosecution. The French Revolution, which began in 1789, transformed the political landscape. Burke’s denunciation of the Revolution (in his Reflections on the Revolution in France, 1790) made him a hero to conservative opinion but destroyed his political standing with the Whig party, splitting it irrevocably. Fox, Sheridan, and the majority of the Whigs supported the Revolution; Burke opposed it. The alliance that had brought Hastings to trial disintegrated in rancour. By 1794, Burke was politically isolated, and the trial had become a partisan embarrassment.

Moreover, many observers — including William Belsham, who recorded the events with a Whig sensibility — believed that Burke’s conduct of the prosecution had been injudicious. His opening speech, though magnificent, was filled with “rhetorical exaggeration” and “matter wholly extraneous to the subject.” The evidence, when it finally came, was less damning than the rhetoric had promised. Hastings, by contrast, was a calm, methodical, and effective witness in his own defence, patiently explaining the complexities of Indian administration to a British audience that had wearied of the whole affair.

Hastings’s Later Life

After his acquittal, Hastings retired to Daylesford in Gloucestershire — an estate he had purchased in 1788 with the intention of re-establishing the family seat that his ancestors had lost two centuries before. He spent his remaining years in obscurity, managing his estate, writing occasional memoranda on Indian affairs, and reading the classical authors who had been his lifelong companions.

The East India Company granted him an annuity of £4,000 per year, and in 1814, at the age of eighty-one, he was summoned to give evidence before the House of Commons on Indian affairs — a moment of belated recognition from the institution he had served. He was also given a loan of £50,000 by the Company to pay his debts, though the generosity was as much about protecting the Company’s reputation as about honouring Hastings’s service.

He died on 22 August 1818, at the age of eighty-five, and was buried in the church at Daylesford. His monument — designed by his admirers in the East India Company — stands in Westminster Abbey, opposite that of Robert Clive.

Key Figures

  • Warren Hastings (1732–1818) — The defendant. First Governor-General of Bengal, architect of British rule in India, patron of Oriental scholarship. Acquitted but financially ruined. See Warren Hastings.

  • Edmund Burke (1729–1797) — The prosecutor. The greatest orator of the age, who made the Hastings impeachment the cause of his political life. His speeches remain foundational texts in the critique of imperialism. See Edmund Burke.

  • Charles James Fox (1749–1806) — Burke’s principal ally in the impeachment, who opened the Benares charge. A leader of the Whig opposition, Fox broke with Burke over the French Revolution, fatally weakening the prosecution’s political base. See Charles James Fox.

  • Philip Francis (1740–1818) — Hastings’s sworn enemy on the Supreme Council (1774–1778), who returned to England and became the prosecution’s most knowledgeable witness. Francis had fought a duel with Hastings in 1778 and pursued him with undying animosity. Possibly the author of the Letters of Junius.

  • Richard Brinsley Sheridan (1751–1816) — The playwright-politician whose speech on the Begums of Oudh was the trial’s greatest rhetorical moment. His oratory reduced the House to tears and was said by contemporaries to have been the finest ever delivered in Parliament.

  • William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) — The Prime Minister. Pitt’s government was ambivalent about the impeachment: he supported some charges (voting for the Begums article) but his ministry never threw its full weight behind the prosecution. His Pitt’s India Act (1784) had already reformed Indian governance. See Pitt’s India Act.

  • Henry Dundas (1742–1811) — Pitt’s minister for Indian affairs. Originally a collaborator with Burke on the Indian committees, Dundas drew back as the impeachment progressed and became a target of Burke’s wrath for his “callous insensibility.”

  • Sir Elijah Impey (1732–1809) — Chief Justice of the Supreme Court at Calcutta, who presided over the trial and execution of Nandakumar. Hastings’s enemies charged that Impey was Hastings’s tool; his recall to England was demanded by the Commons in 1784.

  • Lord Thurlow (1731–1806) — Lord Chancellor, who opposed the impeachment from the outset and pronounced the verdict of acquittal. Famously dismissed the parliamentary reports on Indian abuses as “the romance of Robinson Crusoe.”
