Jacobite Rising of 1745

The Jacobite Rising of 1745 (also known as the Forty-Five Rebellion) was the last major armed attempt by the Stuart dynasty to reclaim the British throne. Led by Charles Edward Stuart — the Young Pretender, popularly known as Bonnie Prince Charlie — the rising erupted in the summer of 1745 and, within four months, carried a Highland army from the remote western islands of Scotland to within a few days’ march of London. The enterprise collapsed in the spring of 1746 at the Battle of Culloden, after which the British government under George II and his son William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland enacted a systematic destruction of the Highland clan system. The ‘45 was the last pitched battle fought on British soil and the final chapter of the Jacobite military challenge to the Hanoverian succession.

Background

The Jacobite Hope after 1715

The failure of the Jacobite Rising of 1715 had not extinguished the Stuart cause. James Francis Edward Stuart, the Old Pretender, continued to reside at Rome, recognised by the papacy and the French court as the rightful king of Great Britain. His exile had become a fixture of European diplomacy. Throughout the 1720s and 1730s, successive French ministries toyed with the idea of a Stuart restoration as a means of weakening Britain, though the pragmatism of Robert Walpole’s long ministry kept the peace. The Jacobite party within Britain — a shifting coalition of disaffected Tories, Catholic recusants, Episcopalians in Scotland, and Highland clansmen bound by feudal loyalty to the Stuarts — nursed their hopes in private, awaiting a conjuncture of events favourable to a new attempt.

The War of the Austrian Succession

The death of Emperor Charles VI in 1740 and the ensuing War of the Austrian Succession provided that conjuncture. Britain, allied with Austria, committed substantial forces to the continent in support of Maria Theresa. A British army under the personal command of George II fought at Dettingen in 1743 — the last time a British monarch led troops in the field — while the Duke of Cumberland commanded in Flanders, fighting the disastrous Battle of Fontenoy in May 1745. By the summer of 1745, the best British regiments were on the continent, leaving the home islands dangerously denuded of regular troops. Only a skeleton force remained in Scotland under Sir John Cope, consisting of raw recruits and a few veteran companies. The French court, seeing an opportunity, encouraged the Young Pretender to make a descent on Britain, promising arms, money, and a diversionary invasion of southern England (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4368–4377).

The Landing

Eriskay and the Gathering

Charles Edward Stuart, aged twenty-four, handsome, charismatic, and utterly determined, resolved to stake everything on a bold venture. He had no permission from the French king for an expeditionary force; his father, the Old Pretender, counselled caution. Charles ignored them both. On 23 July 1745, he landed on the island of Eriskay in the Outer Hebrides, accompanied by only the ‘Seven Men of Moidart’ — a small party of Irish and Scottish exiles, including the elderly Sir Thomas Sheridan and the marquis of Tullibardine. The landing party carried a modest store of arms and a few thousand louis d’or, but no army, no artillery, and no assured support (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4380–4383).

The initial reception was discouraging. Alexander Macdonald of Sleat and Norman Macleod, the most powerful chiefs in the region, both refused to join him, deeming the enterprise hopeless. But Charles’s personal charm, his fluency in Gaelic, and his passionate conviction persuaded the younger generation. Donald Cameron of Lochiel, the most influential of the Jacobite chiefs, reluctantly committed his clan after much agonising — reportedly telling Charles that he would join the rising rather than see the prince’s cause perish for want of a single clan. With Lochiel’s adhesion, others followed.

Glenfinnan

On 19 August 1745, Charles raised his father’s standard — a banner of crimson silk bearing the white cockade — at Glenfinnan at the head of Loch Shiel. Around 800 Camerons and MacDonalds had assembled. Within days, the numbers swelled as clansmen from the Camerons, MacDonalds of Clanranald, MacDonalds of Glengarry, MacDonells of Keppoch, MacGregors, and Grants of Glenmoriston poured in. The army, initially around 3,000 strong, began its march south-eastward through the Great Glen (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4383–4392).

The March to Edinburgh and Prestonpans

The Capture of Edinburgh

Sir John Cope, the commander-in-chief in Scotland, collected what troops he could at Stirling. The Jacobite army descended through the passes of the Highlands, moving more rapidly than Cope anticipated. While Cope marched to Inverness, hoping to block the rebel advance in the north, the Jacobites swept past him through the Corrieyairack Pass and descended on the Lowlands. By 4 September 1745, they had crossed the Forth. Panic spread among the Protestant population. The capital, Edinburgh, was protected only by a city guard and an untrained citizen militia.

On 17 September, a detachment of Highlanders under the command of James Drummond, Duke of Perth (a title in the Jacobite peerage) entered the city through the Netherbow Port under cover of night, seizing the gates without firing a shot. The garrison — the town guard and a few dragoons — offered no resistance. On the morning of 18 September, Charles Edward Stuart entered Edinburgh in triumph, walking in procession to the Palace of Holyroodhouse, the ancestral residence of the Stuart kings, where he took up quarters. His father was proclaimed King James VIII of Scotland and III of England at the Mercat Cross (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4422–4431). A reward of £30,000 was offered by the Regency in London for Charles’s apprehension (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4393–4396).

The Battle of Prestonpans

Cope, hearing of Edinburgh’s fall, hurried back south from Inverness. On 20 September, he encamped with an army of about 3,000 regular troops — infantry, dragoons, and artillery — near the village of Prestonpans, east of Edinburgh, on a flat coastal plain intersected by drainage ditches and enclosures. The Jacobite army, roughly equal in number, approached under cover of a mist on the morning of 21 September.

The battle was over in under ten minutes. The Highlanders charged with broadswords and Lochaber axes, their terrifying war-cries and ferocious momentum overwhelming the government soldiers, who were unaccustomed to the Highland manner of fighting. The government line disintegrated; the dragoons fled. Cope’s regulars were routed with heavy losses. Belsham records that Colonel James Gardiner, a veteran officer of distinction and a native of Scotland, had warned Cope that his dispositions were unsound and predicted defeat. Gardiner, refusing to survive the disaster, fell covered with wounds in sight of his own mansion (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4437–4456).

Cope was heavily censured for his conduct of the engagement. The Jacobite victory at Prestonpans left Charles in effective control of Scotland, with only a few fortresses (Edinburgh Castle, Stirling Castle, Fort William) still in government hands. The victory brought in new adherents: the lords Kilmarnock, Cromarty, and Balmerino, along with many other men of rank, now joined the prince. French ships landed supplies and money at Montrose and Stonehaven. For the first time, the rising, which had seemed a romantic gamble, wore a serious aspect (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4457–4464).

The March into England

The Decision to Invade

After Prestonpans, the Jacobite court at Holyrood debated the next move. The cautious counsel of Lord George Murray — the ablest Jacobite general, a seasoned soldier who had fought in the British army before joining the Stuart cause — was to consolidate control of Scotland, await the promised French invasion of southern England, and advance only with adequate support. Charles, impatient and headstrong, overruled him. The prince believed that a rapid march into England would trigger a popular Jacobite uprising among the English Tories and country gentry, who he imagined were secretly yearning for a Stuart restoration.

On 31 October 1745, the Jacobite army — now approximately 5,000–6,000 strong, though some sources put the figure lower — marched south from Edinburgh. They advanced in two columns, one under Murray and one under the Duke of Perth, maintaining tight discipline and forgoing the plunder that might have alienated the English population (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4469–4484).

Carlisle and Manchester

The first significant obstacle was Carlisle, a walled city on the English border held by a weak garrison of militia and invalided soldiers. Surrender was demanded; the garrison capitulated on 15 November after a token resistance, giving the Jacobites their first English foothold and a store of arms and supplies.

The army marched south through Lancashire, passing through Penrith, Kendal, and Lancaster. At Manchester on 26 November, they met their warmest reception in England. A regiment of Jacobite volunteers, the Manchester Regiment, was formed under the command of a young Catholic merchant, Francis Towneley. Elsewhere, the expected English rising did not materialise. The towns they passed through were sullenly loyal or openly hostile; the mass of the population showed no enthusiasm for the Stuart cause. The Jacobite general John O’Sullivan later complained that the English Jacobites were content to drink to the prince’s health in private but would not risk their necks in public (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4488–4489).

The Crisis at Derby

By the end of November, the Jacobite army had reached the heart of the Midlands. Crossing the Mersey at Stockport on 29 November, they marched through Macclesfield and Congleton, reaching Derby on 4 December 1745. London was in a state of near-panic. King George II prepared to take the field in person, with the royal standard ordered to be erected on Finchley Common. The shops were shut; there was a run on the Bank of England. Marshal Saxe’s promised French invasion — a force of some 15,000 men assembled at Dunkirk — had been frustrated by contrary winds and the vigilance of the Royal Navy, which kept a blockading squadron off the Channel coast.

On 5 December 1745, the Jacobite commanders assembled at a council of war at the house of the Earl of Exeter in Derby. The situation was bleak. The army had been marching and fighting for three months; many Highlanders were barefoot, their supplies exhausted, their pay in arrears. Three government armies were converging on them: the Duke of Cumberland, recalled from Flanders, was approaching with a well-equipped army of veteran troops; General Wade’s army at Newcastle was marching south-west; and a third force under Sir John Ligonier was assembling in Staffordshire. The French invasion had not materialised. The English Jacobites had not risen. Lord George Murray argued forcefully that it would be madness to advance further: they were outnumbered, outmanoeuvred, and dangerously extended. The only rational course was to retreat to Scotland, regroup, and await the promised French reinforcements.

Charles argued passionately for continuing the advance. He insisted that the government’s forces were scattered, that London was defenceless, and that a bold stroke would yet turn the tide. For hours the debate raged. Murray carried the council, and the decision was made: the army would retreat.

This was the decisive moment of the rising. As Belsham records, the retreat from Derby was “a virtual relinquishment of his object, which indeed could only be accomplished by a series of desperate efforts crowned with continual and decisive success” (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4492–4507). Charles never forgave Murray for what he regarded as a cowardly betrayal. On the Jacobite side, the belief took root that they had been only a few days from taking London and that the retreat had cost them the throne.

The Retreat to Scotland

The Harried March North

No sooner was the retreat resolved upon than it was executed with astonishing speed. On 6 December 1745 — the Feast of St Nicholas — the Jacobite army abandoned Derby and marched north. The vanguard entered Manchester on the 9th; by the 19th, they were at Carlisle. The retreat was a remarkable feat of military discipline: the Highlanders covered the distance in just thirteen days, outpacing the pursuing armies of Cumberland and Wade. Belsham notes that no violence was offered, no outrage or rapine committed, despite passing through “a country abounding with plunder, and presenting every temptation to the unrestrained indulgence of military rapacity” (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4518–4528).

Cumberland, now in command of the pursuit, invested Carlisle on 21 December. The garrison of 400 men — mostly the Manchester Regiment — held out for nine days but surrendered at discretion on 30 December. Cumberland showed no mercy: the captured officers were marked for execution as traitors, and the common soldiers were imprisoned, many to be transported to the colonies.

Falkirk and the Last Jacobite Victory

The Jacobite army crossed the Esk back into Scotland by 20 December, reaching Glasgow on 3 January 1746. Charles exacted heavy contributions from the city in punishment for its loyalty to the Hanoverian cause, then marched on Stirling and invested Stirling Castle. General Henry Hawley, who had assumed command of the government forces in Scotland, marched from Edinburgh to relieve the fortress. The two armies met at Falkirk Mur on 17 January 1746.

The battle began in a driving rainstorm. Hawley’s dragoons attempted to charge the Highland line but bogged down in the sodden ground. The Jacobite clans, led by Lord George Murray and supported by the Duke of Perth, delivered one more devastating Highland charge, routing the government left wing. Hawley’s army was driven from the field, though the pursuit was checked by the superior discipline of the regular infantry, which retired in good order. The Jacobite victory was real but incomplete — Hawley escaped with most of his army intact, having lost “more disgrace than loss,” as Belsham puts it, noting Hawley’s empty boast that with two regiments of dragoons he would drive the rebel army from one end of the kingdom to the other (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4539–4550).

Culloden

Cumberland Takes Command

Falkirk proved to be the last Jacobite victory. The Duke of Cumberland himself arrived at Edinburgh with substantial reinforcements and assumed personal command of the government army, moving towards Linlithgow. The Jacobites, their supplies dwindling and their Highland troops drifting away to their homes, abandoned the siege of Stirling Castle on 1 February and retreated northward. Cumberland secured the passes of Stirling and Perth and pushed on to Aberdeen, which he reached in late February.

For six weeks, the armies rested and prepared. Cumberland drilled his army at Aberdeen, training them specifically in bayonet drill designed to counter the Highland charge — the tactic that had swept away Cope and Hawley. The Jacobite army, meanwhile, dwindled as clansmen returned home. Charles, against the advice of his best officers, resolved to fight a pitched battle on open ground.

The Battle

On 8 April 1746, Cumberland began his advance from Aberdeen. On 12 April, he crossed the River Spey without opposition. On 14 April, the Jacobite army, now reduced to around 5,000 exhausted, hungry, and poorly supplied men, was drawn up on Culloden Moor, a flat, boggy expanse to the east of Inverness. Charles Edward Stuart, against the advice of Lord George Murray, insisted on fighting on this unfavourable ground, which was ill-suited to the Highland charge.

The morning of 16 April 1746 was cold, wet, and windy. Cumberland’s army, approximately 9,000 strong — well-fed, well-rested, and drilled to perfection — formed up in three lines, their infantry equipped with plug bayonets and loaded muskets. The Jacobite army was drawn up in thirteen divisions; many of the clansmen had had nothing to eat for two days and had spent the night huddled on the moor in the rain.

The battle began around 1 p.m. with an artillery duel. Cumberland’s guns, served by the Royal Artillery, were far more effective than the Jacobite cannon, whose ammunition was defective and poorly aimed. The Highland line was torn by roundshot for twenty minutes before the order to charge was finally given. The charge — the famous Highland onslaught with broadsword and Lochaber axe — had been the Jacobites’ great weapon at Prestonpans and Falkirk. But at Culloden, the ground was soft and broken, impeding the Highlanders’ momentum. Cumberland’s troops, trained to meet this attack, did not flinch. The front rank fired a volley and the second rank fired through the gaps; then they fixed bayonets and met the Highlanders hand-to-hand, executing the new bayonet drill in which each soldier attacked not the man directly in front of him but the man to his right, where the Highlander’s targe offered no protection.

In less than thirty minutes, the Jacobite army was utterly destroyed. The clans were broken, the survivors fleeing in all directions. Cumberland’s orders — whether explicit or implicit — were to give no quarter. The cavalry pursued the fleeing Highlanders across the moor, cutting down men who had thrown down their arms. Belsham, though generally restrained in his criticism of the government cause, records that “unnecessary and wanton barbarities were committed on the persons and families of the rebels long after the cessation of resistance,” adding that the duke “sullied the glory of his victory by displaying a savage thirst of revenge.” He qualifies this by noting either that Cumberland was “transported into these temporary excesses by that rage which is so frequent and horrid a concomitant of civil discord” or that he genuinely believed such severity necessary to prevent further resistance (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4591–4615).

Aftermath

The Pacification of the Highlands

Culloden was followed by a campaign of systematic brutality that earned the Duke of Cumberland the lasting sobriquet “Butcher Cumberland.” For several months, government troops scoured the Highlands, burning villages, slaughtering livestock, destroying crops, and executing anyone suspected of Jacobite sympathies — often without trial. The policy was deliberate: to make the Highlands incapable of sustaining another rising. The wholesale destruction of the clan lands was an act of state terror that succeeded in its immediate strategic objective but left a legacy of bitterness that persists in Scottish folk memory to this day.

The Disarming and Heritable Jurisdictions Acts

Parliament moved swiftly to dismantle the social and military structure of the Highlands. The Disarming Act of 1746 made it illegal to carry broadswords, dirks, or firearms in the Highlands, and banned the wearing of Highland dress — kilts, tartans, and the belted plaid — which were symbols of clan identity and martial culture. (This ban remained in force until 1782.) The Heritable Jurisdictions Act of 1747 abolished the judicial powers of the clan chiefs — the right to hold courts, administer justice, and call out their tenants for military service — which had been the legal foundation of the clan system. Together, these acts destroyed the hereditary authority of the chiefs and transformed the Gaeltacht from a semi-independent martial society into an integrated — if impoverished — part of the British state.

The abolition of the clan system was swift and total. Clan chiefs, who had once been lords over men, became landlords overseeing sheep. The Highland clearances of the next century would not have been possible without the destruction of the chiefs’ traditional obligations to their people that followed Culloden. Over 1,000 Jacobite prisoners were transported to the colonies; 120 were executed, often after brutal show trials. Among those executed on Tower Hill were the Jacobite peers Lords Kilmarnock, Balmerino, and the aged Simon Fraser, Lord Lovat — the last man in England to be beheaded. Lord Lovat, an inveterate intriguer of notoriously profligate character who had played both sides during the rising, faced the scaffold at the age of eighty with unexpected dignity. Belsham, quoting Sir Dudley Carleton on a similar occasion, observes: “So much easier is it for a man to die well than to live well” (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4635–4654). The Earl of Derwentwater, who had been condemned in 1716, was executed on the same sentence without a new trial; only the Earl of Cromarty received a pardon.

The Escape of Charles Edward Stuart

Charles Edward Stuart escaped from the field of Culloden and spent the next five months as a hunted fugitive in the Highlands and Western Isles, with a price of £30,000 on his head. He moved from hiding place to hiding place, sheltering in caves and bothies, protected by a network of loyal supporters who risked their own lives to keep him safe. The most famous episode of this odyssey involved Flora Macdonald, a young Hebridean woman who accompanied Charles in disguise as her Irish maidservant, “Betty Burke,” on a hazardous crossing from North Uist to the Isle of Skye. After reaching Skye, Charles was passed from safe house to safe house, eluding the systematic searches of government troops.

On 20 September 1746 — five months after Culloden — Charles boarded a French privateer at Moidart and sailed for Morlaix in Brittany. He never returned to Scotland (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4615–4631). The rest of his life was a long, sad Anti-climax: the romantic prince who had captivated the Highlands degenerated into an alcoholic exile, estranged from his family, abandoned by his mistress, and ultimately a pathetic figure in the courts of Europe.

The Fate of the Manchester Rebels

Of the English Jacobites, none suffered more harshly than Francis Towneley and the officers of the Manchester Regiment who had been captured at Carlisle. They were tried for high treason, convicted, and executed at Kennington Common in London. Towneley’s head was displayed on Temple Bar, a grim warning to any Englishman who might contemplate serving the Stuart cause.

Key Figures

Charles Edward Stuart (1720–1788)

The Young Pretender, grandson of James II, was a charismatic but flawed leader. His courage and personal magnetism raised a formidable army from almost nothing, but his strategic judgment was poor, his temperament impatient, and his willingness to listen to his more experienced commanders (notably Lord George Murray) increasingly diminished as the campaign progressed. After the failed rising, he became a tragic figure — the ‘Bonnie Prince Charlie’ of romantic legend bore little resemblance to the sullen, embittered exile he became.

William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland (1721–1765)

The third and favourite son of George II, Cumberland had been the leading British commander in Flanders before the ‘45, where his defeat at Fontenoy in May 1745 had damaged his reputation. His victory at Culloden and the ruthless pacification that followed earned him the lasting title of “Butcher” in Highland memory. He was conspicuously honoured by Parliament, which added £25,000 per annum to his revenue and lauded him as the saviour of the Protestant succession (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 4645–4650).

Lord George Murray (1694–1760)

The military genius of the Jacobite cause. A younger son of the Duke of Atholl and a veteran of the British army, Murray had fought in the Jacobite Rising of 1719 and had been pardoned. He joined Charles in August 1745 and served as lieutenant-general. He planned the manoeuvres that captured Edinburgh, the devastating charge at Prestonpans, and the brilliant retreat from Derby. His disagreements with Charles after the decision to retreat were sharp and permanent; Murray left the army after Falkirk and was not present at Culloden. He later went into exile, dying in Holland in 1760.

Flora Macdonald (1722–1790)

A young woman of Clan Macdonald of Sleat, Flora Macdonald became the most celebrated figure of the Jacobite escape. Her courageous decision to guide the prince in disguise across the Minch to Skye, and her subsequent (brief) imprisonment in the Tower of London, made her a romantic heroine both in Scotland and in England. She eventually married and emigrated to North Carolina, returning to Scotland before her death in 1790.
