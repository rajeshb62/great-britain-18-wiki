Irish Rebellion of 1798

The Irish Rebellion of 1798 (May–September 1798) was the most violent and extensive uprising in Ireland since the Jacobite wars of the previous century. A fusion of Catholic and Presbyterian opposition to the Anglican Ascendancy — organised under the banner of the Society of United Irishmen — it exploded into open insurrection across the counties of Wexford, Antrim, Down, and Kildare, drawing in French revolutionary assistance and threatening the entire British position in Ireland. The rebellion was crushed with exceptional brutality; Belsham records that “upon a moderate computation, more than twenty thousand lives were lost, and a great part of the kingdom exhibited a horrid spectacle of misery and desolation.”

The rebellion was the immediate cause of the Pitt government’s determination to pass the Act of Union 1800, which extinguished the independent Irish Parliament and brought Ireland under direct rule from Westminster — a constitutional revolution that would shape Irish and British politics for the next 120 years.

Background

The Anglican Ascendancy and the Penal Laws

Eighteenth-century Ireland was governed by a narrow Protestant elite — the “Anglican Ascendancy” — who owned the vast majority of the land and monopolised political power through the Irish Parliament in Dublin. The native Catholic majority, comprising perhaps three-quarters of the population, was subjected to the Penal Laws: barred from voting, from sitting in Parliament, from holding public office, from practising law, from bearing arms, and from purchasing land from Protestants. A substantial Presbyterian population in Ulster, descendants of Scottish settlers, was also excluded from full civil rights, though they suffered nothing like the disabilities imposed on Catholics.

By the 1790s, the penal code had been somewhat relaxed — Catholic relief acts in 1778, 1782, and 1792 had removed the worst restrictions on property ownership and worship — but the fundamental structure of Ascendancy rule remained intact. Catholics could neither vote nor sit in Parliament, and the Irish Parliament itself was a corrupt and unrepresentative body, packed with placement and controlled from London through the patronage of the Lord Lieutenant.

The United Irishmen

The Society of United Irishmen was founded in Belfast in October 1791 by Theobald Wolfe Tone, a Dublin-born Protestant barrister of radical sympathies. Tone’s great insight — and it was a revolutionary one in the Irish context — was that the grievances of Catholics and Presbyterians were essentially the same: both were excluded from power by a corrupt Anglican oligarchy that maintained itself through division. As Tone wrote in his pamphlet An Argument on Behalf of the Catholics of Ireland (1791): “To subvert the tyranny of our execrable government, to break the connection with England, the never-failing source of all our political evils, and to assert the independence of my country — these were my objects.”

The United Irishmen drew inspiration from the French Revolution and the writings of Thomas Paine (whose Rights of Man was widely circulated in Ireland). Their early programme was parliamentary reform — the abolition of rotten boroughs, the extension of the franchise, and Catholic emancipation — but as the British government’s response to the Revolution became increasingly repressive, and as the war with France radicalised Irish politics, the movement turned towards armed insurrection and separation from Britain.

By 1795, the United Irishmen had been driven underground. The organisation was restructured along revolutionary lines — a secret oath-bound society, organised in cells on the model of the French Jacobins, with an executive directory at its head. Its membership included both Catholics and Presbyterians (the “Protestant, Catholic, and Dissenter” trinity of Tone’s founding vision), though in practice the Presbyterian element was strongest in Ulster, while the Catholic mass-movement dominated the south.

The French Connection

From the outbreak of war between Britain and Revolutionary France in 1793, the United Irishmen looked to Paris for deliverance. Tone went into exile in the United States in 1795, and from there made his way to France, where he presented himself to the French Directory as the agent of a nation ripe for revolution. His arguments were compelling: Ireland was Britain’s “soft underbelly,” a Catholic nation groaning under Protestant tyranny, whose 4.5 million people would rise en masse at the sight of French bayonets. A French invasion of Ireland, Tone argued, would do more to defeat Britain than a dozen campaigns in Germany or Italy.

The French Directory, eager to strike at Britain after the collapse of the War of the First Coalition, authorised a major expedition under General Lazare Hoche. In December 1796, a fleet of 43 ships carrying some 14,000 troops sailed from Brest. Tone himself sailed with the expedition, as a chef de brigade in the French army.

Hoche’s Expedition and the Bantry Bay Fiasco

The expedition was a disaster — but only because of the weather. The fleet arrived off the south-west coast of Ireland in late December 1796 and entered Bantry Bay, where, Belsham records, one of the frigates “came to anchor, in a very shattered state.” The wind, however, shifted violently to the east, making a landing impossible. For several days the French squadron struggled against the gales; the ships were scattered, the Séduisant was wrecked with the loss of 700 men, and the remnants of the fleet were forced to limp back to Brest. Tone, writing in his journal, recorded his despair: “England has not had such an escape since the Spanish Armada.”

The Bantry Bay failure was a turning point. The British government — and the Irish administration under Lord Camden — now knew that France was serious about Ireland. The response was a campaign of counter-insurgency that, far from pacifying the country, drove it headlong into rebellion. The countryside was flooded with troops, martial law was declared, the militia was quartered on the population, and a campaign of terror — flogging, pitchcapping, half-hanging, the burning of farms — was unleashed against suspected United Irishmen. The most notorious instrument of this repression was the Welsh-born magistrate and yeomanry commander, but the general direction came from Dublin Castle under Camden and the Chief Secretary, Thomas Pelham.

The Road to Rebellion

The Northern Star and the Arrests

Through 1796 and 1797, the United Irishmen grew rapidly in strength. The movement’s newspaper, the Northern Star (published in Belfast), spread radical ideas throughout Ulster. The government struck back: the paper was suppressed in May 1797, the militia and yeomanry were used to disarm the population of Ulster, and suspected leaders were rounded up.

In March 1798, the government’s intelligence network — which had thoroughly penetrated the United Irish organisation — delivered its masterstroke. Acting on information from informers, the Dublin Castle authorities raided the house of Oliver Bond in Dublin and arrested the entire Leinster provincial committee of the United Irishmen, including the revolutionary “Directory” that was supposed to coordinate the rising. Among those taken were Arthur O’Connor, Thomas Addis Emmet, and William James MacNevin. Lord Edward Fitzgerald — the most charismatic and dangerous of the rebel leaders, a younger son of the Duke of Leinster — escaped the initial round-up but was soon hunted down. On 19 May, after a violent struggle in which he killed one of his captors, Fitzgerald was captured; his wounds proved fatal, and he died in Newgate Prison on 4 June.

The arrest of the Directory threw the United Irish leadership into chaos. The rising, originally planned for May, was precipitated in confusion — orders and counter-orders flew across the country, and in many districts the rebellion began not as a coordinated insurrection but as a desperate, leaderless explosion.

The Government’s Position

The British and Irish governments were acutely aware of the danger. In the parliamentary session of 1797–1798, the Opposition had largely seceded from the House of Commons — Belsham notes that “the benches of opposition being in a manner deserted” — leaving Pitt and his ministers with an unopposed majority. The Habeas Corpus Act had been suspended in both Britain and Ireland; the Bank of England had been forced to suspend cash payments; the financial strain of the war was immense. In Ireland, Lord Camden — though personally moderate — presided over an administration that had effectively ceded control to the military, and the brutality of the counter-insurgency was creating a reservoir of bitterness that made rebellion almost inevitable.

The Rebellion

May 1798: The Outbreak

On 23–24 May 1798, the long-anticipated rising finally began. The original plan called for the simultaneous seizure of Dublin and the surrounding counties, but the arrest of the Directory and the death of Fitzgerald had fatally compromised the leadership. In Dublin itself, the rising fizzled; the government had packed the city with troops, and the intended signal — the stopping of the mail coaches — was only partially effected.

In the provinces, however, the rebellion burst forth with terrifying violence. The counties of Kildare, Carlow, Wicklow, and Meath rose in arms. But it was in County Wexford that the rebellion achieved its greatest — and most terrible — success.

Wexford: The Rebellion’s Heart

The rising in Wexford was different in character from the rest. Here, the leadership was predominantly Catholic — priests and farmers, not the Presbyterian radicals of Belfast — and the rebellion took on the character of a religious war. Belsham, drawing on contemporary accounts, paints a lurid picture: “The generality of the priests who appeared openly in this rebellion took the utmost pains to diffuse, as widely as possible, the malignant spirit of religious bigotry and inveterate animosity against the Protestants.”

On 28 May, the rebels carried the town of Enniscorthy “sword in hand.” Two days later, Wexford town surrendered and was occupied. The Protestant inhabitants were imprisoned; three gentlemen of large property, members of the United Irishmen who had been in custody awaiting trial, were liberated, and one of them — Beauchamp Bagenal Harvey — was nominated to the command of the rebel army, “though in no respect qualified for so arduous an office.”

The Battle of New Ross (5 June)

Flushed with success, the rebels marched under Harvey’s nominal command to attack New Ross, where General Johnson commanded with fewer than 3,000 regular troops. The rebels drove a vast herd of horses and oxen before them to disorder the king’s line and attacked with a weight of numbers that forced the troops back into the town. The fighting raged through the streets for eight hours — “a dreadful carnage” — before the rebels were at length compelled to retreat with heavy losses. Lord Mountjoy was killed early in the action, fighting at the head of his regiment.

The defeat at New Ross triggered the worst atrocity of the rebellion.

The Scullabogue Barn Massacre

Enraged by their repulse at New Ross, and by the knowledge that the military had been executing suspected rebels under martial law, the rebel leadership in Wexford resolved on a terrible revenge. Belsham records that “the rebel chiefs condemned a great number of the loyalists of Wexford and Enniscorthy to death — and this sentence was carried into effect a few days after, with circumstances of excessive barbarity.”

The most notorious incident occurred at Scullabogue (or Scollobogue), a barn near the foot of Carrickburn mountain, some miles distant from the battlefield. Here, upwards of two hundred Protestant prisoners — men, women, and children — had been confined under guard. On the orders of Father John Murphy, one of the rebel commanders, the barn was set alight, and all perished “amidst surrounding shouts of savage exultation.”

The atrocity at Scullabogue — and the mass executions that followed — permanently blackened the reputation of the Wexford rising. Belsham, who is generally critical of government policy in Ireland, writes of these events with undisguised horror, describing Murphy as “the bloody bigot” and recording that after the defeat of Vinegar Hill, he was “soon after taken in his flight, and most deservedly hanged.”

The Wexford Bridge Executions

After the recapture of Wexford, the government exacted a terrible retribution. General Lake recorded in his dispatch of 22 June that General Moore had entered the place “so opportunely as to prevent it from being laid in ashes; and, which was still more interesting, to render impracticable the premeditated massacre of the remaining prisoners — eighty-six persons having been murdered by them the preceding day, military music attending and playing a dead march, and their bodies, pierced with pikes, thrown over the bridge.”

Harvey, the rebel general who had “expressed some disapprobation of these enormities,” was stripped of his command after the battle of Ross. He attempted to conceal himself in a cave on one of the rocky islands in Wexford harbour, but was discovered, tried by court-martial, and executed on 26 June on Wexford Bridge, along with several other rebel leaders.

Vinegar Hill (21 June)

By mid-June, General Lake — the most aggressive of the British commanders — had assembled a large force to crush the Wexford insurrection. The main rebel army, numbering 18,000 to 20,000 men, had taken up an extraordinarily strong position on Vinegar Hill, a mile from Enniscorthy. From this eminence, Belsham observes, “had they possessed any share of military skill, it would have been difficult, if not impracticable, to dislodge them.”

On 21 June, Lake launched his attack. Four columns — under Generals Johnson, Dundas, Duff, and Needham — converged on the hill from different directions. The rebels maintained their ground obstinately for an hour and a half, but when they perceived the danger of being surrounded, they “fled with great precipitation” — some to the mountains of Wicklow, others to the chain of hills separating Carlow and Wexford. The pursuit was merciless: “no quarter given,” Belsham records, “they sustained immense loss; while of the king’s troops the whole number of killed, wounded, and prisoners, was something less than a hundred — a surprising proof of the superiority of skill and discipline over mere unenlightened courage.”

The Rebellion in the North (Antrim and Down)

While Wexford burned, the northern Presbyterians rose in arms. On 7 June, the town of Antrim was briefly seized by the rebels under Henry Joy McCracken, but they were driven out after a sharp engagement in which Lord O’Neill received a wound of which he later died. On 12 June, the main northern force — no more than 6,000 or 7,000 men — was “attacked and totally defeated at Ballynahinch” by General Nugent. A party of rebels was also repulsed at Carrickfergus. Within a short time, the general body of insurgents in the north laid down their arms, and “the tranquillity of the province of Ulster was restored.”

The Suppression

Lord Cornwallis Appointed

The rebellion had been largely crushed by the time the government’s response changed character, but the change was decisive. In Dublin, it was judged that Lord Camden had lost control of the situation; what was needed was a military commander of the highest rank who could at once pacify the country and restrain the orgy of execution and reprisal that the local authorities were conducting. The man chosen was Charles Cornwallis, 1st Marquess Cornwallis — the same Cornwallis who had surrendered at Yorktown in 1781, but whose subsequent career as Governor-General of India had restored his reputation for ability and moderation.

Cornwallis arrived in Dublin on 20 June. Under his auspices, Belsham records, “the general system of government immediately changed to that of moderation and lenity.” A general amnesty was proclaimed; the state prisoners — including O’Connor, Emmet, and MacNevin — were allowed to transport themselves to any country not at war with Britain; and the systematic executions that had followed the suppression of the Wexford rising were brought under control.

Nevertheless, some severe examples were deemed necessary. A special commission was opened in Dublin for the trial of the principal conspirators. The brothers John and Henry Sheares were tried and executed; William Michael McCann, secretary to the provincial meeting, and O’Byrne, a noted member of the United Association, suffered the same fate. Oliver Bond was convicted on 23 July and condemned, though he died in prison before the sentence could be carried out — or, as some accounts have it, the government offered to commute his sentence to banishment if he would betray his associates, a proposition he “nobly rejected.”

March of the Rebels to Wicklow

After Vinegar Hill, most of the rebel corps that had retreated to the mountainous parts of Wexford and Wicklow took the benefit of the amnesty and laid down their arms. Those who still resisted, Belsham notes, “were rather banditti, who confined themselves to nocturnal depredations, than troops in arms against the government.”

The French Landings

Humbert at Killala (August 1798)

The rebellion had been suppressed before any French assistance arrived — but the French came anyway, too little and far too late. On 22 August, three weeks after the Wexford rising had been crushed, a small French squadron appeared off the coast of Connaught and cast anchor in Killala Bay. The force — a mere 1,100 men under General Jean Joseph Amable Humbert — was part of a much larger expedition that had been scattered or delayed.

Humbert’s men landed unopposed and occupied the bishop’s palace at Killala. A green flag was raised over the gate with the inscription “ERIN GO BRAGH!” — “Ireland for ever!” The French troops, according to the bishop of Killala (Dr. Stock), whose published Narrative Belsham cites, “conducted themselves with the most exemplary moderation.” They were soon joined by several thousand of the Irish peasantry, all Catholics. Humbert and his officers expressed “the utmost chagrin and surprize” that the Protestants “continued, to a man, firm in their attachment to the government.”

The “Races of Castlebar”

Humbert marched on Castlebar, the chief town of County Mayo. General Lake — the same who had crushed the Wexford rising — was posted there with a force far superior to the French. But on 27 August, the French attacked by surprise, and the British troops, after a resistance that Belsham diplomatically describes as “not very vigorous,” were compelled to retreat with the loss of 800 men and ten pieces of cannon. The engagement became known, with bitter Irish irony, as the “Races of Castlebar” — a reference to the speed of the British flight.

Castlebar surrendered, and the French were joined by deserters from the Irish militia regiments. For a few days, it seemed possible that the west of Ireland might become a second Wexford.

Ballinamuck (8 September)

But Cornwallis was now in command, and he acted with decisive energy. Collecting a great army, he moved to enclose the French on all sides. Humbert, who had from the first entertained no hope of ultimate success, began a circuitous retreat, allowing the Irish recruits to escape — an opportunity “the far greater number embraced.”

On the morning of 8 September 1798, the British van came up with the French rearguard at Ballinamuck in County Longford. After a short but gallant resistance, the French were summoned to surrender and laid down their arms. The Irish who remained with them — perhaps a few hundred — fled in all directions and “suffered very severely.” When the return of French prisoners was made, the public was astonished to find that “this formidable host amounted to no more, officers included, than the insignificant number of 844.”

The garrison left at Killala — French troops under Colonel Charost — defended the place with great bravery but was overwhelmed. Charost’s conduct was “marked throughout by valor, tempered with honor and humanity.” When the bishop’s palace was recaptured, “not a single article of private property was found missing.”

Sir John Borlase Warren’s Victory (October 1798)

A second French expedition, sailing from Brest in October, met an even worse fate. The squadron — consisting of the Hoche (80 guns) and eight frigates — was intercepted off the coast of Ulster by a superior British force under Sir John Borlase Warren. On the morning of 12 October, the action commenced; by eleven o’clock, the Hoche had struck her flag, and six of the frigates were captured. Among the prisoners taken in the Hoche was Theobald Wolfe Tone.

The Trial and Death of Wolfe Tone

Tone was brought to Dublin and tried by court-martial. He did not attempt to deny or extenuate his offence. Belsham records his speech — one of the most famous in Irish history — in full:

“Into the service of the French republic I originally entered with the view of serving my country. From that motive I have encountered the toils and terrors of the field of battle; I have braved the dangers of the sea, covered with the triumphant fleets of the power I opposed; I have sacrificed my prospects in life; I have courted poverty; I have left my wife unprotected, and my children fatherless. After doing this, for what I thought a good cause, it is but little that I die for it. In such a cause as this success is every thing. I have attempted that in which Washington succeeded and Kosciusko failed. What awaits me I am aware of, but I scorn to supplicate or to complain.”

Belsham adds: “After a long pause, no doubt of involuntary admiration and regret, he was informed that his request would be submitted to his excellency.” Tone, who had been promised a soldier’s death — death by firing squad, rather than the degradation of hanging — found that the government was unlikely to grant his request. On 19 November 1798, he died in prison; according to the accepted account, he cut his own throat with a razor. “As this extraordinary man was the original author,” Belsham concludes, “his trial might be considered also as the last concluding scene of this short but bloody rebellion.”

Atrocities and the Character of the Rebellion

The Irish Rebellion of 1798 was marked by atrocities on both sides. The rebels massacred Protestant prisoners at Scullabogue and at Wexford Bridge; they tortured and killed in the name of religion, urged on by priests like Murphy. The government, for its part, conducted a campaign of counter-insurgency terror before the rebellion — burning, flogging, pitchcapping — and, after the suppression, executed thousands of suspected rebels under martial law, often without anything resembling a fair trial.

Belsham, writing within a few years of the events, offers a Whig — and morally complex — judgement. He condemns the atrocities of the rebels in the strongest terms, but he also condemns the government that drove them to rebellion: “Thus was the kingdom of Ireland once more reduced to peaceable subjection to the crown of Great Britain, and a dangerous rebellion crushed, which, had the government of the country been previously actuated by the beneficent spirit of the nobleman who now filled the highest office in it, would never have existed.”

He quotes Samuel Johnson’s Journey to the Western Islands: “To hinder insurrection by driving away the people, and to govern peaceably by having no subjects, is an expedient that argues no great profundity in politics: to soften the obdurate, to convince the mistaken, to mollify the resentful, are worthy of a statesman; but it affords a legislator little self-applause to consider that where there was formerly an insurrection there is now a wilderness.”

The Political Consequences: The Act of Union

The rebellion convinced William Pitt the Younger that the existing constitutional relationship between Britain and Ireland was unsustainable. The Irish Parliament — the corrupt and exclusively Protestant Parliament in Dublin — had manifestly failed to govern Ireland; the Ascendancy had proved itself incapable of either conciliating the Catholic population or defending itself without British military assistance. Pitt’s solution was the complete legislative union of the two kingdoms: the abolition of the Irish Parliament and the representation of Ireland at Westminster.

The Union was Pitt’s own measure, driven through against fierce opposition in both countries. In Ireland, the brilliant orator Henry Grattan — who had won legislative independence for Ireland in 1782 — led the opposition, denouncing the Union as the betrayal of Irish nationhood. But Pitt offered irresistible inducements: peerages, pensions, and cash payments to the Irish borough-owners whose seats would be abolished, and the promise of Catholic emancipation (the removal of the remaining civil disabilities on Catholics) once the Union was accomplished.

The Act of Union received the royal assent in July 1800 and came into effect on 1 January 1801, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. The promise of Catholic emancipation — which had been the bait that reconciled many Irish Catholics to the Union — was betrayed when George III refused his consent, believing that his coronation oath forbade it. Pitt resigned in consequence, though he returned to power in 1804. The failure to deliver emancipation poisoned the Union from its birth and ensured that the “Irish Question” would remain the most persistent problem in British politics for the next 120 years.

Key Figures

  • Theobald Wolfe Tone (1763–1798) — Founder of the United Irishmen, architect of the French-Irish alliance, captured on the Hoche and died by suicide in prison. The founding father of Irish republican nationalism.
  • William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) — British Prime Minister, who drove the Act of Union through Parliament in response to the rebellion.
  • Charles Cornwallis, 1st Marquess Cornwallis (1738–1805) — Lord Lieutenant of Ireland from June 1798, whose policy of “moderation and lenity” after the initial suppression prevented further bloodshed.
  • General Gerard Lake (1744–1808) — Commander of the forces that crushed the Wexford rising at Vinegar Hill; notorious for the brutality of his counter-insurgency tactics before the rebellion.
  • Lord Edward Fitzgerald (1763–1798) — United Irish leader, captured and mortally wounded in May 1798, died in Newgate Prison.
  • General Jean Joseph Amable Humbert (1767–1823) — Commander of the French expedition that landed at Killala; defeated at Ballinamuck.
  • Father John Murphy (c. 1753–1798) — Rebel priest, commander at the Scullabogue massacre, hanged after Vinegar Hill.
  • Henry Joy McCracken (1767–1798) — Presbyterian leader of the rebellion in Antrim; executed after the defeat at Ballynahinch.
  • Beauchamp Bagenal Harvey (1762–1798) — United Irishman and nominal commander of the Wexford rebels; executed on Wexford Bridge.
  • Sir John Borlase Warren (1753–1822) — British admiral who intercepted the second French expedition and captured Tone.
