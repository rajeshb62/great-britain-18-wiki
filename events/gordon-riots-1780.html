The Gordon Riots (2–9 June 1780)

The Gordon Riots were the most destructive civil disorder in the history of London — six days of arson, looting, prison-breaking, and mob violence that brought the capital of the British Empire to the brink of anarchy in the summer of 1780. What began as a mass Protestant petition against Catholic relief quickly escalated into a conflagration that destroyed Catholic chapels, private homes, the great prison of Newgate, the King’s Bench, the Bank of England, and dozens of other buildings, before the army — deployed on the personal order of George III — suppressed the rising with indiscriminate volleys that left an estimated 500 rioters dead in the streets.

The riots erupted at one of the most vulnerable moments in the American War of Independence, when Britain was fighting a global war against the American rebels, France, and Spain, and the ministry of Lord North was already reeling from the parliamentary censure of Dunning’s resolution. The Gordon Riots exposed the fragility of civil order in the unreformed metropolis and the depth of anti-Catholic sentiment that still animated the Protestant common people of England — a sentiment that the ruling classes had fatally underestimated.

Background: The Catholic Relief Act (1778)

The proximate cause of the riots was the Catholic Relief Act of 1778 (18 Geo. III, c. 60), a modest measure that removed some of the most oppressive penal laws against Roman Catholics in England and Wales. The Act was sponsored by Sir George Savile, a Yorkshire MP of liberal Whig principles, and supported by Edmund Burke, who had long argued that the penal laws against Catholics were not only cruel but counterproductive — they drove Catholic subjects into disaffection and deprived the state of the services of loyal men.

The penal laws had been enacted in the aftermath of the Gunpowder Plot and the alleged Popish Plot of the late seventeenth century, and they subjected Catholics to a regime of civil disabilities: they could not inherit or purchase land, could not practise law, could not bear arms, could not hold public office, and were subject to double taxation. The 1778 Act gave Catholics a limited relief: it allowed them to inherit and purchase land, and it removed the penalty of life imprisonment for Catholic priests who celebrated Mass. It did not grant Catholics the right to vote, sit in Parliament, or hold public office — it was, by any measure, a cautious and conservative reform.

The motive for the Act was not humanitarian sentiment but military necessity. Britain was fighting the American War of Independence and needed men. The ancient enemy — France — had entered the war on the American side in 1778, and Spain followed in 1779. The government calculated that the Catholic population of Britain and Ireland, if conciliated, could provide a valuable source of recruits for the army and navy. The Act was partly inspired by the Quebec Act of 1774, which had granted religious freedom to Canadian Catholics — a measure that had itself provoked furious Protestant opposition in Britain and America.

A separate Catholic Relief Act was passed for Ireland in 1778, and together the two Acts set in motion the slow, halting process of Catholic Emancipation that would occupy British politics for the next half-century. But in the short term, they provoked a violent Protestant backlash — and the most charismatic and reckless of its leaders was Lord George Gordon.

Lord George Gordon and the Protestant Association

Lord George Gordon (1751–1793) was the third and youngest son of the 3rd Duke of Gordon, a Scottish nobleman. He had entered Parliament in 1774 as MP for Ludgershall, a pocket borough in Wiltshire, but his political career had been restive and erratic. He was a man of intense, almost fanatical, religious conviction — a convert to a severe Calvinist Protestantism that saw the Catholic Church as the Whore of Babylon and the Pope as Antichrist. He combined this religious fervour with a genuine, if disordered, sympathy for the poor and a talent for popular agitation that made him a dangerous figure in the volatile politics of London.

In 1779, Gordon founded the Protestant Association, an organisation dedicated to securing the repeal of the Catholic Relief Act. The Association was modelled on the extra-parliamentary associations that had sprung up during the Wilkes affair and the county reform movement — it organised public meetings, distributed pamphlets, and collected signatures for a mass petition to Parliament. Its appeal was not limited to the lunatic fringe; many respectable Protestant clergymen, merchants, and gentlemen joined or supported it, alarmed by what they saw as the first step toward the legalisation of popery in England.

The Association’s campaign drew on the deep well of anti-Catholic sentiment that had been a constant of English popular culture since the Reformation. The memory of the Gunpowder Plot, the fires of Smithfield under Mary I, the Popish Plot of 1678, and the Jacobite risings of 1715 and 1745 — in which Catholic Highlanders had marched on London — were still vivid in the Protestant imagination. The Catholic Relief Act seemed to many ordinary Protestants a betrayal of the Revolution Settlement of 1688, which had established the Protestant succession and excluded Catholics from power. The Quebec Act, which had granted toleration to Canadian Catholics, was already a grievance; the 1778 Act brought the danger home.

By the spring of 1780, the Protestant Association claimed to have gathered 120,000 signatures on its petition for the repeal of the Act — an enormous number in a population of perhaps six million in England and Wales. Gordon, by now the darling of the Protestant mob, resolved to present the petition to Parliament in person, accompanied by a mass demonstration of his supporters. The date was set for Friday, 2 June 1780.

The March on Parliament (2 June 1780)

On the morning of 2 June 1780, Lord George Gordon presided over a rally of the Protestant Association in St. George’s Fields, Southwark — the same ground where eleven years earlier the Massacre of St. George’s Fields had marked the escalation of the Wilkes affair. The crowd that assembled was immense. Estimates vary, but Gordon’s supporters numbered between 40,000 and 60,000 — a vast, disciplined, and ostensibly peaceful gathering of men and women wearing blue cockades, the badge of the Protestant Association, and marching under banners that read “No Popery” and “God Save the King.”

The march to Westminster was, in its first phase, orderly. Gordon, wearing a blue cockade in his hat, led the procession across London Bridge and through the streets of the City toward the Houses of Parliament. The crowd filled the approaches to Westminster Hall, the Palace of Westminster, and the streets around. Members of Parliament attempting to enter the House were jostled, threatened, and in some cases physically assaulted by the crowd. Bishops — whose support for Catholic relief was presumed — were singled out for special abuse. The Bishop of Lichfield was dragged from his coach and forced to take refuge in a nearby house; the Bishop of Lincoln was roughly handled; the Bishop of London was assailed with cries of “No Popery!” and pelted with mud.

Inside the House of Commons, Gordon presented the petition. The House was full, the atmosphere electric with tension. The debate that followed was one of the most extraordinary in parliamentary history, conducted to the sound of the mob’s cries through the windows and the crash of breaking glass. Edmund Burke — who had supported the Catholic Relief Act and who spoke powerfully against the repeal — was recognised and marked by the crowd for vengeance.

The House adjourned — but the crowd did not disperse. As evening fell, the discipline that had marked the day’s march collapsed. The mob, swollen by apprentices, labourers, thieves, and the volatile poor of London’s slums, turned from demonstration to destruction.

The Six Days of Riot

Friday 2 June — The First Outbreak

The violence began late on the afternoon of Friday 2 June. The first targets were the Catholic chapels attached to the embassies of Sardinia and Bavaria in Lincoln’s Inn Fields and Warwick Street. These were protected by diplomatic privilege — technically foreign soil — but the mob gave no heed to the niceties of international law. Both chapels were sacked, gutted by fire, and reduced to shells. The furnishings — altars, statues, vestments, and sacred vessels — were dragged into the street and burned in great bonfires. The spectacle of the burning Catholic images, watched by tens of thousands, was a deliberate echo of the fires of the Reformation.

From the chapels, the mob turned to the houses of known Catholics and of those who had supported the Relief Act. The home of the Catholic bookseller Thomas Parker was ransacked. The house of Sir George Savile — the author of the Act — was attacked but successfully defended by the sheriff and a party of constables. The mob, however, was not yet at full strength. The worst was to come.

Saturday 3 June — The Fires Spread

Saturday saw the violence spread across the city. The mob, now organised into bands that moved through the streets with terrifying efficiency, systematically attacked Catholic homes and businesses. The rich Catholic merchants of the West End, the Irish labourers of St. Giles, the French and Italian craftsmen of Soho — all were targets. The cry “No Popery!” was the signal; a blue cockade pinned to a door or a chalk mark on a lintel was sometimes enough to save a house from destruction — but those without the mark were looted and burned.

The Lord Mayor of London, Brackley Kennett, was conspicuously inactive. When called upon to read the Riot Act and call out the militia, he refused, or was too terrified to act. The City magistrates, intimidated by the mob, did nothing. The civil power in London had effectively collapsed.

Sunday 4 June — The King’s Birthday and the Prisons

Sunday 4 June was the King’s birthday. George III was at the Queen’s House (later Buckingham House) with his family, and the day passed quietly at court — but across London the riots were entering a new phase. The mob, which had so far confined itself to Catholic property, now turned on the prisons.

The first and most spectacular target was Newgate Prison, the great gaol at the western edge of the City, a symbol of arbitrary power and the brutality of the old criminal law. The mob attacked the prison with sledgehammers, crowbars, and fire. The gaolers, hopelessly outnumbered, fled. The gates were forced, the cells were opened, and the prisoners — some 300 men and women, felons and debtors, the innocent and the guilty — were released into the streets. Newgate was then set alight, and the great gothic edifice, with its famous iron gates, burned through the night.

The example set by Newgate was followed at every prison in London. The King’s Bench Prison in Southwark — where John Wilkes had once been confined — was stormed and burned, its prisoners released. The Fleet Prison, the Bridewell, and the Clerkenwell House of Correction were all attacked and opened. By the end of Sunday, every major prison in London except the Tower was either destroyed or evacuated. At least 1,000 prisoners were at large in the capital.

The destruction of the prisons was a turning point. The character of the riot changed. What had begun as a Protestant demonstration against Catholic relief became a general assault on property and authority. Thieves, desperadoes, and the desperate poor joined the mob, and the motives shifted from religious zeal to criminal opportunism. The Gordon Riots were now a proto-insurrection.

Monday 5 June — The Distillery Fire and the Looting of London

Monday 5 June saw some of the most horrifying scenes of the riots. A distillery in Holborn, belonging to a Mr. Langdale — a Catholic — was broken into by the mob. The rioters, instead of burning the place, tapped the vats of gin and spirits. The alcohol flowed into the streets — a river of gin. Men, women, and children drank from the gutters, scooped the liquor in their hats and hands, and fought over the flow. Dozens died of alcohol poisoning that night — estimates range from twenty to fifty — and others drank themselves into a stupor and drowned in the flood of spirits. The distillery was then set alight, and the flames spread to the surrounding houses. The whole of Holborn seemed to be on fire.

Across London, the looting continued unabated. Shops were broken into, houses stripped, and the goods carried off for sale in the markets of the East End. The mob had grown to a size that overwhelmed any possibility of civil resistance. The magistrates had fled. The constables had been beaten back. The military had not yet been deployed in force.

Tuesday 6 June — The Bank of England Attacked

By Tuesday 6 June, the mob had turned its attention to the great institutions of the state. The most audacious attack of the whole six days was attempted against the Bank of England — the financial heart of the British Empire, the repository of the government’s credit and the savings of the mercantile class. A large body of rioters advanced on the Bank in Threadneedle Street, intending to burn it to the ground.

The Bank was defended by a scratch force: the clerks, the armed servants of the Bank, and a detachment of soldiers hastily assembled by the authorities. The mob attempted to force the gates, but they were met with a volley of musket fire. The soldiers, commanded by Alderman John Wilkes — now the City Chamberlain — held their ground. Wilkes, the former radical who had once been the darling of the London mob, had now become the defender of property against popular violence. He gave the order to fire, and the crowd was driven back. The Bank was saved.

The paradox was not lost on contemporaries. Wilkes, who had made his political career by riding the wave of popular agitation, now stood with musket in hand against the same social forces. The cry of “Wilkes and Liberty!” had given way to the defence of property against the mob.

Wednesday 7 June — The Surrender of Gordon

On Wednesday 7 June, Lord George Gordon surrendered himself to the authorities. He had spent the preceding days at the head of the Protestant Association, issuing proclamations calling for order — but his influence over the mob was by now exhausted. The rioters no longer listened to him; the violence had acquired its own momentum. Gordon was arrested and committed to the Tower of London, charged with high treason.

His arrest did nothing to stop the rioting. The worst night was yet to come.

Wednesday night / Thursday morning 7–8 June — The Military Suppression

The government had been slow to act. For four days, the ministry of Lord North had hesitated, uncertain whether the civil power could restore order without the army, reluctant to call in the troops for fear of inflaming the situation further. The military commanders in London — Lord Amherst, the Commander-in-Chief — were constrained by the legal niceties that required a magistrate to read the Riot Act before troops could fire.

It was George III who broke the paralysis. The King, who had been kept informed of events throughout the riots, insisted on the deployment of the army. His response to the hesitations of his ministers was characteristically blunt: he declared that if the magistrates would not act, the military must act without them. There would be no negotiation with the mob.

On the night of Wednesday 7 June and into the early hours of Thursday, the army moved decisively. Troops of the Household Cavalry, foot guards, and militia patrolled the streets in force. They were given orders to shoot anyone engaged in rioting, arson, or looting. The King himself reviewed the troops, a dramatic gesture that became legendary.

The results were devastating. The soldiers fired volleys into the crowds. The streets of London were cleared by the bayonet. Bodies lay in heaps in the gutters — men, women, and children, many of them innocent onlookers caught in the indiscriminate fire. The exact death toll is unknown, but the best estimate is approximately 500 killed, with many hundreds more wounded. Some were shot by the troops; others died in the fires; others were trampled in the panics and stampedes of the suppression.

By dawn on Thursday 8 June, the riots were effectively over. The streets were quiet, littered with rubble, broken glass, and the dead. The great bonfires had burned down. London was a city of smouldering ruins.

Aftermath

The Destruction

The physical damage to London was enormous. The following had been destroyed or severely damaged:

  • Newgate Prison — burned to the ground
  • The King’s Bench Prison — burned
  • The Fleet Prison — burned
  • The Clerkenwell House of Correction — burned
  • The Bridewell — damaged
  • The Sardinian Embassy Chapel — gutted
  • The Bavarian Embassy Chapel — gutted
  • Langdale’s Distillery — destroyed by fire (the most destructive single fire, consuming several neighbouring houses)
  • Over 100 private houses — burned, including many belonging to Catholics and to supporters of the Relief Act
  • The Bank of England — attacked but saved
  • The Temple Bar — attacked but not destroyed

The total cost of the damage was estimated at £100,000 — an enormous sum in 1780, equivalent to many millions in modern currency.

The Trial of Lord George Gordon

Lord George Gordon was committed to the Tower on charges of high treason. He remained in confinement for eight months while the government prepared its case. His trial — presided over by Lord Mansfield, the Lord Chief Justice — was one of the great state trials of the eighteenth century.

Gordon’s defence was conducted by the brilliant lawyer Thomas Erskine (later Lord Chancellor Erskine), who argued that Gordon had not intended the riots and had in fact attempted to restrain them once the violence began. The Crown’s case was that by assembling the mob and presenting the petition in a manner calculated to intimidate Parliament, Gordon had incited the riot and was therefore guilty of treason.

The jury acquitted him. The verdict was a shock to the government and a vindication of the right of popular petition — but it was also, in retrospect, a recognition that Gordon, for all his recklessness, had not intended the catastrophe that followed. He was released, though his political career was effectively over. He later converted to Judaism — a bizarre coda to the career of the man who had led the most destructive anti-Catholic riot in English history — and died in Newgate Prison in 1793, having been convicted of libel for attacking the French Revolution.

The Political Consequences

The Gordon Riots had profound political consequences. They demonstrated, first and most obviously, the volatility of anti-Catholic sentiment in eighteenth-century England. The Protestant majority had shown, in the most violent terms, that the penal laws against Catholics were not merely dead letters — they were garrisoned in the hearts of the people. Any attempt to relieve the disabilities of Catholics would have to contend with the visceral hostility of the Protestant mob.

The riots also exposed the weakness of civil order in London. The magistrates had failed; the Lord Mayor had been useless; the police — such as they were — had been overwhelmed. The only force that could restore order was the army, and the use of the army against civilians raised uncomfortable questions about the nature of the British state. The Gordon Riots were the first time that the army had been deployed in force against a London crowd since the days of the Stuarts, and the precedent was troubling.

Politically, the riots weakened the North ministry, which was already struggling with the American war. The government’s slowness in responding to the crisis — four days of hesitation — was widely criticised. The King’s decisive intervention, by contrast, enhanced his popularity among the propertied classes, who saw him as the defender of order against the mob. The cry of “God Save the King!” replaced “No Popery!” in the streets of London.

The riots also had a chilling effect on reform. The county reform movement and the Economic Reform campaign, which had been gaining momentum in 1779 and early 1780, were set back by the riots. The association of popular agitation with mob violence made reform suspect in the eyes of the propertied classes. Edmund Burke’s plans for economical reform — the reduction of the influence of the Crown — had to contend with the argument that any weakening of the executive would leave the state defenceless against popular violence.

Catholic Relief in the Longer Term

The Gordon Riots delayed but did not halt the progress of Catholic relief. The cause was set back for a generation: any politician who proposed further concessions to Catholics knew that he risked provoking a repeat of 1780. It was not until 1829, after the heroic campaign of Daniel O’Connell and the threat of civil war in Ireland, that the Catholic Emancipation Act was finally passed — and even then, it was passed over the bitter opposition of the Protestant mob and the Tory party.

Key Figures

Lord George Gordon (1751–1793)

The central figure of the crisis — a Scottish lord, MP, and religious enthusiast who combined Protestant zealotry with a genuine sympathy for the poor. Gordon was not a cynical demagogue; he genuinely believed that the Catholic Relief Act was a betrayal of the Protestant constitution and that the Pope was Antichrist. His fatal flaw was that he did not understand — or did not care — that the forces he unleashed could not be controlled. He was acquitted of treason but lived the rest of his life in political obscurity, dying in the same prison he had helped to empty.

George III (1738–1820)

The King whose decisive intervention broke the riots. George III was the first member of the royal family to insist on military force — when the civil authorities faltered, the King gave the order to shoot. His conduct during the crisis enhanced his popularity among the propertied classes, who saw him as the defender of order. The Gordon Riots marked a turning point in the King’s relationship with his people: he was no longer the target of Wilkesite agitation but the symbol of resistance to anarchy.

Lord North (Frederick North, 2nd Earl of Guilford, 1732–1792)

The Prime Minister during the crisis. North’s government was already reeling from the disasters of the American War of Independence and the parliamentary censure of Dunning’s resolution (April 1780). His handling of the Gordon Riots was cautious to the point of paralysis: he hesitated for four days before authorising the military suppression, and he was overtaken by the King’s more decisive intervention. The riots further weakened his ministry, which survived only until March 1782.

John Wilkes (1725–1797)

The City Chamberlain of London during the riots — a remarkable reversal of roles for the man who had once been the most famous radical in Britain. Wilkes commanded the defence of the Bank of England, organising the clerks and soldiers who repelled the mob. He had completed the journey from popular tribune to defender of property. His conduct during the riots earned him the gratitude of the government and the establishment, though it cost him some of his popular following. He is remembered for giving the order to fire on the crowd he had once led.

Edmund Burke (1729–1797)

The great Whig philosopher-politician, who had supported the Catholic Relief Act of 1778 and spoke powerfully against the Protestant Association’s petition during the debate of 2 June. Burke was one of the few politicians of the first rank to defend Catholic relief against the popular tide, and his house was attacked by the mob. His support for Catholic emancipation was consistent throughout his career: he argued that religious persecution was incompatible with the principles of the Glorious Revolution and that the penal laws against Catholics were a relic of a darker age. He later supported the Catholic Relief Act of 1793 and the Irish Catholic cause.

The Riots in Historical Perspective

The Gordon Riots were the worst urban disorder in eighteenth-century Britain — far surpassing the Sacheverell riots of 1710, the excise riots of 1733, or the Wilkesite disturbances of the 1760s in scale, destructiveness, and loss of life. They were, in the words of one historian, “the nearest Britain ever came to a revolution from below” — a spontaneous rising of the London poor against property and authority, cloaked in the respectable language of Protestantism.

The riots also occupy a peculiar place in British historical memory. They are not, like the Wilkes affair or the Popish Plot, part of the Whig narrative of the advance of liberty: they were a reactionary movement, a defence of the old order of Protestant supremacy against the new spirit of toleration. They have no heroes — Lord George Gordon was a fanatic, the mob was destructive, the military response was brutal — and they left no positive legacy. But they mattered profoundly: they showed the limits of religious toleration in eighteenth-century Britain, the fragility of the civil order in the unreformed metropolis, and the enduring power of anti-Catholic sentiment in the English popular imagination.

“Never was such a consternation, such an alarm, in the city of London. The whole capital seemed to be in a flame.” — William Belsham, Memoirs of the Reign of George III

“The mob is the people — a terrible compound of ignorance, passion, and prejudice.” — Attributed to Edmund Burke
