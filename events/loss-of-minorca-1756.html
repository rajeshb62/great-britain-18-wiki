Loss of Minorca and Execution of Admiral Byng (1756–1757)

The Loss of Minorca in 1756 and the subsequent execution of Admiral John Byng in 1757 form one of the most controversial and consequential episodes in the history of the Royal Navy. The failure of a British relief expedition to save the Mediterranean island of Minorca — a British possession since the Treaty of Utrecht — from a French invasion ignited a political firestorm in Britain that cost the Newcastle ministry its majority, and culminated in the judicial execution of a British admiral by firing squad on the quarterdeck of his own flagship. The affair became proverbial for harsh military justice when Voltaire wrote that the British “shoot an admiral from time to time to encourage the others.”

Background

The Diplomatic Revolution

The Seven Years’ War (1756–1763) was preceded by a sweeping realignment of European alliances known as the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756. The traditional enmity between Bourbon France and Habsburg Austria — which had structured European diplomacy for nearly two centuries — gave way to a startling new configuration: France and Austria became allies, while Great Britain formed a defensive pact with Prussia (the Treaty of Westminster, January 1756). The rapprochement between France and Austria, sealed by the First Treaty of Versailles (May 1756), freed France to concentrate her military resources against Britain and Prussia on a global scale (Belsham, Vol. IV, lines 4877–4900).

In the Mediterranean, this meant that Minorca, which Britain had held since its capture in 1708 and formal cession at Utrecht in 1713, became a prime French target. Minorca’s superb natural harbour at Port Mahon was Britain’s principal naval base in the Mediterranean — the key to controlling the western basin, commanding the sea lanes between Gibraltar, the Italian states, and the Levant. Its loss would sever Britain’s Mediterranean communications and gravely weaken her strategic position.

The French Expedition

In the winter of 1755–1756, the French court resolved to strike at Minorca before Britain could reinforce it. A powerful expeditionary force, assembled at Toulon, was placed under the command of Marshal the Duke of Richelieu, one of the most celebrated soldiers of his age and a grand-nephew of Cardinal Richelieu. The naval escort was entrusted to Lieutenant-General Roland-Michel Barrin de la Galissonière, a seasoned naval commander who had served with distinction in Canada.

On 10 April 1756, Richelieu’s transports, escorted by twelve ships of the line under de la Galissonière, landed some 15,000 French troops on the coast of Minorca. The island’s small British garrison, commanded by the aged General William Blakeney, fell back before the overwhelming French force and concentrated in the fortress of Fort St. Philip at the mouth of Port Mahon harbour. Richelieu immediately laid siege to the fort, establishing batteries that began a systematic bombardment (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 148–153).

Byng’s Expedition

The Decision to Sail

The British Admiralty had been slow to appreciate the danger. Although intelligence of French preparations in Toulon had reached London as early as January 1756, the administration of the Duke of Newcastle — notoriously indecisive — dithered over the response. Only in March did the Admiralty order a squadron to the Mediterranean, and the command was given to Admiral John Byng, a man whose courage and abilities were as yet untried in independent command (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 137–140).

Byng was the son of George Byng, 1st Viscount Torrington, the victor of the Battle of Cape Passaro (1718) and one of the most celebrated admirals of the previous generation. The younger Byng had risen through the Navy on the strength of his family name and respectable prior service, but he had never commanded a fleet in action. His appointment reflected the peacetime habit of giving important commands to well-connected officers rather than to those proven in war.

Byng was ordered to sail with ten ships of the line and a small transport carrying a battalion of the English fuzileers (the 60th Regiment of Foot), together with a number of officers and recruits destined for the Minorca garrison. His instructions from the Admiralty were emphatic: he was to relieve Minorca “at all events,” using every possible exertion to throw succour into Fort St. Philip and, if necessary, to engage the French fleet (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 206–210).

Gibraltar

Byng reached Gibraltar on 2 May 1756. There he received alarming intelligence from Captain Edgecombe of the Louisa, who had been driven from Minorca by de la Galissonière’s squadron: the French had already landed fifteen thousand men, the siege of Fort St. Philip was well advanced, and the French fleet of thirteen ships of the line commanded the waters around the island (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 148–153).

At Gibraltar, Byng encountered a bureaucratic tangle that fatally delayed his operations. General Thomas Fowke, the governor of Gibraltar, had received three contradictory letters from the War Office concerning the battalion destined for Minorca. The first ordered the fuzileers to remain at Gibraltar; the second — contradicting the first — ordered a battalion to be embarked for Minorca; the third concerned only the embarkation of women and children. At a council of war held at Gibraltar, Byng and Fowke attempted to reconcile these conflicting orders. The council resolved to embark only a small detachment — one captain, six subalterns, five drums, and two hundred and thirty-five privates — far short of the full battalion originally intended. The engineers at Gibraltar also expressed the opinion that throwing succours into Fort St. Philip would be “extremely difficult, if not impossible” (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 156–210).

Campbell’s narrative is unequivocal on this point: “This resolution of the council of war was certainly wrong.” The council had been overcautious, and Byng had acquiesced in a decision that fatally reduced his capacity to reinforce the garrison (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 181–186).

Byng sailed from Gibraltar on 8 May with his squadron still not fully manned and with insufficient troops to make a decisive difference.

The Battle of Minorca (20 May 1756)

The Fleets Sight Each Other

Byng made the coast of Minorca on the morning of 19 May. To his relief, the British flag still flew over the castle of Fort St. Philip, though he could see several French bomb-batteries playing upon it from the enemy’s works. He despatched Captain Hervey in the Phoenix, together with the Chesterfield and Dolphin, to reconnoitre the entrance to the harbour and, if possible, to convey a letter to General Blakeney. Hervey got round the harbour before nine o’clock and made signals for a boat, but none came off — the garrison, apparently, could not spare a boat under the intensity of the bombardment. Shortly afterward, Byng’s lookouts sighted the French fleet to the south-east, and he recalled Hervey (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 215–247).

Byng stood toward the enemy. About two in the afternoon, he made the signal for the line of battle ahead, distributed seamen from the frigates to the ships most in want of hands, and converted the Phoenix into a fire-ship. At seven in the evening, the French squadron — about two leagues distant — tacked to gain the weather-gage. Byng, unwilling to surrender that advantage, put his ships about as well. The two fleets manoeuvred through the night.

The Engagement

The morning of 20 May was hazy, and the French fleet could not be seen until nearly noon. At two o’clock, Byng made the signal to bear away two points from the wind and engage. What followed was one of the most baffling and controversial actions in British naval history.

Rear-Admiral Temple West, commanding the van division, was at too great a distance to comply with both of Byng’s signals — the signal to bear away and the signal for the line of battle — and chose to interpret his orders aggressively. He bore away seven points from the wind and attacked the French van with such impetuosity that several of the enemy’s ships were soon obliged to quit the line. West’s division fought magnificently (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 258–296).

But Byng’s own division did not advance. The admiral’s flagship, the 90-gun Ramillies, hung back. The Intrepid, the sternmost ship of the van, lost her fore-topmast, and Byng later claimed that this obliged his whole division to back their sails to avoid falling foul of each other. The court-martial that subsequently examined the action found otherwise: immediately after the signal for engaging, while West’s van bore down upon the enemy, Byng in the Ramillies had edged away some points, causing the Trident and Louisa to get to windward of him. To bring them back into station, he backed his mizen-topsail and attempted to back his main-topsail — a manoeuvre that “necessarily retarded all the ships in his division, and gave the enemy time to escape” (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 298–311).

De la Galissonière seized the opportunity. His ships, being cleaner and more weatherly than the British, made sail and withdrew. Byng did not pursue.

The engagement had been indecisive. British casualties were forty-two killed and one hundred and sixty-eight wounded — remarkably light for a fleet action. No ship was lost on either side. But the strategic result was a French victory. The French fleet had been engaged and had retired in good order, while the British had failed to cripple it or to establish command of the sea around Minorca.

Byng’s Retreat

Instead of renewing the action the following day, Byng called a council of war. The council, noting the damage to the Intrepid and the battered state of several other vessels, the superiority of the French fleet in numbers (twelve ships of the line against Byng’s ten), and the impossibility of throwing reinforcements into Fort St. Philip while the French fleet remained in being, resolved that it was “not expedient to make any further attempt” to relieve the garrison. Byng ordered the squadron to return to Gibraltar.

He reached Gibraltar on 19 June 1756. Minorca was left to its fate.

Fort St. Philip held out under General Blakeney against Richelieu’s siege for seventy days, surrendering on 28 June 1756. The garrison, which had suffered terribly from the bombardment and from disease, was granted the honours of war. Blakeney himself returned to England to a hero’s welcome, becoming a folk-hero precisely because the disaster was so universally blamed on Byng.

The Outcry

When news of the loss of Minorca reached London in July 1756, the public reaction was volcanic. The British people had endured several years of mounting humiliation at French hands — the loss of the Ohio Valley, Braddock’s defeat on the Monongahela, the French seizure of the Mediterranean settlements — and the collapse of Minorca was the culminating blow. The mob burned Byng in effigy; the London press vilified him in terms as savage as any ever directed at a British officer.

The Newcastle ministry was itself in crisis. The Duke of Newcastle had been First Lord of the Treasury since 1754, but his administration had proven lamentably unequal to the challenges of war. The public, searching for a scapegoat for a catastrophe that was at least partially the result of ministerial incompetence — the unpreparedness of the fleet, the contradictory orders that had paralysed the council at Gibraltar, the failure to reinforce Minorca before the French struck — fastened on Byng with ferocious intensity. The government, desperately seeking to deflect blame from itself, did nothing to discourage the clamour.

Byng, Admiral West, and General Fowke arrived at Portsmouth on 3 July 1756. West was ordered to London and graciously received by the King; his conduct in the action was universally praised. Fowke was court-martialled for disobedience of orders in failing to send the battalion from Gibraltar and was sentenced to be suspended for a year — a sentence the King later aggravated by dismissing him from the service. Byng was placed under arrest and confined, first at Portsmouth and then at Greenwich Hospital, where he remained a close prisoner until December (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 643–659).

The Court-Martial

Proceedings

Byng’s court-martial opened on 28 December 1756, on board HMS St. George in Portsmouth harbour. The court consisted of four admirals and nine captains of the navy — a panel of Byng’s peers, many of whom had served alongside him. They sat for a month, examining evidence daily for and against the prisoner (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 660–663).

The case against Byng was damning. Admiral West testified that he saw no reason why the whole squadron could not have been brought into action. The evidence showed that the British fleet had held the weather-gage, that Byng had not brought his own division into effective range, that he had ordered the Deptford out of the line of battle (reducing his line to the same number of ships as the French — a decision for which the court censured him), and that he had failed to pursue the retiring French fleet. The signal books and logs demonstrated that Byng’s manoeuvring of the Ramillies had retarded the whole centre division.

But the court also found mitigating circumstances. Byng had warned the Admiralty before sailing that his squadron was undermanned. The contradictory orders at Gibraltar had been beyond his control. His personal courage — though put in question by the finding — was not equivalent to the cowardice that the popular fury alleged. The court explicitly found that Byng’s misconduct “did not proceed from want of courage, or from disaffection” (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 703–707).

The 12th Article of War

The charge against Byng was under the 12th Article of War, which provided:

“Every person in the fleet, who through cowardice, negligence or disaffection, shall not use his utmost exertions to take or destroy every ship which it shall be his duty to engage; and to assist and relieve all and every of his majesty’s ships which it shall be his duty to assist and relieve.”

The penalty prescribed for breach of this article was death. The court-martial, by the narrowest possible construction of the law, found Byng guilty of “negligence” — though they had acquitted him of cowardice and disaffection — and sentenced him to be shot. But the same court, in a powerful expression of their own unease, unanimously recommended him to his majesty’s clemency (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 695–707).

The Legal Wrangle

The Admiralty was troubled. The court had found Byng guilty of “negligence” under the 12th Article, yet the article itself named cowardice, negligence, or disaffection as the culpable states of mind. Since the court had explicitly acquitted Byng of cowardice and disaffection, the Lords of the Admiralty were uncertain whether the sentence was legally sound. They presented a memorial to George II requesting that the opinion of the twelve judges of the realm be taken (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 709–719).

The judges pronounced the sentence legal.

Some members of the court-martial then petitioned Parliament to be released from their oath of secrecy, hoping to reveal information that might incline the King to pardon Byng. A bill to this effect passed the House of Commons, but when it reached the House of Lords, each member of the court-martial was separately asked whether he had anything to reveal that might warrant a pardon. “Strange as it may seem,” Campbell records, “they all answered in the negative!” (Campbell, Vol. V, lines 721–728).

It has been whispered ever since — Campbell records the rumour explicitly — that Lord Anson’s private remonstrances deprived Byng “of that last ray of hope which some scruples of conscience gave him reason to expect.” Anson, as First Lord of the Admiralty, had been instrumental in the conduct of the war. A pardon for Byng would have been, by implication, a reflection on the Admiralty that had sent him out with an inadequate force.

The Execution

On 14 March 1757, Admiral John Byng was brought on board the 74-gun [[HMS Monarch|HMS Monarch]] in Portsmouth harbour for execution. Byng himself had requested death by shooting rather than hanging, a privilege accorded to his rank.

The scene was solemn and theatrical. Byng walked to the quarterdeck, where a cushion had been placed for him to kneel on. He refused a blindfold, saying that he would look his executioners in the eye. He was persuaded to permit a handkerchief to be bound over his eyes. He dropped a handkerchief as the signal — his own command — and the marines, six in number, fired. Byng was killed instantly.

The shot left a crowd of thousands watching from the shore and from small boats in the harbour, many of whom had come expecting a last-minute reprieve that never came. Campbell notes that Byng faced his death with perfect composure, accepting the sentence as the due of any officer who accepted a commission with the articles of war in his hand.

He was buried at his family estate in Southill, Bedfordshire.

Significance

Voltaire’s Epigram

The most enduring epitaph for the Byng affair was written not by an Englishman but by Voltaire, who watched the affair with sardonic detachment from his exile in Geneva. In his novel Candide (February 1759), Voltaire has the hero arrive in Portsmouth and witness the execution of an admiral. When he asks why the officer is being shot, he is told that “it is the custom here to shoot an admiral from time to time, to encourage the others” (pour encourager les autres). The phrase has passed into every European language as a byword for harsh, arbitrary, or politically motivated punishment.

The Legacy of Injustice

The Byng affair left a profound and lasting scar on the Royal Navy. The message sent to every officer was unmistakable: failure — even failure born of inadequate forces, contradictory orders, or honest misjudgement — could cost a man his life. The effect on naval decision-making was paradoxical. Some later historians have argued that the Byng execution made British admirals more cautious, more prone to call councils of war, and less willing to take the bold risks that had characterised the navy under Sir John Leake or Sir George Rooke. Others have contended that it had the opposite effect — that the threat of execution drove later commanders like Sir Edward Hawke to ever more aggressive tactics, culminating in the daring pursuit into the reefs of Quiberon Bay in 1759.

What is certain is that the execution was a political act. The Newcastle ministry fell within months of Byng’s conviction, and William Pitt the Elder came to power in December 1756, inaugurating the extraordinary run of global victories that would secure Britain’s imperial supremacy. Byng was the sacrifice offered to a public that demanded blood, and the political establishment — including the Admiralty, the Crown, and the judiciary — had co-operated in the sacrifice.

The Byng Family

The tragedy was rendered more poignant by the family history. Sir George Byng, John’s father, had been the victor of Cape Passaro (1718), the greatest British naval victory of the previous generation — a smashing, decisive triumph in which Byng’s father had destroyed the Spanish fleet without losing a single ship of his own. The father had been rewarded with a peerage (Viscount Torrington), the thanks of Parliament, and a place in the nation’s pantheon of naval heroes. The son’s fate — to be shot on the quarterdeck as a warning to his fellow officers — could not have been more cruelly different.

John Byng’s brother, Edward Byng, served as a commissioner of the Navy, and the Byng family’s naval tradition continued through the 18th century. But the shadow of the execution never lifted.

Constitutional Reflections

The case raised profound questions about military justice that continued to reverberate. The 12th Article of War, under which Byng was convicted, had been deliberately framed in the broadest possible terms to ensure that officers could not evade responsibility by legalistic quibbling. But its application in Byng’s case revealed the dangers of such breadth. A man who was not cowardly and not disaffected — a man found to have acted from negligence, but negligence in a context where his ships were undermanned, his instructions contradictory, and the enemy superior — was nonetheless executed.

The Earl of Hardwicke, Lord High Chancellor of Great Britain and one of the most eminent legal minds of the age, was deeply involved in the deliberations over Byng’s sentence. Hardwicke advised that the sentence was legal under the articles of war as they stood, and he opposed the parliamentary bill that would have released the court from its oath of secrecy. The Byng affair thus stands as a landmark in the history of military law, a case study in the tension between discipline and justice that has never been fully resolved.

In 1779, the 12th Article of War was amended to give courts-martial greater discretion in sentencing — the direct result of the Byng case — so that no future officer could be condemned to death for negligence alone. But the amendment came twenty-two years too late for John Byng.
