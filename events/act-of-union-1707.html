Act of Union (1707)

The Act of Union (1707) — formally the Union with England Act (Scottish) and the Union with Scotland Act (English) — created the Kingdom of Great Britain, uniting the previously separate Kingdoms of England (including Wales) and Scotland into a single sovereign state with a single parliament. The Union took effect on 1 May 1707. It was the culmination of a century of on-again-off-again negotiations, driven into being by the disastrous Darien Scheme, English fears of a Jacobite Scotland allied with France, and the hard-headed calculations of Lord Godolphin’s ministry.

Background: The Road to Union

The Failed Darien Scheme (1698–1700)

The single most important proximate cause of the Union was the catastrophic failure of the Darien Scheme (1698–1700), Scotland’s attempt to establish a colony on the Isthmus of Panama under the auspices of the Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies. The scheme had been promoted by the Scottish financier William Paterson, co-founder of the bank-of-england, who envisioned Darien as a global trading entrepôt connecting the Atlantic and Pacific oceans.

The project captured the Scottish imagination: between one-quarter and one-half of Scotland’s liquid capital was invested in the Company. Four expeditions were sent, but the colony — called New Caledonia — was doomed from the start. The Spanish Crown claimed the territory as its own, the English Crown (under William III), eager to preserve peace with Spain during the Nine Years’ War, refused to supply or support the settlement, and the climate and disease devastated the colonists. By 1700, the settlement was abandoned with the loss of some 2,000 lives and a ruinous financial loss.

Burnet records that the loss was so universal and so operative in Scottish sentiment that “the Design on Darien, which the Jacobites had set on foot and prosecuted with so much fury, and with bad Intentions, did now engage many to promote the Union, who, without that Consideration, would have been at least neutral, if not backward in it.” Burnet, Vol. II

The Economic Consequence: The Darien disaster left Scotland’s mercantile class convinced that Scotland could not succeed as an independent colonial power. Without access to English markets and English naval protection, the Scottish economy could not compete. The investing classes who had lost heavily in Darien saw in the Union’s Equivalent a means of recovering their losses, and many threw their weight behind the project.

The Act of Security (1704) and English Alarm

If the Darien Scheme pushed Scots toward Union, English fears about Scottish independence pulled them. In 1703–1704, the Scottish Parliament, frustrated by English intransigence on trade and the succession, passed the Act of Security, which provided that upon the death of Queen Anne (who had no surviving children), Scotland would be free to choose a different successor to the Crown than the Hanoverian heir designated by the English Act of Settlement. The Act further provided for Scotland to be armed and prepared for war if the succession were not settled to its satisfaction.

This was a direct challenge to English strategic interests. If Scotland chose a Jacobite claimant — James Francis Edward Stuart, the “Old Pretender” — after Anne’s death, England would face a hostile, French-allied state on its northern border. As Burnet notes, the Act of Security “was imputed chiefly to the Lord Treasurer” (Godolphin), and threats of impeaching him for advising it were “often let fall” by his political enemies. Burnet, Vol. II

The English Parliament responded with the Alien Act (1705) , which threatened to treat Scots as aliens and cut off trade in cattle, coal, and linen unless Scotland either accepted the Hanoverian succession or entered into negotiations for a full incorporating union. The economic coercion was deliberate: Scotland’s primary export trade ran through England, and the threat of exclusion was devastating.

English Fears of Jacobite Scotland

Beneath the tactical posturing lay a deeper English strategic fear: the prospect of a Jacobite Scotland allied with Louis XIV’s France. Throughout the War of the Spanish Succession (1702–1714), the Jacobite court-in-exile at Saint-Germain maintained close contacts with dissident Scottish nobles. A French-backed Jacobite rising in Scotland, co-ordinated with the war on the Continent, could open a deadly second front. The Union would extinguish this possibility permanently by absorbing Scotland’s Parliament and foreign policy into a single British state.

As Campbell notes in his Naval History, the Union had profound naval implications: a unified British navy, Scottish access to English trade routes, and the birth of a truly British naval dominance. Campbell, Vol. III

The Negotiations

The Commissioners

In 1705, the English Parliament passed an Act authorising the Queen to name Commissioners to treat of a union. The Scottish Parliament reciprocated. Each side appointed 31 Commissioners, who met in London from 16 April to 22 July 1706 at the Cockpit in Whitehall.

English Commissioners included:

  • Sidney Godolphin, 1st Earl of Godolphin — Lord High Treasurer and chief minister
  • John Churchill, Duke of Marlborough — Captain-General, commanding the war on the Continent
  • Charles Montagu, 1st Earl of Halifax — Whig statesman
  • John Somers, 1st Baron Somers — former Lord Chancellor, leading Whig intellectual
  • Robert Harley — Speaker of the House of Commons
  • William Cowper — Lord Keeper of the Great Seal
  • Sir Thomas Trevor — Chief Justice of the Common Pleas

Scottish Commissioners included:

  • James Douglas, 2nd Duke of Queensberry — Lord High Commissioner to the Scottish Parliament, the dominant figure on the Scottish side. Created Duke of Dover in the Peerage of Great Britain as a reward.
  • John Erskine, 6th Earl of Mar — Secretary of State for Scotland
  • James Ogilvy, 1st Earl of Seafield — Lord Chancellor of Scotland
  • John Dalrymple, 2nd Earl of Stair — leading unionist, who “maintained the Debate on the last Day” and died suddenly the following night, his spirits “quite exhausted by the Length and Vehemence of the Debate” Burnet, Vol. II
  • David Boyle, 1st Earl of Glasgow — Lord Treasurer of Scotland
  • Sir James Ogilvy — Lord Advocate
  • Adam Cockburn, Lord Ormiston — Lord Justice Clerk
  • William Morrison — Lord President of the Court of Session

The Squadrone Volante

A pivotal role in the negotiations was played by an informal parliamentary group known as the Squadrone Volante (“Flying Squadron”), a group of some twenty to thirty Scottish nobles and gentlemen who were “in no Engagements with the Court” and who had “been disobliged, and turned out of great Posts.” They included the Marquess of Tweedale, the Earls of Rothes, Roxburgh, Hadington, and Marchmont. Burnet writes that they “kept themselves very close and united, and seemed to have no other Interest, but that of their Country” and that if they “had set themselves against the Union, the Design must have miscarried.” Burnet, Vol. II

The Opposition

The Union faced formidable opposition in Scotland. The leading opponents were:

  • James Hamilton, 4th Duke of Hamilton — the premier peer of Scotland, who led the parliamentary opposition with great vehemence but was “against all desperate Motions,” having “much to lose” Burnet, Vol. II
  • John Murray, 1st Duke of Atholl — believed by Burnet to be “in a foreign Correspondence” with the Jacobite court
  • John Johnstone, 1st Marquess of Annandale
  • Andrew Fletcher of Saltoun — the most intellectually formidable opponent, a Scottish patriot and political theorist who argued passionately for a federal rather than incorporating union, warning that Scotland would be “sunk into a Dependence on England”

Fletcher’s arguments were the most sophisticated articulated against the Union. He had earlier proposed a scheme of limitations on the Crown and a federal arrangement. His concern was that Scotland’s 45 MPs and 16 representative peers would be so outnumbered in the British Parliament — “above an hundred Peers and five hundred and thirteen Commoners” on the English side — that they could never hold the balance. Burnet, Vol. II

Popular Opposition

The debates in Edinburgh were accompanied by intense popular agitation. Mobs gathered in the streets; there were riots in Edinburgh, Glasgow, and Dumfries. The Presbyterian clergy and their flocks feared that the Union would submerge their Church in the English episcopal establishment. As Burnet records: “if the Presbyterians had not been possessed with a Jealousy, that the consequence of this Union would be, the Change of Church-Government among them, and that they would be swallowed up by the Church of England” — a fear that “took such root in many, that no Assurances, that were offered, could remove their Fears.” Burnet, Vol. II

The old Duchess of Hamilton (Anne Hamilton, 3rd Duchess) was said to have great credit with the Presbyterians and it was suggested that she and her son “had particular Views, as hoping, that if Scotland should continue a separated Kingdom, the Crown might come into their Family, they being the next in Blood, after King James’s Posterity.” Burnet, Vol. II

At one point, the situation appeared so dangerous that Queensberry despaired of succeeding and feared for his personal safety. He wrote to Godolphin suggesting an adjournment. Godolphin’s answer was blunt: “a delay was, upon the Matter, laying the whole Design aside.” He ordered troops in England and Ireland to be ready, and more forces from Flanders if necessary, with the observation that “the French were in no condition to send any Assistance” to potential rebels. Burnet, Vol. II

The Treaty: Twenty-Five Articles

The Commissioners produced 25 Articles of Union, which were laid before the Scottish Parliament in October 1706 and debated clause by clause over three months. The main provisions were:

IIThe succession to the Crown of Great Britain shall be according to the Act of Settlement (i.e., the Hanoverian line)Settled the Protestant succession, extinguishing Jacobite hopes
IIIThe United Kingdom shall have one Parliament, styled the Parliament of Great BritainThe most controversial provision — Scotland gave up its independent legislature
IVFull freedom of trade and navigation between Scotland and England, and with the colonies and plantationsThe great economic prize for Scotland

The Equivalent

Article XV provided for the Equivalent, a lump sum of £398,085 10s to be raised in England and sent to Scotland. Its purposes were threefold:

  1. To compensate Scotland for assuming a share of the English national debt accumulated during the War of the Spanish Succession
  2. To discharge the public debts of Scotland
  3. To reimburse the Company of Scotland (the Darien investors) for their losses, with interest — upon which the Company was to be dissolved, with any overplus applied to the encouragement of manufactures

As Burnet notes, “the Equivalent was to be applied, to the Encouragement of Manufactures” and that in its disposition “great Partialities appeared, which were much complained of.” The ministry and those who depended on them “moved for very extravagant Allowances to those, who had been employed in this last, and in the former Treaty.” Burnet, Vol. II

Free Trade and Economic Integration

Article IV, guaranteeing free trade, was the single most potent argument for the Union in Scotland. As Burnet writes, “The opening a free Trade, not only with England, but with the Plantations, and the Protection of the Fleet of England, drew in those who understood these Matters, and saw there was no other way in view, to make the Nation rich and considerable.” Burnet, Vol. II

For Scotland’s trading classes, already burned by the Darien disaster, access to England’s protected colonial markets — the West Indian sugar islands, the North American tobacco colonies, and soon the East — was transformative. For England, the extension of its Navigation Acts to Scotland closed a loophole through which Scottish merchants had traded with the colonies as de facto aliens and secured a unified imperial trade system.

Scottish Representation

Scotland’s representation in the new Parliament of Great Britain — 16 representative peers in the Lords and 45 MPs in the Commons — reflected a careful balancing act. Scotland bore approximately one-fortieth of the tax burden, yet received “near the eleventh part of the Legislature” (16 of ~190 Lords, 45 of ~558 Commons). Burnet observes that “it is held a Maxim, that in the framing of a Government, a Proportion ought to be observed, between the Share in the Legislature, and the Burden to be born.” Burnet, Vol. II

The representative peer system was particularly controversial. Scottish peers lost their individual right to sit in the House of Lords — “the greatest of them all, which was the Voting in the House of Lords, was restrained to Sixteen, to be elected by the rest at every new Parliament.” Despite this, a greater majority of the Scottish nobility concurred in voting for the Union than in the other estates. As Smollett later records, the system proved contentious: when the Duke of Hamilton was created Duke of Brandon in the Peerage of Great Britain, the House of Lords ruled that Scottish peers created peers of Great Britain after the Union had no right to sit, a decision that “infringement of the union” inflamed Scottish opinion. Smollett, Vol. X

Preservation of Scottish Institutions

The Treaty guaranteed the preservation of two of Scotland’s most cherished institutions:

The Legal System (Article XXIV): The separate legal system of Scotland, based on Roman-Dutch civil law rather than English common law, was preserved in perpetuity. This guaranteed the continued existence of the Court of Session (the supreme civil court), the High Court of Justiciary (criminal), and the distinctive Scottish sheriff courts. Scots law continued to develop independently, and the Act of Union has been a foundational constitutional document in Scottish legal reasoning ever since.

The Presbyterian Church: Religious matters were explicitly excluded from the Commissioners’ remit — the Acts authorising the negotiations “expressly limited” that the Commissioners “should not treat of those Matters.” Instead, separate Acts of Parliament in both kingdoms were passed to secure the respective church establishments. The Act for Securing the Protestant Religion and Presbyterian Church Government (1706) — passed by the Scottish Parliament alongside the Union — guaranteed the permanent establishment of the Presbyterian Church (the Kirk). Smollett records that the Presbyterian clergy and General Assembly regarded this as “an essential and fundamental condition of the treaty of union” and remonstrated vigorously when the British Parliament later passed legislation affecting Scottish religious matters, such as the toleration granted to Episcopalian clergy. Smollett, Vol. X

The Legislative Process

In the Scottish Parliament

The Articles were debated in the Scottish Parliament from October 1706 to January 1707. Each Article was voted on separately, and the government’s majority held firm through every division. As Burnet describes it:

“The Commissioners from the Shires and Boroughs, were almost equally divided, tho’ it was evident they were to be the chief Gainers by it; among these the Union was agreed to, by a very small Majority: It was the Nobility, that in every Vote turned the Scale for the Union.” Burnet, Vol. II

The Squadrone Volante ultimately declared for the Union, and “not only joined in it, but promoted it effectually, and with Zeal.” The great debates were managed for the Union by the Earls of Seafield and Stair (for the Ministry) and the Earls of Roxburgh and Marchmont (for the Squadrone); against it by Hamilton, Atholl, and Annandale.

The Act passed the Scottish Parliament on 16 January 1707 and was sent up to London “in the beginning of February.”

In the English Parliament

The English legislative process was deliberately engineered to minimise opposition. Burnet records that the Bill was prepared by Sir Simon Harcourt with “so particular a Contrivance, that it cut off all Debates.” The Preamble was a mere recital of the Articles as passed in Scotland, together with the Acts for church security, and then “in conclusion, there came one Enacting Clause, ratifying all.” This drafting “put those, upon great Difficulties, who had resolved to object to several Articles… for they could not come at any Debate about them.”

The matter “was carried on with such Zeal, that it passed through the House of Commons, before those, who intended to oppose it, had recovered themselves out of the Surprize.” In the Lords, where the Articles “had been copiously debated there for several Days, before the Bill was sent up to them,” it passed with the support of Lord Treasurer Godolphin, the Earls of Sunderland and Wharton, Lords Townshend and Halifax, and above all Lord Somers. The bishops of Oxford, Norwich, and Burnet himself argued for it. Burnet, Vol. II

The royal assent was given on 6 March 1707.

The Union Takes Effect: 1 May 1707

The Treaty provided that the Union “was to commence on the First of May” and that “till that time, the two Kingdoms were still distinct, and their two Parliaments continued still to sit.” On 1 May 1707, the Parliaments of England and Scotland were dissolved, and the first Parliament of Great Britain met.

Burnet captures the significance with a note of providential reflection: “Thus this great Design, so long wished and laboured for in vain, was begun, and happily ended, within the Compass of Nine Months.” Burnet, Vol. II

Economic Consequences

The Union transformed the Scottish economy over the following decades:

  • Colonial Trade: Scottish merchants gained immediate access to English colonial markets, and the tobacco trade in particular boomed. Glasgow became the leading tobacco-importing port in Britain by the 1720s.
  • Agricultural Improvement: Exposure to English markets and English agricultural techniques spurred the beginnings of the Scottish Agricultural Revolution.
  • The Equivalent: The £398,085 Equivalent compensated Darien investors and discharged Scottish public debt, but the actual disbursement was delayed — Burnet records that “Three Months passed, before the Equivalent was sent to Scotland” — and was attended by charges of partiality.
  • Naval and Imperial: As Campbell emphasises, the Union created a unified British navy and freed the English state from the strategic nightmare of a hostile Scotland allied to France. Campbell, Vol. III

Historiographical Notes

Burnet, writing within a decade of the Union, provides the most detailed contemporary account of the parliamentary politics. His narrative emphasises the providential hand of God in the affair, the decisive role of the Squadrone Volante, and Godolphin’s steely nerve during the Edinburgh riots. He also acknowledges the widespread use of bribery — “those of Scotland had learned from England, to set a Price on their Votes, and they expected to be well paid for them” — but treats this as a necessary means to a desirable end.

Smollett, writing half a century later, focuses more on the post-Union disputes — the Hamilton peerage case, the toleration controversy, the patronage bill — suggesting that the fruits of Union were contested from the start.

For subsequent historians, the Union of 1707 remains one of the most debated events in British history: was it a voluntary partnership of equals, or an English takeover lubricated by bribes? The truth lies somewhere between, as it was all of these things at once — a salvage operation for a bankrupt Scottish elite, a strategic necessity for an English state at war, and the foundation of the British imperial state.
