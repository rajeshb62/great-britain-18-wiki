Excise Crisis of 1733

The Excise Crisis of 1733 was the most serious domestic political defeat of Robert Walpole’s long ministry — a furious collision between the Crown’s chief minister and a popular opposition that mobilised the streets, the press, and the petitions of the trading nation against a well-intentioned fiscal reform. Walpole’s proposal to extend the excise system to tobacco and wine was met with a campaign of such ferocity — orchestrated by the exiled Henry St John Bolingbroke and the dissident Whig William Pulteney, amplified through the pages of the Craftsman newspaper — that the Minister was forced to retreat in April 1733, abandoning his bill before it could pass. Though Walpole survived the crisis, his prestige was permanently diminished, and the episode forged the ideological weapons of the Patriot opposition that would eventually hound him from office in 1742.

The crisis is remembered as a defining moment in the long struggle between the executive and the “country” interest in eighteenth-century British politics — a struggle over the meaning of liberty, property, and the proper limits of state power.

Background: The Problem of the Customs Revenue

The fiscal architecture of early Hanoverian Britain rested on two pillars: the land tax, levied on real property at a rate that varied with the needs of the state, and the customs duties, collected at the ports on imported commodities. The system was riddled with leakage. Smuggling was endemic along the entire coast — a semi-respectable trade in which entire communities participated, from the fishermen of Kent to the merchants of Bristol. The tobacco trade was especially corrupt. The duties on tobacco were payable on importation, requiring the merchant to either pay at once or furnish bonds with sureties. This forced the importer to finance the duty long before the commodity found a market — a burden that drove many into the arms of smugglers and fraudulent dealers.

Walpole had spent much of his early career studying these problems. As Chancellor of the Exchequer and First Lord of the Treasury, he had inherited the machinery of the South Sea Company crash and had devoted himself to the restoration of public credit and the reduction of the national debt. His Sinking Fund — established in 1716, though increasingly raided for current expenditure — was designed to amortise the debt gradually. He had pursued a policy of low land taxes, keeping the rate at two shillings in the pound or lower throughout his ministry, a policy that endeared him to the landed gentry who dominated the House of Commons. But the land tax could not be kept low forever unless the customs revenue could be made more productive.

The solution, to Walpole’s methodical mind, was elegant: why not apply the excise system — the system of inland duties on consumption that already governed malt, beer, candles, soap, and salt — to tobacco and wine, the two commodities most afflicted by smuggling? Instead of collecting the duty at the port on importation, the tobacco would be deposited in secure warehouses, locked with two keys: one held by the merchant, one by the excise officer. The merchant would pay the inland duty only when the tobacco was actually withdrawn for domestic sale. Tobacco destined for export would be discharged of the small customs duty paid at importation and re-shipped free of charge.

The advantages seemed compelling to Walpole:

  • Reduction of smuggling: The warehousing system would make it far harder for fraudulent dealers to evade the duty.
  • Ease to the fair trader: The merchant would no longer need to finance the duty before finding a market.
  • Additional revenue: Walpole estimated the scheme would add £200,000–£300,000 per annum to the public revenue.
  • Lower land tax: Increased excise receipts could fund a further reduction in the land tax, cementing Walpole’s support among the squirearchy.
  • Stability: The excise was a more predictable revenue stream than the volatile customs.

Smollett records that Walpole, when he finally opened his scheme to the House of Commons, declared that he had “no intention to promote a general excise.” He assured the House that he meant only to remedy the specific frauds that plagued the tobacco and wine trades. But his opponents — and the public — did not believe him.

The Excise Scheme: Walpole’s Proposal

The House of Commons having resolved itself into a committee to deliberate upon the “better security and improvement of the duties and revenues charged upon tobacco and wines,” all the papers relating to these duties were submitted to the perusal of the members. The commissioners of the customs and excise were ordered to attend. The avenues of Westminster were crowded with multitudes of people. Both sides appeared ready and eager for the contest.

On 14 March 1733, Sir Robert Walpole rose to open his design. He took notice of the arts that had been used to prejudice the people against his plan before it was known. The clamours, he said, had “originally risen from smugglers and fraudulent dealers, who had enriched themselves by cheating the public” — and these had been “strenuously assisted and supported by another set of men, fond of every opportunity to stir up the people of Great-Britain to mutiny and sedition.”

He expatiated on the frauds committed in the tobacco revenue, the hardships suffered by the American planters, the injury done to the fair trader, and the loss sustained by the public. The scheme he proposed was this:

  1. The further subsidy of three farthings per pound on imported tobacco would continue to be levied at the custom-house and paid to the civil list, as before.
  2. The tobacco would then be lodged in warehouses appointed by the commissioners of excise.
  3. The warehouse-keeper (appointed by the commissioners) would have one lock and key; the merchant-importer would have another.
  4. The tobacco would be secured until the merchant found a vent for it, either by exportation or home consumption.
  5. The portion destined for exportation would be discharged of the three farthings paid at importation and exported without further trouble.
  6. The portion destined for home consumption would be delivered to the purchaser upon his paying the inland duty of four pence per pound to the excise officer.
  7. All penalties and forfeitures formerly belonging to the crown would for the future be applied to the use of the public.
  8. Appeals in excise cases would be heard summarily by two or three judges named by the King, or by the judge of assize on the next circuit, “without the formality of proceedings in courts of law or equity.”

Such was the substance of the famous Excise Scheme. Walpole moved that the duties and subsidies on tobacco should cease on 24 June 1733.

The Parliamentary Debate

The debate that followed was one of the great parliamentary contests of the age. All the ablest speakers on both sides of the question were engaged.

The Defence

The scheme was espoused by the ablest ministerial speakers: Sir Philip Yorke (soon to be Lord Chief Justice and ennobled as Lord Hardwicke), Sir Joseph Jekyll (Master of the Rolls), Lord Hervey, Sir Thomas Robinson, Sir William Yonge, Henry Pelham, and Thomas Winnington — whom Smollett judged to have “excelled all his cotemporaries of the ministry in talents and address.” Walpole himself spoke at length, defending the scheme as a prudent and necessary reform.

The Opposition

The opposition was led by the leading figures of the organised opposition to Walpole:

  • William Pulteney — the most formidable parliamentary orator of the opposition, a former Whig who had broken with Walpole and become his bitterest enemy.
  • Sir William Wyndham — the leader of the Tory party in the Commons, a man of great integrity and ability.
  • Sir John Barnard — a merchant and representative of the City of London, who spoke with the authority of commercial experience.
  • Mr Perry — member for the City of London, who first answered Walpole.
  • Sir Paul Methuen — a former diplomat and Whig who had joined the opposition.

The arguments against the scheme were comprehensive and devastating:

  1. Misrepresentation of frauds: The commissioners of the customs had been examined, and it appeared that the frauds did not exceed £40,000 per annum — far less than Walpole’s estimate — and could be remedied by a due extension of the laws already in being.

  2. No benefit to planters: No planter had ever complained until “instigated by letters from London.” The scheme, far from relieving the planters, would expose the factors to such grievous oppression that they could not continue the trade, and the planters would be ruined.

  3. Dangerous to liberty: The extension of the excise was represented as a threat to the liberties of the subject. The excise laws permitted officials to enter premises to inspect production and to enforce payment by summary process, without trial by jury. The opposition argued that a general excise would arm the Crown with an army of tax-collectors and informers, and that every Englishman’s home and warehouse would be subject to the intrusion of revenue officers.

  4. The thin end of the wedge: Though Walpole denied that he intended a general excise, his opponents insisted that the principle once admitted could not be contained. “Should a law be enacted in consequence of this motion,” they warned, “it would in all probability be some time or other used as a precedent for introducing excise-laws into every branch of the revenue; in which case the liberty of Great-Britain would be no more.”

  5. Multiplication of dependents: The scheme would produce an “additional swarm of excise officers and warehouse-keepers, appointed and paid by the treasury,” multiplying the dependents on the crown and enabling it “still farther to influence the freedom of elections.”

  6. Burden on trade: The merchant would be debarred access to his own commodities except at certain hours when the excise officers were present. Every sale would require a journey to the excise office for a permit — “which could not be obtained without trouble, expense, and delay.”

The debate was long and violent. But Walpole’s majority held: the first resolution passed by a margin of sixty-one votes. Several further resolutions were founded on the proposal, and to these the House agreed, “though not without another violent contest.”

The Popular Outcry

If the parliamentary battle was a narrow victory for Walpole, the battle for public opinion was a rout. The opposition — particularly Bolingbroke and Pulteney — had prepared the ground with masterful skill.

The ‘Craftsman’ and the Patriot Campaign

Henry St John Bolingbroke, living in exile in France (though pardoned in 1723 and permitted to return to England in 1725, he was still excluded from the House of Lords), waged his war against Walpole through the printed word. With Pulteney and the journalist Nicholas Amhurst, Bolingbroke founded and wrote for the Craftsman, the most effective opposition newspaper of the age. For years it had attacked Walpole’s “corruption,” his management of Parliament through places and pensions, and his subservience to the Hanoverian court.

The excise scheme was the gift the Craftsman had been waiting for. It allowed Bolingbroke to fuse his philosophical critique of ministerial corruption with a concrete, visceral grievance. The excise — with its hated excisemen, its power of entry, its summary jurisdiction — was made to stand for everything that was wrong with Walpole’s system. It was the physical embodiment of “corruption”: the Crown armed with the power to enter every man’s warehouse, to search every man’s cellar, to tax every man’s consumption.

The cry went up, and spread across the kingdom with astonishing speed:

“Liberty, Property, and No Excise!”

Petitions and Riots

Petitions poured in from the cities and trading towns of England. The Lord Mayor, aldermen, and Common Council of the City of London — the commercial heart of the nation — presented a petition against the bill. The cities of Coventry and Nottingham did the same. A motion in the Commons that counsel should be heard for the City of London was rejected by the ministerial majority, and the petitions were ordered to lie upon the table. But the gesture did nothing to quiet the storm.

The populace crowded around Westminster Hall, blocking up all the avenues to the House of Commons. They insulted the persons of those members who had voted for the ministry. Members were jostled, spat upon, and threatened. Some were followed home by hostile crowds. Walpole himself began to fear for his life.

Smollett records that the traders and merchants of London were “so inflamed against this project, that they came down to the House of Commons in a body, demanding to be heard.” Sir John Barnard defended their right to do so, declaring that “merchants of character had a right to come down to the court of requests, and lobby of the house of commons, in order to solicit their friends and acquaintance against any scheme or project which they might think prejudicial to their commerce.”

Walpole, in a moment of uncharacteristic imprudence, insinuated that the men who led the petitions were “sturdy beggars” — a remark that Barnard turned back on him with withering effect.

The Duchess of Marlborough’s Contribution

The formidable Sarah Churchill, Duchess of Marlborough — widow of the great Duke of Marlborough and one of the wealthiest women in England — threw her immense personal fortune and political influence into the opposition. Though a lifelong Whig, she had broken with Walpole years earlier over his management of public affairs and his reluctance to pursue war with Spain. The excise scheme gave her a cause. She distributed anti-excise pamphlets, entertained opposition politicians at Marlborough House, and was widely believed to have financed the opposition campaign. Her hatred of Walpole was legendary — she referred to him as “that monster” — and her contribution to the defeat of the excise bill was celebrated by his enemies.

The Retreat (April 1733)

Walpole, seeing the tempest he had raised, faced a cruel choice. His majorities in the Commons were intact: the resolutions had passed, the bill had been committed, and he could probably have forced it through by sheer weight of parliamentary management. But the price would have been a nation in a state of near-insurrection. The mob that surrounded Westminster could not be dispersed by parliamentary majorities. The opposition was growing bolder; the petitions were multiplying; the cry of “No Excise!” was heard in every market town.

He thought proper to drop the design.

On 11 April 1733, Walpole rose in the Commons and moved that the second reading of the bill be postponed until the twelfth day of June — a date far enough in the future that everyone understood the measure was dead. The motion was carried. The Excise Bill was withdrawn.

The rejoicing was immediate and spectacular. The populace of London and Westminster celebrated with bonfires, illuminations, and the ringing of church bells. Walpole himself was burned in effigy. The Minister’s effigy was carried through the streets and committed to the flames with cries of “Liberty! Property! No Excise!” — the taunt that would haunt him for the remainder of his career.

The House of Commons, having lost the substance, busied itself with the shadow. Complaint was made of the insolence of the populace who had maltreated several members. Divers resolutions were taken against the “tumultuous crowds and their abettors,” communicated to the Lord Mayor of London, the Sheriff of Middlesex, and the High Bailiff of Westminster. Some individuals were apprehended in the Court of Requests as having fomented the disturbances — but they were soon released. The ministry could not afford to turn the petitioners into martyrs.

Aftermath and Significance

A Defeat, Not a Fall

The excise crisis was the most serious defeat Walpole had suffered since taking office in 1721 — but it did not destroy him. His majority in the Commons held. The King (George II) and Queen Caroline continued to support him. The opposition, though triumphant in the streets, could not translate popular clamour into a parliamentary majority. Walpole remained First Lord of the Treasury and continued to manage Parliament with his accustomed skill.

But the damage was real. His reputation for invincibility — the sense that he could manage any crisis — was shattered. The opposition had discovered that Walpole could be beaten, and that the way to beat him was to mobilise public opinion outside the walls of Parliament. The excise crisis taught the “Patriot” opposition the power of extra-parliamentary agitation — petitions, newspapers, public meetings, and the systematic cultivation of the “country” interest against the “court.”

The Forging of the ‘Patriot’ Opposition

The excise crisis was the crucible in which the Patriot opposition was forged. Bolingbroke’s Idea of a Patriot King — written in the wake of these events, though not published until 1738 — was the philosophical culmination of the campaign. The Patriot was to stand above party, devoted to the good of the whole nation, and pledged to root out the “corruption” of the Walpole system. The language of “Liberty, Property, and No Excise” was the vocabulary of Patriotism.

The crisis also gave the opposition its most potent rhetorical weapon: the excise itself. Walpole’s retreat was not the end of the excise controversy. The memory of 1733 was revived whenever any future minister proposed an excise duty — it haunted the Cider Tax of 1763 (which brought down George Grenville’s ministry among the cider counties of the West Country), and it echoed in the American colonists’ cry of “No taxation without representation” a generation later.

Walpole’s Response

Walpole drew the appropriate lessons. He never again attempted a major fiscal reform that would disturb the commercial interests. His remaining years in power were marked by a cautious, almost defensive, management of the Commons. He focused on foreign policy — or rather, on keeping Britain out of foreign wars — as his domestic capital had been depleted. But the pressure for war with Spain, fanned by the same opposition that had defeated the excise, eventually overwhelmed him. The War of Jenkins’ Ear began in 1739, and with it the beginning of the end of Walpole’s ministry.

The Legacy of the Excise Crisis

The Excise Crisis of 1733 is remembered as one of the great popular victories in eighteenth-century British politics — a moment when the “country” triumphed over the “court,” when the people of England rose up and forced their government to retreat. In truth, it was more complex. Walpole’s scheme was a sensible fiscal reform, carefully designed and rationally argued. It failed not because it was a bad policy but because it was a political miscalculation: Walpole underestimated the depth of popular hostility to the excise — a hostility rooted not in economic calculation but in a profound, almost instinctive, attachment to the common-law rights of Englishmen: trial by jury, the sanctity of the home, the freedom of the trader.

The excise — with its administrative apparatus of search, seizure, and summary justice — touched something raw in the English political imagination. It was the tax that did not trust you. It was the state in your cellar. And Walpole, the master of Parliament, had forgotten that there were limits to what even the most skilful management could achieve.

The crisis also demonstrated the extraordinary power of the press in the age of Walpole. The Craftsman — established in 1726 — had created a mass readership for opposition politics. The excise campaign showed how effectively that readership could be mobilised. Walpole’s later attempts to silence the press — his prosecution of the Craftsman for libel, his attempted censorship of parliamentary reporting — were driven by the wounds he received in 1733.

In the longer arc of British history, the excise crisis is a landmark in the development of extra-parliamentary politics — the idea that legitimate political opposition could be conducted not only within the walls of Westminster but through the mobilisation of public opinion. The petitions, the newspaper campaigns, the public meetings, the cries in the streets — these were the techniques that would be used again and again in the great struggles of the later eighteenth century: the Wilkes affair, the American crisis, the reform movement of the 1780s.

“Had the minister encountered no opposition but that which appeared within doors, his project would have certainly been carried into execution; but the whole nation was alarmed, and clamoured loudly against the excise-bill.” — Tobias Smollett, A Complete History of England, Vol. X

Key Figures

Robert Walpole (1676–1745)

The architect and victim of the excise scheme. Walpole had been First Lord of the Treasury and effective Prime Minister since 1721, the master of the House of Commons and the creator of the Whig system of parliamentary management. The excise crisis was his greatest domestic miscalculation. He proposed the scheme rationally and defended it ably, but he failed to anticipate the ferocity of the popular opposition. His retreat in April 1733 — the postponement of the bill — was the first major defeat of his career. He survived, but his authority was permanently diminished.

Henry St John Bolingbroke (1678–1751)

The intellectual architect of the opposition. In exile from public office (barred from the Lords by his attainder, though pardoned and permitted to return to England), Bolingbroke fought Walpole through the Craftsman and through his philosophical writings. His campaign against the excise fused his general critique of “corruption” with a specific, concrete grievance. The excise crisis was Bolingbroke’s greatest triumph: he had shown that the “Patriot” opposition could mobilise the nation against the court. His later Idea of a Patriot King (written in 1738, published 1749) was the philosophical legacy of this campaign.

William Pulteney (1684–1764)

Walpole’s most formidable parliamentary enemy. Pulteney had been a Whig, a colleague of Walpole in the early years, but had broken with him in the 1720s and become the leader of the dissident Whigs in the Commons. He was a brilliant orator — Smollett compares his style to “a torrent that bore down all before it” — and he threw his full force against the excise bill. He was the parliamentary voice of the campaign that Bolingbroke orchestrated from the press. Pulteney’s reward would come in 1742, when Walpole’s fall made him (briefly) the most powerful man in the Commons — though he would famously “take the thirty pieces of silver,” accepting a peerage as Earl of Bath and abandoning the popular cause that had made his reputation.

Sarah Churchill, Duchess of Marlborough (1660–1744)

The most formidable woman in English politics. Widow of the Duke of Marlborough, former confidante of Queen Anne, and the wealthiest woman in the kingdom after the Queen, the Duchess had broken with Walpole years before the excise crisis. Her hatred of him was visceral — she called him “that monster” and “the greatest villain that ever lived.” She poured her fortune into the opposition, entertaining Patriot politicians at Marlborough House, distributing anti-excise pamphlets, and using her vast network of contacts to coordinate the campaign. Her contribution to the defeat of the excise bill was celebrated by Walpole’s enemies and privately lamented by the Minister himself.

Sir John Barnard (c. 1685–1764)

The representative of the City of London in the Commons and one of the most effective critics of the excise scheme. A merchant of great integrity and commercial knowledge, Barnard spoke with authority against the claim that the scheme would relieve the tobacco trade. He defended the right of the merchants to petition and to appear at the bar of the House, and he humiliated Walpole in debate when the Minister imprudently called the petitioners “sturdy beggars.” Barnard remained a consistent opponent of Walpole throughout his career and is remembered as one of the most independent-minded MPs of the age.

Sir William Wyndham (1688–1740)

The leader of the Tory party in the Commons. Wyndham was a man of considerable ability and personal integrity — though his Jacobite sympathies (he had been implicated in the Atterbury Plot of 1722) made him suspect to the court. He threw his support behind the opposition to the excise, speaking powerfully against the extension of the excise laws.

William Pitt the Elder (1708–1778)

A young backbencher in 1733, Pitt had entered Parliament in February of that year as member for the pocket borough of Old Sarum — a seat controlled by the Cobham family. He made his maiden speech in the course of the excise debates, and it was noted. His oratory — the famous “Pittite thunder” — was already present in embryo. The excise crisis was his first political education. He learned from Bolingbroke and Pulteney the language of Patriotism, and from Walpole’s defeat he learned the power of public opinion. He would, a generation later, use both lessons to become the architect of Britain’s imperial greatness.
