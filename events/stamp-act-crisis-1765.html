Stamp Act Crisis (1765–1766)

The Stamp Act Crisis (1765–1766) was the first great constitutional confrontation between Great Britain and its American colonies, arising from Parliament’s attempt to impose a direct tax on the colonies to help service the enormous national debt incurred during the Seven Years’ War. The crisis pitted the British doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty — the claim that Parliament could legislate for the colonies “in all cases whatsoever” — against the colonial claim that there could be “no taxation without representation.” It produced the Stamp Act Congress, the first coordinated inter-colonial protest, wide-scale civil disobedience and mob violence, a crippling non-importation movement, and ultimately the repeal of the Act — but only after Parliament had asserted its absolute authority in the Declaratory Act. The crisis settled nothing about the fundamental constitutional question of colonial representation, and it established the framework of arguments that would lead, within a decade, to the American Revolution.

Background: The Debt of Empire

The Seven Years’ War (1756–1763) had been, from the British perspective, a war of unparalleled success. By the Peace of Paris (1763), Great Britain had expelled France from Canada, secured Florida from Spain, established unchallengeable supremacy in India, and won a naval dominance that no European power could contest. But the cost of victory was staggering. Britain’s national debt had risen from approximately £72 million in 1754 to £133 million by 1763 — a sum so vast that the annual interest payment alone consumed more than half of the government’s peacetime revenue. Belsham records that the Annual Register of 1763 described this burden as “a debt which, in the ordinary course of affairs, could never be discharged.”1

The British taxpayer, already the most heavily taxed in Europe, could bear no further impositions. Parliament’s attention therefore turned to the American colonies, which had benefited from the war’s enormous expenditure — much of it spent in America itself for troop pay, provisions, and logistics — and which, it was argued, now enjoyed the protection of British arms without contributing proportionately to their cost. The colonies, far from a drain on the treasury, were in fact immensely profitable: Belsham notes that the profits to Great Britain from the trade of the colonies amounted to “two millions per annum,” and that this was “the fund which carried you triumphantly through the last war.”2 But George Grenville, who became First Lord of the Treasury in April 1763 upon the resignation of the Earl of Bute, was determined to make the colonies pay directly into the exchequer.

Grenville had already taken a first step in 1764 with the Sugar Act (the American Duties Act), which reduced the duty on foreign molasses imported into the colonies from sixpence to threepence per gallon — but added new enforcement mechanisms, gave the Admiralty courts jurisdiction over smuggling cases, and was clearly intended as a revenue measure rather than a regulation of trade. The colonies protested vigorously, but the Act passed with little parliamentary opposition.

In March 1764, Grenville announced in the House of Commons that he would propose a stamp tax on the colonies in the following session, giving the Americans a year to propose alternative means of raising revenue. None were forthcoming. When Parliament reconvened in January 1765, Grenville proceeded with his plan.

The Stamp Act

The Stamp Act was introduced in the House of Commons in February 1765 and passed on 22 March 1765, to take effect on 1 November 1765. It imposed a tax on virtually every piece of paper used in the American colonies: legal documents (deeds, mortgages, wills, contracts), newspapers, pamphlets, almanacks, licences, ship’s papers, playing cards, and dice. All such papers were required to bear a revenue stamp, purchased from official distributors appointed by the British government.

The declared purpose was, in the language of the Act, “raising a revenue in America in aid to the mother country.” Opposition in Parliament was “weak in respect to numbers,” Belsham records, but “unusually ardent and animated.”3 General Henry Conway protested against the right; Colonel Isaac Barré delivered a devastating reply to Charles Townshend’s claim that the Americans were “children planted by our care, and nourished by our indulgence”:

“They planted by your care! No — your oppressions planted them in America… They nourished by your indulgence! No — they grew by your neglect of them.”4

Barré’s speech — in which he first used the phrase “Sons of Liberty” to describe the American colonists — was widely circulated in America and contributed powerfully to the rising mood of resistance. But it could not stop the Act, which passed with comfortable majorities in both Houses and received the royal assent without controversy.

The Act was, in Belsham’s scathing judgment, a hideous specimen of despotism. The practical difficulties of enforcing it in a thinly settled continent were immense: in many parts of America, the cost of obtaining the necessary stamps from the distributors was said to be “tenfold the amount of the duty.” The Act permitted prosecutors to bring actions against supposed delinquents in courts more than fifteen hundred miles from where the alleged offence had been committed, while defendants who prevailed could recover no damages if the judge certified a probable foundation for the prosecution. The judge himself had a direct financial interest in conviction, being “allowed a large proportion of the penalty in case of conviction.”5

“Well might it be asked, whether the most abject vassals of the most tyrannic Government would passively submit to so humiliating a yoke!” — William Belsham6

Colonial Reaction

The Virginia Resolves

The news of the Stamp Act reached America in the spring of 1765. The first organised protest came from the Virginia House of Burgesses, where a young and relatively obscure member named Patrick Henry introduced a set of resolutions — the Virginia Resolves — in May 1765. Henry’s resolves declared that the colonists possessed all the rights of Englishmen, that the right of taxation was vested solely in the colonial assemblies, and that any attempt to vest that power elsewhere was a threat to colonial liberties. The most radical of the resolutions — which asserted that the General Assembly of Virginia had “the only and sole exclusive right and power to lay taxes and impositions upon the inhabitants of this colony” — passed by a single vote.

The Virginia Resolves were published in newspapers throughout the colonies, often with additional resolutions appended that were more extreme than anything Henry had actually proposed. They electrified the colonial resistance and set the pattern for similar declarations from other colonial assemblies. Belsham notes that the Provincial Assemblies, “far from taking any effectual measures to enforce submission to the act, passed resolutions in opposition to it, declaratory of their constitutional and unalienable rights.”7

The Loyal Nine and the Sons of Liberty

In Boston, resistance took a more direct and menacing form. A secret society called the Loyal Nine — artisans, merchants, and tradesmen — organised public protests against the Stamp Act. On the morning of 14 August 1765, an effigy of Andrew Oliver, the Massachusetts stamp distributor, was found hanging from a large elm tree on the Boston Common — the tree that would become famous as the Liberty Tree. That evening, a mob stormed Oliver’s office, demolished his building, and hanged his effigy. Oliver resigned his commission the next day.

On the night of 26 August, the violence escalated. A mob attacked the homes of William Story, Deputy Register of the Vice-Admiralty Court, and Benjamin Hallowell, the Comptroller of Customs. They then turned on the house of Thomas Hutchinson, the Lieutenant-Governor and Chief Justice of Massachusetts — a man who had privately opposed the Stamp Act but was determined to enforce the law. The mob gutted Hutchinson’s mansion, destroyed his furniture, scattered his papers and manuscript history of Massachusetts, and carried off his money and plate. Hutchinson barely escaped with his life.

The Loyal Nine evolved into the Sons of Liberty, a network of resistance organisations that would coordinate colonial opposition over the next decade. Similar disturbances broke out in cities from Portsmouth, New Hampshire, to Charleston, South Carolina.

The General Attack on the Stamp Distributors

Throughout the colonies, the appointed stamp distributors were forced to resign. Those who declined to resign faced mob violence. In New York, the stamp distributor James McEvers resigned within weeks of receiving his commission. In Rhode Island, the stamps were stored in a fortress but never distributed. In Maryland, the distributor was rescued from a mob by the sheriff and promptly resigned. Belsham records the scene:

“Those who had accepted commissions to act as distributors of stamps, were forced by public oath to renounce all concern in them at this or any future juncture; and in some instances the houses of those who were most obnoxious were demolished, and their effects plundered or destroyed.”8

The justices of the peace in many parts gave public notice “that they would never, by acting in that capacity, be instrumental to the subversion of their country’s liberties.” The gentlemen of the law, “fired by the same patriotic enthusiasm, universally renounced, in the exercise of their profession, the use of the British stamps.”9

The Stamp Act Congress

By the autumn of 1765, the colonial response had moved from spontaneous violence to organised political resistance. At the invitation of the Massachusetts House of Representatives, delegates from nine colonies — Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, and South Carolina — assembled in New York City in October 1765. This was the Stamp Act Congress, the first inter-colonial meeting ever convened in America.

The Congress met from 7 to 25 October 1765 at City Hall in New York. After extended debate — the more conservative delegates from the middle colonies restraining the fiercer spirits from Massachusetts and Virginia — the Congress adopted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances, composed of fourteen resolutions drafted largely by John Dickinson of Pennsylvania. The Declaration affirmed the colonists’ allegiance to the Crown and their “all due subordination” to Parliament, but insisted that the colonists possessed all the rights of Englishmen, that the right of taxation could only be exercised by representatives chosen by the people, and that trial by jury was a fundamental right that could not be infringed. Belsham records that the delegates “unanimously concurred in framing and publishing a sort of manifesto expressive of their grievances.”10

The Congress also petitioned the King and both Houses of Parliament for the repeal of the Stamp Act and the other revenue measures of the Grenville ministry.

Non-Importation and Economic Resistance

The most potent weapon of colonial resistance proved to be economic. In October 1765, merchants in New York City agreed to stop importing British goods until the Stamp Act was repealed. By December, merchants in Boston, Philadelphia, and other major ports had followed suit. The agreements were enforced by the Sons of Liberty, who published the names of merchants who continued to import British goods and, in some cases, intimidated them into compliance.

The effect on British trade was immediate and devastating. Belsham records that “the most alarming opposition was made by the merchants, who entered into solemn engagements not to import any more goods from Great Britain till the Stamp Act was repealed.”11 British exports to the colonies, which had been worth over £2 million annually, fell sharply. Petitions from the principal commercial cities of the kingdom — London, Bristol, Liverpool, Glasgow — began to pour into the House of Commons, demanding repeal.

By 1 November 1765, the date on which the Act was to take effect, “not a sheet of stamped paper was to be found throughout the Colonies; so that all business which could not be legally carried on without stamps was entirely at a stand.” Everywhere, “the courts of justice were shut, and the ports closed.”12 In Boston, the ships in the harbour hung out their colours half-mast high as a signal of deepest distress; the bells of the churches throughout the city, “being muffled, rang out a dumb peal.” The Act itself, as soon as it came from the King’s printing-house, “was burnt by the populace, with the effigies of the men most active in passing it.”13

The British Debate

The Fall of Grenville

The news of the American disturbances reached London in the autumn of 1765. By that time, however, the political landscape in Britain had changed. George III, who had endured months of Grenville’s lecturing and condescension — the minister treated the King, Belsham suggests, with something approaching insolence — had dismissed Grenville in July 1765 after the minister demanded the removal of all officials connected with the Earl of Bute. The King turned to the Marquis of Rockingham, a young Whig grandee of thirty-five, to form a ministry.

Rockingham’s ministry was composed of the great Whig families — the Cavendishes, the Bentincks, the Watsons-Wentworths — who had been excluded from power since the fall of the Duke of Newcastle. It was a ministry of amateurs and idealists, held together by the conviction that the Grenville system of American taxation was a disastrous mistake. The guiding intellectual force of the ministry was Edmund Burke, a young Irishman of dazzling abilities who served as Rockingham’s private secretary and who would become the great philosopher of the Whig party.

The Great Debates of 1766

Parliament convened on 14 January 1766, and the Stamp Act crisis immediately dominated its proceedings. The King’s speech expressed “firm confidence in their wisdom and zeal” to find resolutions that would “at once preserve the constitutional rights of the British Legislature over the Colonies, and to restore to them that harmony and tranquillity which have lately been interrupted by disorders of the most dangerous nature.”14

The great debate opened in the House of Commons when William Pitt the Elder — who had been absent from Parliament during the passage of the Stamp Act, lying ill in bed — rose to speak. His speech was the most celebrated of the entire crisis. He denounced every capital measure taken by the late ministry as entirely wrong, declared that Parliament had “NO RIGHT to lay a tax upon the Colonies,” and drew a sharp distinction between Parliament’s sovereign authority over commerce and legislation and its lack of authority over internal taxation:

“Taxation is no part of the governing or legislative power; the taxes are a voluntary gift and grant of the Commons alone… When in this House we give and grant, therefore, we give and grant what is our own; but can we give and grant the property of the Commons of America? It is an absurdity in terms. There is an idea in some, that the Colonies are virtually represented in this House. I would fain know by whom? The idea of virtual representation is the most contemptible that ever entered into the head of a man: it does not deserve a serious refutation.”15

Pitt went further: he defended the American resistance itself:

“Sir, I REJOICE that America has resisted; three millions of people so dead to all the feelings of liberty, as voluntarily to submit to be slaves, would have been fit instruments to make slaves of all the rest.”16

He concluded with a peroration that echoes through English constitutional history:

“It is my opinion, ‘That the Stamp Act be repealed ABSOLUTELY, TOTALLY, and IMMEDIATELY.‘”17

Grenville rose in reply, defending his measure with “all the systematic obstinacy of a sincere but cloudy and narrow mind.” He argued that taxation was “a branch of the sovereign power” that had been exercised over those who were never represented — the East India Company, chartered corporations, the Palatinate of Chester and the Bishopric of Durham before they sent representatives to Parliament. He warned that the American tumults bordered on rebellion and that if Pitt’s doctrines were confirmed, the colonists “would lose this name to take that of revolution.” He concluded with a bitter indictment of American ingratitude:

“The nation has run itself into an immense debt to give them protection; bounties have been extended to them… and now they are called upon to contribute a small share towards the public expence, they renounce your authority, insult your officers, and break out, I might almost say, into open rebellion.”18

Pitt, “fired with disdain and resentment,” immediately rose to reply. He was called to order by Lord Strange for speaking twice — but the House, crying “Go on,” allowed him to continue. He denounced Grenville’s accusation that gentlemen in the House had given birth to sedition in America: “The freedom with which they have spoken their sentiments against this unhappy Act, is imputed to them as a crime; but the imputation shall not discourage me.” He declared that Grenville “ought to have desisted from his project” and that the true blame lay with the minister who had passed the Act, not with those who now opposed it.19

Between Pitt’s absolute denial of Parliament’s right to tax and Grenville’s claim of unlimited parliamentary sovereignty, a middle ground was carved by the Marquis of Rockingham and his ministry. Rockingham’s position — the position that ultimately prevailed — was that the Stamp Act should be repealed as a matter of policy and prudence, while Parliament’s ultimate sovereignty over the colonies should be affirmed in the strongest possible terms. This was the logic of the Declaratory Act.

The petitions from the commercial cities were laid before Parliament. The late ministers and their partisans, Belsham records, “falsely and ridiculously represented” these petitions as “the effects of ministerial artifice,” exclaiming that “the distress of the mercantile part of the community be however what it may, it ought to be submitted to, rather than by a repeal of the act to hazard the total loss of the just superiority of Great Britain over the Colonies.”20

The Repeal and the Declaratory Act

After months of fierce debate, the Rockingham ministry carried the day. A bill for the repeal of the Stamp Act was brought in and, “after very vehement opposition,” passed both Houses by considerable majorities, receiving the royal assent on 18 March 1766.

But on the same day, Parliament passed the Declaratory Act — a measure that, in retrospect, was far more significant than the repeal itself. The Act declared that Parliament “had, hath, and of right ought to have, full power and authority to make laws and statutes of sufficient force and validity to bind the colonies and people of America, subjects of the crown of Great Britain, in all cases whatsoever.”

The conjunction of repeal and declaratory assertion was the essence of the Rockingham compromise: withdraw the offensive measure, but assert the principle on which it rested. Belsham’s judgment on the Declaratory Act is subtle:

“The Declaratory Act, however theoretically disputable, served to display their solicitude to sustain the honor of the nation.”21

The joy in America on receiving news of the repeal was extreme. Belsham records that the colonies were largely untroubled by the Declaratory Act: the mere declaration of “an abstract right, which they imagined to be with respect to the claim of taxation in fact relinquished, and which seemed chiefly intended as a salvo for the honor of Parliament, gave them little apprehension or uneasiness.”22 It was only years later, after a long series of vexations and encroachments on their established privileges, that the Assembly of Massachusetts formally voted the Declaratory Act to be a grievance.

The repeal was accompanied by a circular letter from Henry Seymour Conway, the Secretary of State, to the colonial governors, written “in that firm and temperate style which was at once calculated to maintain the honor of Government, and to appease the discontents and apprehensions of the People.” Conway particularly insisted that full compensation be made to those “who, from the madness of the people, have suffered for their deference to the Acts of the British Legislature.”23

Key Figures

George III (1738–1820)

King of Great Britain and Ireland. George III had supported Grenville’s American policy and believed the colonies ought to contribute to the costs of empire. He assented to the repeal reluctantly — his private conviction was that the Stamp Act was a legitimate exercise of parliamentary authority and that repeal would be seen as weakness. His personal relations with Grenville had soured badly by July 1765, but his reservations about Rockingham were almost equally strong: the new ministry contained too many of the old Whig families he distrusted. The King’s consistent position throughout the crisis was that Parliament’s authority over the colonies was absolute — a position he would maintain for the rest of his reign.

George Grenville (1712–1770)

Prime Minister from 1763 to 1765 and the author of the Stamp Act. Grenville was a man of “a sincere but cloudy and narrow mind” — an able administrator who was nevertheless “utterly destitute of those commanding talents” required of a first minister.24 His Stamp Act was, in Belsham’s judgment, the product of a narrow fiscal logic that took no account of the constitutional sensibilities it would affront. Grenville never held office again after his dismissal in July 1765. He opposed the repeal in the 1766 debates, arguing with bitterness that it would be seen as a surrender, and spent his remaining years in impotent opposition.

William Pitt the Elder (1708–1778)

The great wartime leader, now in opposition, whose speeches in the 1766 repeal debates were among the most celebrated of his career. Pitt drew the sharpest constitutional line of any British statesman: Parliament had sovereign authority over the colonies in all matters of legislation and commerce, but it had no right to tax them. His distinction between internal and external taxation — however logically questionable — became the constitutional foundation on which the colonial case rested. His declaration that he “rejoiced” America had resisted became a watchword of the American cause. Pitt returned to office in July 1766 as Lord Privy Seal and Earl of Chatham, but his health was already failing, and his second ministry was a disaster.

Charles Watson-Wentworth, 2nd Marquis of Rockingham (1730–1782)

The young Whig nobleman who formed a ministry in July 1765 and carried the repeal of the Stamp Act. Rockingham was not a man of brilliant intellect — Edmund Burke called him “a man of great honour and integrity” but not of commanding abilities — but he had the good sense to surround himself with talented advisors. His ministry’s achievement was the repeal of the Stamp Act; its flaw was the Declaratory Act, which affirmed a principle that made future conflict inevitable. Rockingham fell from power in July 1766 when Pitt abandoned him.

Edmund Burke (1729–1797)

The Irish-born philosopher and politician who served as Rockingham’s private secretary during the crisis and who emerged, in the course of the Stamp Act debates, as the most powerful intellect in the Whig party. Burke’s thinking on America — developed fully in his later speeches and writings — was grounded in the conviction that the colonies should be governed by conciliation rather than coercion, and that Britain’s imperial authority rested ultimately on the consent of the governed. His influence on Rockingham’s policy was profound, though his greatest contributions lay ahead.

Patrick Henry (1736–1799)

The young Virginia lawyer and orator whose resolutions in the House of Burgesses in May 1765 inaugurated the colonial resistance to the Stamp Act. Henry’s speech — in which he is said to have cried “Caesar had his Brutus, Charles the First his Cromwell, and George the Third…” before being called to order, to which he replied “…may profit by their example” — became the founding legend of American revolutionary oratory.

Charles Townshend (1725–1767)

President of the Board of Trade under Grenville and later, as Chancellor of the Exchequer, the author of the Townshend Duties. In the 1766 debates, Townshend took a characteristically complex position: nominally supporting the Rockingham government’s policy of repeal, he made speeches that seemed to concede the Americans’ position while simultaneously embracing the claim of unlimited parliamentary sovereignty. His performances baffled friend and foe alike.

Henry Seymour Conway (1721–1795)

Secretary of State for the Southern Department in the Rockingham ministry. Conway had distinguished himself in the 1765 parliamentary debates by protesting alone against the passage of the Stamp Act. As Secretary of State, he oversaw the communication of the repeal to the colonies and drafted the conciliatory circular letter to the colonial governors. He was one of the few British statesmen of the era who consistently advocated a conciliatory policy toward America.

Significance

The Stamp Act Crisis was the pivotal moment in the disintegration of the first British Empire. It established the constitutional framework within which the American Revolution would be fought: Parliament claimed unlimited sovereignty; the colonies claimed that their assemblies were co-ordinate legislatures with exclusive authority over internal taxation. The crisis settled nothing.

The repeal, so joyfully received in America, was in fact the beginning of the tragedy. The Americans believed they had won a permanent concession on the principle of representation; the British believed they had, through the Declaratory Act, preserved the principle of sovereignty while tactically retreating on a particular measure. Both sides were wrong, and neither understood the position of the other. As Belsham observed, the repeal produced “all the happy and beneficial effects which had been previously expected or predicted” — but only temporarily. Within eighteen months, Charles Townshend would rekindle the crisis with a new set of duties, and the Declaratory Act would ensure that the ensuing conflict would be fought not over a particular tax but over the nature of the empire itself.25

Belsham, writing from the perspective of the 1790s, saw the Stamp Act crisis as the first step on a road that led inevitably to American independence. His judgment on Grenville is severe but revealing:

“Little, certainly, did this Minister imagine how fertile would be the invention of his successors in office, or how thoroughly subdued by time and custom the spirit of the people.”26
