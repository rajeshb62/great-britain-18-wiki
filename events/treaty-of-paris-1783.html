Peace of Paris / Treaty of Paris (1783)

The Treaty of Paris, signed on 3 September 1783, was the definitive settlement that ended the American War of Independence between the Kingdom of Great Britain, the newly-recognised United States of America, the Kingdom of France, the Kingdom of Spain, and the Dutch Republic. Together with the Treaty of Versailles of the same date (between Britain, France, and Spain), it constituted the Peace of Paris — a comprehensive reordering of the Atlantic world that marked the terminus of the first British Empire and the birth of the American republic.

The treaty was the product of eighteen months of complex, overlapping, and frequently duplicitous negotiations in Paris, conducted against the backdrop of domestic political upheaval in London: the fall of Lord North’s ministry (March 1782), the brief and tragic Rockingham ministry (March–July 1782), and the unstable Shelburne ministry that followed (July 1782 – February 1783). It was signed by three American commissioners — Benjamin Franklin, John Jay, and John Adams — and by the British negotiators Richard Oswald and later David Hartley, under the guidance of the French foreign minister, the Comte de Vergennes.

By its terms, Great Britain formally recognised the independence of the thirteen United States, established the Mississippi River as the western boundary of the new republic, ceded Florida to Spain, and made territorial concessions in the Caribbean, Africa, and the Mediterranean to France and Spain. For Britain, it was a treaty of necessity rather than choice — the formal acknowledgement of a defeat that had been military since Yorktown, political since the fall of North, and strategic since the entry of France and Spain into the war in 1778–1779.

Background: From Yorktown to the Fall of North

The Catastrophe of Yorktown

The military road to the Peace of Paris began at the Siege of Yorktown (28 September – 19 October 1781), where Lieutenant General Lord Cornwallis surrendered his entire army of 7,000 British and Hessian troops to the combined Franco-American forces under George Washington and the Comte de Rochambeau. The surrender effectively ended major combat operations in North America. When the news reached London in late November 1781, Lord North was famously said to have cried out, “Oh God! It is all over!” — a recognition that the war, after six years of bloodshed and expenditure, was irretrievably lost.

The Fall of Lord North (March 1782)

The parliamentary session that opened on 27 November 1781 became the stage for the destruction of the North ministry. The Opposition — led by Charles James Fox, Edmund Burke, and the younger William Pitt — launched a relentless assault on the government’s conduct of the war. On 22 February 1782, General Henry Conway moved an address to the King to end the offensive war in America; the ministry survived by a single vote — 193 to 192. On 27 February, Conway’s renewed motion was carried after the House rejected a ministerial substitute. On 8 March, Lord John Cavendish’s censure resolutions were defeated by only ten votes. The handwriting was on the wall.

On 19 March 1782, Lord North rose in the House of Commons and announced that His Majesty had determined upon “an entire change of administration.” The twelve-year North ministry — the longest of George III’s reign — was dissolved. “To the inexpressible joy of all ranks and orders of people,” Belsham records, this “famous, or, to use a term more characteristically appropriate, this infamous Administration” came to an end.

The Rockingham Ministry (March – July 1782)

North was succeeded by the Marquis of Rockingham, returned to power after sixteen years in opposition. Rockingham assembled a ministry that represented a coalition of Whig factions: the Rockinghamite Whigs proper, led by the Marquis himself and including Edmund Burke (Paymaster of the Forces) and the Duke of Richmond (Master-General of the Ordnance); and the Shelburne Whigs, led by the Earl of Shelburne, who took the Home and Colonial Secretaryship, with responsibility for the American negotiations.

Charles James Fox — the most brilliant parliamentary orator of the age — became Foreign Secretary, with oversight of relations with France, Spain, and the other European powers. The division of responsibility was inherently unstable: Shelburne handled the American talks from the Home Office, while Fox handled the European talks from the Foreign Office, and the two men detested one another. Shelburne was a philosophic reformer, a disciple of Adam Smith and the Earl of Chatham, given to speculation and reserve. Fox was a man of passion and eloquence, a gambler and a bon vivant, the idol of the Whig rank-and-file. They could barely speak to one another without quarrelling.

Rockingham himself might have held the ministry together, but he died suddenly on 1 July 1782, after only three months in office. The King, who detested Fox as a personal enemy and a former ally of the detested Lord North, immediately offered the Treasury to Shelburne. Fox refused to serve under Shelburne and resigned; his followers, including Burke, followed him into opposition. The Rockingham ministry had broken apart on the question of who would succeed Rockingham, and the stage was set for a quarrel that would shape the peace negotiations themselves.

The Shelburne Ministry (July 1782 – February 1783)

Lord Shelburne became First Lord of the Treasury in July 1782, at the head of a much weaker government than Rockingham’s. His ministry comprised the Shelburne Whigs and the remnants of the King’s friends, but it lacked the broad Parliamentary support that Rockingham had commanded. Shelburne himself was widely mistrusted — “the Jesuit of Berkeley Square,” his enemies called him — and he faced a formidable opposition coalition that was already taking shape: Fox and his Rockinghamites, joined now by the displaced Lord North and his followers, united by nothing but their shared hatred of Shelburne.

Nevertheless, Shelburne pressed forward with the peace negotiations. His policy was pragmatic and strategic: recognise American independence quickly and generously, split the Franco-American alliance, and salvage what could be salvaged for Britain in the Caribbean, India, Africa, and Europe. It was the policy of a man who understood that the loss of America was a strategic fact to be managed, not a wound to be mourned.

The Negotiations in Paris (April 1782 – September 1783)

The Cast of Characters

On the American side, the Congress had appointed five commissioners to negotiate the peace: Benjamin Franklin, John Jay, John Adams, Henry Laurens, and Thomas Jefferson (who did not serve, remaining in America). The effective negotiators were the three who arrived in Paris:

  • Benjamin Franklin — the venerable philosopher-diplomat, already in Paris as the American Minister to France since 1776, aged 76, the intellectual godfather of the American Revolution, and the man with the closest personal relationship to the French court. Franklin was inclined to trust the French and to work within the framework of the Franco-American alliance.
  • John Jay — the New York lawyer and former President of the Continental Congress, aged 37, deeply suspicious of French motives and convinced that Vergennes was secretly working to keep America weak and dependent.
  • John Adams — the Massachusetts lawyer and former commissioner to France, aged 47, who had been in the Netherlands negotiating a Dutch loan and recognition; he arrived in Paris in October 1782 with a monumental distrust of both Franklin and the French.

On the British side, the negotiations were initially conducted by Richard Oswald, a Scottish merchant and friend of Henry Laurens, who was appointed by Shelburne in April 1782. Oswald was later joined and ultimately replaced by David Hartley, a Member of Parliament and friend of Franklin.

On the French side, the negotiations were overseen by the Comte de Vergennes (Charles Gravier, Comte de Vergennes), the French foreign minister since 1774. Vergennes was the most experienced diplomat in Europe — calculating, patient, and ruthless in pursuit of French interests. His strategy was to keep the war going just long enough to extract maximum concessions from Britain in Europe and the colonies, while binding the Americans to France as dependent allies.

The First Phase: Recognition of Independence (April – November 1782)

The negotiations proceeded in two tracks: the British-American track, managed by Shelburne and Oswald, and the British-French-Spanish track, managed by Fox’s successor as Foreign Secretary (and subsequently by the whole Cabinet). The tracks were formally separate but in practice deeply entangled.

From the outset, Shelburne made a critical concession: he was prepared to recognise American independence before the final treaty, as a preliminary to negotiations rather than as a result of them. This was a radical departure from the position of the North ministry, which had refused even to negotiate with the Americans except as rebellious subjects. On 28 April 1782, Oswald was instructed to treat with the American commissioners for a general peace, and on 21 September, Shelburne authorised Oswald to negotiate on the basis of American independence.

The American commissioners, however, were not satisfied with a preliminary promise. They insisted on explicit recognition of independence as a precondition for discussing other terms. After three months of haggling, the British finally conceded. On 30 November 1782, the British and American negotiators signed the Preliminary Articles of Peace, Article I of which declared that “His Britannic Majesty acknowledges the said United States … to be free, sovereign and independent States.”

The signing of the preliminary articles was a masterstroke of American diplomacy — and a betrayal of France. The instructions of the Continental Congress had required the American commissioners to “make the most candid confidential communications” to the French minister and to “undertake nothing in the negotiations for peace or truce without their knowledge and concurrence.” Franklin, Jay, and Adams signed the preliminary articles without consulting Vergennes, without informing him of the terms, and without his approval.

When Vergennes discovered what had happened, he was furious. He wrote to Franklin that the Americans had “signed a treaty with Great Britain without communicating it to us” — a breach of the Franco-American alliance of 1778. Franklin responded with characteristic diplomacy, and the crisis passed, but the Franco-American relationship was never the same. The American commissioners had calculated that the British offer was too good to risk by waiting for the French, and that Vergennes, with war-weariness and financial exhaustion pressing on France, would accept a fait accompli rather than rupture the alliance entirely. They were right.

The Definitive Treaty (3 September 1783)

The preliminary articles were to serve as the basis for a definitive treaty, to be signed when Britain also concluded peace with France and Spain. Those European treaties were signed on the same day — 3 September 1783 — in an elaborate diplomatic choreography at the Hôtel d’York in Paris.

The definitive Treaty of Paris between Great Britain and the United States consisted of a preamble and ten articles. It was signed by David Hartley (on the British side, Oswald having been replaced) and by Adams, Franklin, and Jay (on the American side). On the same day, Britain signed the Treaty of Versailles with France at Versailles, and the Treaty of Versailles with Spain at the same location — the documents that collectively gave the Atlantic world a new map.

The Anglo-French Treaty

By the separate Treaty of Versailles between Britain and France, signed on 3 September 1783:

  • France gained: the island of Tobago in the West Indies, and the island of Gorée and the territory of Senegal in West Africa.
  • France restored to Britain: the islands of Grenada, St. Vincent, Dominica, St. Christopher (St. Kitts), Nevis, and Montserrat — most of the French conquests in the West Indies during the war.
  • France recovered: the ports of St. Pierre and Miquelon off Newfoundland (fishing stations) and the trading post of Chandernagore in Bengal, with minor settlements in India.
  • The Newfoundland fisheries: the French retained their existing fishing rights on the Grand Banks and under the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), with modifications.
  • Dunkirk: the fortifications, long a point of contention, were restored to their pre-war state.

Vergennes had not achieved the crushing humiliation of Britain that France had hoped for. The French war effort, launched in 1778, had been enormously expensive — some one billion livres — and had brought France to the verge of bankruptcy without corresponding territorial gains. The French court had shed blood and treasure to humble its ancient rival, and the result, while not negligible, was far from the decisive blow of 1763 in reverse. The financial strain of the American war would prove to be a direct cause of the French Revolution.

The Anglo-Spanish Treaty

By the separate Treaty of Versailles between Britain and Spain:

  • Spain gained: East Florida and West Florida — the entire Florida peninsula and the Gulf coast from the Apalachicola River to the Mississippi — restoring Spanish dominion to territory lost in 1763.
  • Spain gained: the island of Minorca in the Mediterranean, captured from Britain in 1782 after a seven-month siege, ceded fully to Spain.
  • Spain restored to Britain: the Bahamas and the island of Providence (captured by the Spanish in 1782).
  • Gibraltar: the great prize that Spain had fought hardest to recover remained British. The epic defence of Gibraltar by General George Augustus Eliott from 1779 to 1783 had held, despite the famous combined French-Spanish assault of September 1782; the treaty confirmed British sovereignty. Spain had to content itself with Florida and Minorca as consolation.

The Anglo-Dutch Treaty

Peace with the Dutch Republic was concluded later, in May 1784. The Dutch, who had joined the war on the French side in 1780, surrendered the port of Negapatam in India to Britain.

Parliamentary Debate and the Fall of Shelburne (February 1783)

The preliminary articles were laid before the British Parliament on 27 January 1783. The debate that followed was one of the most bitter and consequential in eighteenth-century British history.

The Attack on Shelburne

The Opposition — now a formidable coalition of Fox’s Rockingham Whigs and North’s followers — attacked the preliminary articles as a disgraceful capitulation. The criticisms centred on several points:

  1. American independence granted too lightly: Shelburne had recognised American independence immediately, without extracting any counter-concession. Fox and North argued that independence should have been bartered for guarantees for the Loyalists or commercial advantages.

  2. The Loyalists abandoned: The precarious “recommendation” in Article V, by which Congress was only to “earnestly recommend” the restoration of Loyalist property to the state legislatures, was denounced as worthless. Fox declared that the Loyalists had been “basely betrayed” by a ministry that professed to care for them.

  3. The boundaries too generous: The Mississippi River boundary and the inclusion of the trans-Appalachian territory in the United States conceded too much. The Opposition argued that Britain should have insisted on an Indian barrier state — a buffer between the United States and Canada to protect both the Native American allies and the Canadian provinces.

  4. The fisheries compromise: The American fishermen were confirmed in their pre-war fishing rights on the Grand Banks, a concession that the Newfoundland and Nova Scotia merchants and the Opposition alike condemned as handing over a national resource.

  5. The European treaties too weak: France had recovered its West African posts and Tobago, Spain had gained Florida and Minorca, and Britain had gained nothing commensurate in return. The Opposition accused Shelburne of having negotiated from weakness and of having surrendered the substance of victory for the shadow of peace.

Shelburne’s Defence

Shelburne defended his treaty on strategic grounds. He argued that the war was already lost at Yorktown and that the choice was not between a better peace and a worse one, but between peace on the terms offered and the indefinite continuation of a ruinously expensive war. Every additional month of war cost Britain over a million pounds and risked further losses — in the West Indies, in India, or in Europe. The treaty:

  • Split the Franco-American alliance by giving the Americans a generous separate peace
  • Left Britain with Canada, Nova Scotia, and the Newfoundland fisheries largely intact
  • Preserved Gibraltar
  • Restored most of the West Indian conquests to Britain
  • Established a framework for commercial relations with the United States that Shelburne hoped would be mutually beneficial

Shelburne also looked to the future: he had already begun planning a liberal commercial treaty with the United States that would have created a free-trade zone across the Atlantic, a project cut short by his fall.

The Defeat of the Ministry

The debate was carried on through January and February 1783 with extraordinary ferocity. Fox and North, the most implacable enemies of the previous decade, now sat together on the Opposition benches — an alliance so unnatural that it was widely denounced as “the Coalition.” Burke called it a coalition of “the two rotten parties of the state.” But the Coalition commanded a majority.

On 17 February 1783, the House of Commons voted on the preliminary articles. The ministry was defeated by a wide margin — 224 votes to 208. On 24 February, a direct motion of censure against Shelburne’s peace preliminaries was carried 207 to 190. Shelburne resigned later that week, having been in office barely seven months.

“Never was a treaty so obnoxious to Parliament peaceably concluded,” wrote the annual register. Shelburne himself, in a famous speech, declared that he had made the best peace he could and that posterity would judge him more kindly than his contemporaries.

The Fox-North Coalition (April 1783)

Shelburne’s resignation produced a constitutional crisis. The King, after failing to persuade William Pitt the Younger to accept the Treasury, was forced to accept the formation of a ministry headed by the Duke of Portland, with Charles James Fox and Lord North as Secretaries of State — the Fox-North Coalition (April – December 1783). The Coalition, unnatural as it was, presided over the final ratification of the Peace of Paris. The Definitive Treaty was signed on 3 September, and the instruments of ratification were exchanged in Paris on 12 May 1784, after the Coalition had itself fallen and William Pitt the Younger had assumed power.

Aftermath and Consequences

The End of the First British Empire

The Treaty of Paris (1783) is conventionally taken as the terminus of the first British Empire — the empire of settlement and commerce in North America that had been built since the early seventeenth century. Between 1763 and 1783, Britain had conquered an empire in North America and then lost the most populous and prosperous part of it. The loss of the thirteen colonies was a demographic and economic catastrophe: the new United States contained some 2.5 million people, roughly one-third of the white English-speaking population of the Atlantic world. The tax base, the trade, and the strategic depth that the colonies had provided were gone.

The Loyalist Crisis

No issue was more painful or more contentious than the fate of the American Loyalists — the colonists who had remained faithful to the Crown during the war. The treaty’s Article V, which merely “recommended” their restoration to the state legislatures, was understood by everyone as a dead letter. The states ignored it. Loyalists were subjected to confiscation, exile, and violence. Some 60,000 to 80,000 Loyalists fled the United States for British territory after the war:

  • Approximately 30,000 settled in Nova Scotia (including the newly-created province of New Brunswick, carved out for their benefit in 1784)
  • Some 10,000 went to the Province of Quebec (later Upper Canada)
  • Several thousand went to the Bahamas and the West Indies
  • Others returned to Britain, where they were granted pensions and compensation from a Loyalist Claims Commission established by Parliament

The British government ultimately paid over £3 million in compensation to Loyalist claimants — a substantial sum that testified both to the scale of the Loyalist sacrifice and to Britain’s sense of obligation. But the bitterness on both sides was enduring. Belsham, writing a decade later, records that “the recommendation of the treaty proved nugatory and useless; and the unfortunate loyalists were left to the mercy of their exasperated countrymen.”

The Massive Loss of Prestige

For a nation that had stood at the summit of European power in 1763, the loss of the American colonies was a staggering blow to prestige. Britain had fought the war in alliance with no European ally of consequence; it had been defeated by a combination of its own colonists, the French, the Spanish, and the Dutch. The Royal Navy, the foundation of British power, had been humbled at times — the Chesapeake had been lost to de Grasse, the English Channel had seen the combined Franco-Spanish fleet in 1779, Minorca had fallen. The war had cost Britain over £100 million and added £250 million to the national debt.

Yet the defeat was not as absolute as it first appeared. Canada remained British. The West Indian sugar islands, the economic jewel of the empire, were largely restored. Gibraltar had held. India was secure. British trade with the United States recovered rapidly after the war and soon exceeded pre-war levels. And within two decades, Britain would be at war with Revolutionary France, leading a European coalition and building a second empire in India, Africa, and the Pacific.

The Road to 1789

For France, the Peace of Paris was a far more ambiguous outcome. Vergennes had achieved his primary goal — the humiliation of Britain and the vindication of France’s role as a great power — but the cost had been enormous. The one billion livres spent on the American war pushed the French monarchy into bankruptcy, forcing Louis XVI to summon the Estates-General in 1789 and setting in motion the French Revolution. The Treaty of Paris, in this sense, was not only the end of the first British Empire but the beginning of the end of the ancien régime.

The Treaty’s Legacy

The Treaty of Paris (1783) established the fundamental geography of the United States — the Mississippi River boundary, the Great Lakes frontier, the Florida border — that would endure for decades, modified only by the Louisiana Purchase (1803) and the later annexation of Florida (1819). It set the terms of Anglo-American relations for a generation, a relationship characterised by mutual suspicion punctuated by further crises (the Northwest posts dispute, the impressment controversy, the War of 1812). It was the first modern peace treaty in which a European power formally recognised the independence of a colonial rebellion, setting a precedent that would reverberate across the Americas in the decades that followed.

For Britain, the Peace of Paris was a moment of painful but necessary reckoning. The old colonial system — the mercantilist framework of the Navigation Acts, the taxing authority of Parliament over the colonies — had been repudiated by force. The empire that survived would be built on different foundations: free trade, colonial self-government, and, eventually, the second British Empire of India, Africa, and the Pacific. Belsham, reflecting on the peace, wrote that “the only consolation which the friends of Britain could derive from the contemplation of this humiliating treaty was, that it terminated a war equally unjust, impolitic, and calamitous.”

Key Figures

  • George III: The King whose unyielding insistence on the sovereignty of Parliament over the colonies had driven the conflict, and who was forced by the collapse of the North ministry to accept both the independence of the United States and the humiliation of the peace. He contemplated abdication in March 1783.
  • Lord Shelburne: The prime minister who negotiated the peace and who fell from power for his pains. His vision of a liberal commercial relationship with the United States was ahead of its time; his political reputation never recovered from the charge of having given away too much.
  • Charles James Fox: The great Opposition orator who denounced the peace as a betrayal and then, in the Fox-North Coalition, presided over its ratification. His decision to ally with North destroyed his reputation among many reformers and kept him out of power for most of the next two decades.
  • Lord North: The former prime minister who had lost America and who, in a bizarre turn of political fortune, became Foreign Secretary under Fox in the Coalition that ratified the peace he had failed to prevent.
  • Benjamin Franklin: The American negotiator whose combination of diplomatic skill and scientific celebrity made him the most effective American diplomat of the age. His role in both securing the French alliance and then breaking free of it to achieve a separate peace was masterful.
  • John Jay: The American commissioner whose suspicion of Vergennes and insistence on direct Anglo-American negotiations shaped the final treaty. He refused to accept the Spanish claim to the Mississippi navigation and held firm for the western boundary.
  • John Adams: The third American commissioner, who arrived late to the negotiations but whose sharp legal mind was essential in drafting the treaty’s terms.
  • Comte de Vergennes: The French foreign minister who orchestrated the Franco-American alliance, spent a billion livres to humble Britain, and was outmanoeuvred by the Americans in the final negotiations. The financial consequences of his policy destroyed the monarchy he served.
  • William Pitt the Younger: Rockingham’s Chancellor of the Exchequer in 1782, Shelburne’s Chancellor in 1782–1783, and the future prime minister who would rebuild British finances and British power in the decade after the peace. His refusal to join the Fox-North Coalition preserved his political future.
