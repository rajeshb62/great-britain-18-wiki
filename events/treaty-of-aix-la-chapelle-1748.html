Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)

The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, signed on 18 October 1748, was the peace settlement that ended the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748). It was a treaty of status quo ante bellum — a mutual restitution of conquests that restored the pre-war territorial status quo while leaving the fundamental questions that had provoked the war entirely unresolved. Widely regarded even at the time as a truce rather than a final peace, the treaty satisfied none of the principal belligerents and set the stage for the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 and the Seven Years’ War (1756–1763).

The negotiations were conducted at the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle (March–October 1748), with the Earl of Sandwich (John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich) and Sir Thomas Robinson acting as British plenipotentiaries, and the comte de Saint-Séverin representing France. The central bargain was a general restoration of all conquests — a solution that infuriated British colonists who had captured Louisbourg in 1745, gratified Frederick the Great who kept Silesia, and left Maria Theresa nursing a grievance that would reshape the alliances of Europe.

Background: The Exhaustion of the Combatants

By 1747, the War of the Austrian Succession had run for seven years with no decisive result. The war had expanded far beyond its original issue of Maria Theresa’s succession to the Habsburg dominions, embracing the separate conflicts of the War of Jenkins’ Ear (1739–1748) between Britain and Spain, the Jacobite Rising of 1745 in Britain, and the Italian campaigns of Spain and Austria.

The military situation on the continent was unfavourable to the allies. Marechal de Saxe, the greatest French commander of the age, had won a brilliant series of victories in Flanders — Fontenoy (1745), Raucoux (1746), and Lauffeld (1747) — and had overrun the Austrian Netherlands. The Duke of Cumberland, despite his victory at Culloden (1746), had proved no match for Saxe in open battle. By 1747, the French had penetrated into Dutch Brabant and Flanders, and the Dutch Republic was in real danger of invasion.

Yet France, too, was exhausted. The war had been ruinously expensive; the French navy had been driven from the seas; and the British blockade had crippled French commerce. Britain, for its part, had seen its Jacobite rebellion crushed, its continental campaigns stalled, and its colonial war in India produce mixed results — Madras had fallen to the French under La Bourdonnais in 1746, while Louisbourg on Cape Breton Island had been captured by New England colonial forces under William Pepperrell and Commodore Peter Warren in 1745.

Neither side could deliver a knockout blow, and both faced mounting domestic pressure for peace. In the winter of 1746–1747, Louis XV made direct and repeated advances of accommodation to the courts of London and The Hague.

The Congress at Aix-la-Chapelle (March–October 1748)

The congress opened in March 1748 at the free imperial city of Aix-la-Chapelle (modern Aachen). The chief negotiators were:

  • Earl of Sandwich (John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich) and Sir Thomas Robinson — British plenipotentiaries
  • Comte de Saint-Séverin (Alphonse Marie Louis, comte de Saint-Séverin d’Aragon) — French plenipotentiary
  • Count von Kaunitz (later prince) — Austrian representative, already laying the groundwork for his celebrated diplomatic revolution
  • Count Podewils — Prussian representative
  • The Marquess of Tablada — Spanish representative

While the diplomats deliberated, hostilities continued. Marechal de Saxe, in a final show of strength, invested the fortress of Maastricht in April 1748. The allies, under the Duke of Cumberland and the Prince of Waldeck, were unable to prevent the investment, and the fortress seemed doomed. But the preliminaries of peace were signed in the space of a few weeks, and a suspension of arms was agreed. Maastricht was preserved, largely through the skilful defence of the governor, Baron d’Alva, and the unexpected speed of the diplomatic resolution.

The negotiations proceeded with unusual rapidity by the standards of eighteenth-century diplomacy. The basis had been agreed in principle between Britain and France before the congress formally opened: a mutual restitution of conquests. The detailed terms were hammered out over the spring and summer of 1748, and the definitive treaty was signed on 18 October.

Terms of the Treaty

The treaty was a complex of bilateral instruments rather than a single document. The essential provisions are summarised below.

Summary of Terms

ProvisionDetails
Mutual restitution of conquestsAll conquests made during the war, whether in Europe, the East Indies, or the Americas, were to be mutually restored. This was the central bargain.
Louisbourg returned to FranceThe fortress of Louisbourg on Cape Breton Island, captured by New England forces in 1745 after a celebrated seven-week siege, was restored to France — the most controversial provision from the British perspective.
Madras returned to BritainThe settlement of Madras on the Coromandel Coast of India, captured by the French under La Bourdonnais in September 1746, was restored to Britain in exchange for Louisbourg.
Prussia confirmed in possession of SilesiaFrederick the Great was confirmed in his conquest of Silesia, along with the county of Glatz — the principal gain of the war for Prussia, secured at Maria Theresa’s expense.
Parma, Piacenza, and Guastalla to Don PhilipThe Duchy of Parma (with Piacenza and Guastalla) was ceded to Don Philip, younger son of Philip V of Spain and Elisabeth Farnese, creating a new Bourbon state in northern Italy.
Sardinia gainsThe Kingdom of Sardinia received portions of the Milanese, including Vigevano and other territories.
Asiento renewedThe Asiento de Negros — the exclusive right to supply slaves to the Spanish American colonies — was renewed for four years, continuing the contract originally granted to Britain at the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) and administered by the South Sea Company.
Restoration of the Duke of ModenaThe Duke of Modena, a French ally, was restored to his dominions.
HostagesFrance required British hostages to reside in France pending the full execution of the mutual restitution of conquests. The Earls of Sussex and Cathcart were nominated for this purpose — a point seized upon by the opposition as humiliating.
Boundary commissionsThe disputed boundaries between French and British territory in North America were referred to the decision of commissaries — a provision that effectively deferred the question to future conflict.
Spanish grievances unmentionedThe original cause of the War of Jenkins’ Ear — the Spanish right of search and seizure on the high seas — was passed over in complete silence. Not a syllable was mentioned on the subject that had provoked such clamour in 1738–1739.

What the Treaty Did Not Settle

  • The boundaries of Nova Scotia and the Ohio Valley — a question referred to commissioners who never agreed
  • The right of search — the original casus belli of the Anglo-Spanish war
  • Maria Theresa’s claim to Silesia — she never recognised Frederick’s possession and resolved on revenge
  • The French and British claims in India — left to the rivalry of Dupleix and Robert Clive in the years that followed

The British Reaction: “The Peace Without Honour”

The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle was received in Britain with a mixture of relief and fury — relief from a war-weary nation that had borne crushing taxes, but fury from those who saw the terms as a betrayal of British and colonial sacrifice.

The most explosive grievance was the return of Louisbourg. The fortress on Cape Breton Island had been captured in 1745 by a volunteer force of New England militiamen under William Pepperrell, co-operating with Commodore Peter Warren of the Royal Navy. The siege had been a triumph of colonial enterprise — six thousand raw but determined provincials had taken one of the strongest fortresses in North America in a campaign of only eight weeks. The conquest was celebrated throughout the colonies and in Britain as proof of the rising importance of the American colonies.

The news that Louisbourg was to be handed back to France struck the New England colonies like a thunderbolt. The colonists felt, with bitter justice, that their blood and treasure had been thrown away. Belsham records that “the nation had reason to recollect the coarse but sagacious political adage of Hyde, Earl of Rochester, ‘that to attack France in Flanders was to take a bull by the horns.‘” The sacrifice of Cape Breton was the price of recovering Madras — a calculus that made strategic sense from London’s imperial perspective but carried no weight in the town halls of Boston and New England.

In Parliament, the opposition denounced the peace as “disgraceful to the nation.” The requirement that British hostages reside in France gave the patriots a particular handle for attack. Yet the nation at large, Belsham notes, “who were with reason wearied with the expenses and disasters of the war, were well satisfied with the terms of the peace, and it was celebrated with great and universal rejoicings.” The judgment was divided between popular relief and colonial outrage — a division that would deepen as the century progressed.

The Treaty as a Truce: Significance and Legacy

The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle has been described by almost every historian who has examined it as a truce masquerading as a peace. The status quo ante bellum formula restored the territorial map of 1740 with two decisive exceptions: Silesia remained in Prussian hands, and Parma passed to the Spanish Bourbons. But it settled nothing.

For Britain, the treaty was a disappointment. The colonial question in North America — the boundaries of Nova Scotia, the Ohio Valley, the hinterland of the French and British possessions — was left to commissioners who never resolved it. The war with Spain had produced no mention of the right of search. The capture of Louisbourg had been thrown away. The peace was bought at the price of returning a fortress that would prove enormously costly to retake (it fell again to Edward Boscawen and James Wolfe in 1758). The interval between Aix-la-Chapelle and the outbreak of the Seven Years’ War in 1756 was not a period of genuine peace but of armed truce, during which both Britain and France prepared for a renewal of the struggle they knew was inevitable.

For Austria, the treaty was a humiliation. Maria Theresa had lost Silesia, the richest province of the Habsburg monarchy, and received nothing in exchange. She emerged from the congress with a burning determination to recover her lost territory and a cold recognition that Britain — her traditional ally — was an unreliable partner. Her great minister, Count von Kaunitz (later Prince Kaunitz), who represented Austria at the congress, had already begun the diplomatic work that would produce the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 — the reversal of alliances by which Austria abandoned Britain and allied with her ancient enemy France.

For Prussia, the treaty was a triumph. Frederick the Great was confirmed in the possession of Silesia, the conquest that had begun the war and that would remain the foundation of Prussian power. The treaty gave Frederick a breathing-space to consolidate his new province, drill his army, and prepare for the next round — which he knew would come when Austria had found a new ally.

For France, the treaty was a missed opportunity. Marechal de Saxe had given France military dominance in Flanders, yet the negotiators gave back all the conquered territory. The French court was divided between the war party, which wanted to press the advantage, and the peace party, led by Madame de Pompadour and the finance minister, which recognised that France could not afford to continue. The result satisfied no one and left France with the worst of both worlds: the resentment of maintaining a vast war establishment without territorial gain.

For Spain, the treaty was a modest success: the Asiento was renewed, and Don Philip received the rich duchy of Parma, satisfying Elisabeth Farnese’s long-standing ambition to establish her younger sons in Italian states.

The Diplomatic Revolution

The discontent of the principal powers created the conditions for the most dramatic realignment in eighteenth-century European diplomacy. Maria Theresa, deeply alienated from Britain, which she felt had abandoned her at Aix-la-Chapelle, authorised Kaunitz to pursue a rapprochement with France. Kaunitz’s diplomacy, conducted with extraordinary patience and secrecy, culminated in the Treaty of Westminster (January 1756) between Britain and Prussia — a defensive convention that astonished Europe — and the First Treaty of Versailles (May 1756) between France and Austria, reversing the alliances that had endured for nearly two centuries. The Seven Years’ War (1756–1763) that followed was, in large part, the war that Aix-la-Chapelle had failed to prevent.

Key Figures

George II

King of Great Britain and Ireland, and Elector of Hanover. George II had commanded personally at the Battle of Dettingen (1743), the last British monarch to lead troops in battle. By 1748, he was weary of war and eager for peace, though he shared his subjects’ dismay at the return of Louisbourg. His attachment to his Hanoverian electoral interests had been a persistent source of political controversy throughout the war.

Earl of Sandwich (John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich, 1718–1792)

British plenipotentiary at Aix-la-Chapelle and a rising figure in the Pelham ministry. Sandwich had already distinguished himself as a naval administrator and diplomat. At Aix-la-Chapelle, he steered the British negotiation through the complexities of the mutual restitution clause and the exchange of Louisbourg for Madras. He later served as First Lord of the Admiralty during the Seven Years’ War and again during the American Revolutionary War. His name would become synonymous with the food-item he was said to have invented to sustain himself at the gaming table.

Sir Thomas Robinson (1695–1770)

British co-plenipotentiary and a career diplomat who had served as ambassador to Vienna during the early years of the war. Robinson was a capable but unspectacular negotiator; his greatest fame came later when, as Secretary of State, he proved unequal to the parliamentary rough-and-tumble and was famously destroyed in debate by William Pitt the Elder.

Comte de Saint-Séverin (Alphonse Marie Louis, comte de Saint-Séverin d’Aragon, 1705–1757)

The chief French negotiator. Saint-Séverin was a skilled diplomat who extracted the maximum advantage from France’s strong military position. He secured the return of Louisbourg and preserved the French position in North America, but could not persuade his government to retain the Austrian Netherlands — a territorial prize that would have fundamentally altered the European balance.

Maria Theresa (1717–1780)

Empress-Queen of Hungary and Bohemia. The War of the Austrian Succession had begun with the challenge to her succession; it ended with her confirmed on her thrones but stripped of Silesia. The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle left her with a bitter sense of betrayal by Britain and a cold determination to recover her losses. Her resentment set the stage for the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 and the great reform of the Habsburg monarchy that she and Kaunitz carried out in the following decade.

Frederick the Great (1712–1786)

King of Prussia, who had seized Silesia in December 1740 and defended it through three hard-fought wars. The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle confirmed his conquest — the single most important territorial gain of the war. Frederick spent the peace years consolidating his new province, building the palace of Sanssouci, and preparing the Prussian army for what he correctly anticipated would be a renewed struggle with Austria. He was the only major sovereign to emerge from the treaty entirely satisfied.

Duke of Cumberland (William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland, 1721–1765)

Captain-General of the British army, hero of Culloden, but thoroughly outgeneralled by Marechal de Saxe in Flanders. His inability to prevent Saxe’s conquest of the Austrian Netherlands was one reason the allies could not negotiate from strength at Aix-la-Chapelle.

Count (later Prince) von Kaunitz (1711–1794)

Austrian plenipotentiary at the congress, already the architect of the diplomatic strategy that would produce the Franco-Austrian alliance. Kaunitz’s reading of the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle was coldly realistic: Britain would not fight for Silesia, and only France could help Austria recover it. His embassy to Paris (1750–1753) and his subsequent direction of Austrian policy produced the diplomatic revolution that reshaped Europe.
