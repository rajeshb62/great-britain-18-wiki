War of Jenkins’ Ear & Capture of Porto Bello (1739)

The War of Jenkins’ Ear (1739–1748) was a conflict between Great Britain and Spain that originated in long-standing commercial disputes in the West Indies and was triggered by a notorious incident of Spanish brutality against a British merchant captain. The war is best remembered for Admiral Edward Vernon’s stunning capture of Porto Bello (modern Portobelo, Panama) in November 1739 with only six ships — an achievement that made him the most popular man in Britain and gave London one of its most famous street names. The war would soon merge into the wider War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) and produce, in its turn, the disastrous Cartagena expedition of 1741.

Background: Spanish Depredations in the West Indies

The Treaty of Utrecht (1713), which ended the War of the Spanish Succession, had granted Britain the asiento — the exclusive right to supply slaves to Spanish America — together with permission to send one annual trading ship (navío de permiso) to the Spanish colonies. These concessions were the source of endless friction. British merchants exploited them as a cover for widespread contraband trade, while the Spanish authorities responded with increasingly aggressive enforcement.

The instrument of Spanish enforcement was the Guarda Costas — armed vessels commissioned by the Spanish colonial authorities to patrol the coasts of the West Indies and the Spanish Main, intercepting smugglers and searching British merchant ships for contraband. In practice, the Guarda Costas operated with little restraint. According to John Campbell’s Lives of the British Admirals, they “boarded and plundered every English ship they met, under a pretence of searching for contraband goods” and “seized several English vessels, confiscated their cargoes, and threw the sailors into prison” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 431). The British government received a constant stream of petitions and complaints from merchants, ship-owners, and sailors who had been abused in the Caribbean.

As early as March 1731, “several masters and sailors of merchant-ships, who had been taken by the Spanish Guarda Costas, came to London to give an account to parliament of the cruel treatment they had received from the Spaniards” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 430). The House of Commons addressed the King in 1733 demanding satisfaction, and though some minor reparations were made — one Guarda Costa was sold at Santiago de Cuba and the proceeds paid to the South Sea Company factors — the underlying problem persisted. The Spanish authorities, moreover, began to dispute Britain’s right to cut logwood in the Bay of Campeachy or gather salt on the island of Tortugas, adding territorial grievances to the catalogue of commercial disputes.

The Jenkins’ Ear Incident (1731)

The single incident that inflamed British public opinion above all others occurred in 1731, though it did not become a political cause célèbre until 1738. Captain Robert Jenkins, master of the Scottish merchant vessel Rebecca, was stopped in the West Indies by a Spanish Guarda Costa. The Spanish boarding party, suspecting him of smuggling, subjected him to a brutal search. According to the account later presented to Parliament, the Spaniards tore off one of Jenkins’s ears, declaring they would do the same to his king. They then tortured him with “wanton inhumanity” and threatened him with instant death (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 432).

Jenkins preserved his severed ear and, seven years later, in 1738, appeared before a committee of the House of Commons to give evidence. He stood at the bar of the House holding his ear in his hand. When a member asked him what his thoughts were while in the hands of the Spaniards, he replied: “I recommended my soul to God, and my cause to my country.” This dramatic answer, reported and reprinted across the country, produced a sensation. The incident became a symbol of Spanish insolence and cruelty, and the cry for war became irresistible.

Public Clamour and Walpole’s Reluctance

Throughout the 1730s, the government of Sir Robert Walpole had pursued a consistent policy of peace. Walpole, Britain’s first and longest-serving Prime Minister (1721–1742), believed that war was ruinous to trade and to the public finances that his entire system of government depended upon. He had maintained peace with Spain through a series of diplomatic expedients — the Treaty of Seville (1729) and the Convention of El Pardo (1739) — the latter of which provided for Spanish payment of £95,000 in compensation for captured British ships.

But by 1738, the public mood had turned decisively against Walpole. The opposition, led by William Pitt the Elder, Lord Carteret, and the “Patriot” faction, used the Jenkins’ Ear incident to rally the country. Petitions poured into Parliament from merchants and maritime communities. The press, including The Craftsman and other opposition journals, fanned the flames. “The nation in general was too much exasperated to afford any hopes of preventing a war with Spain” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 432). Even as Walpole secured a majority in Parliament against the most bellicose motions, he could not contain public fury.

The turning point came when the Spanish government failed to make the payments stipulated by the Convention of El Pardo. Lord Carteret moved in the Lords that this failure was “a high indignity to the king, and an injustice to the nation.” Though the motion was lost, the damage was done. Walpole, against his better judgment, was forced to yield. Letters of marque and reprisal were issued against Spanish shipping; the army was augmented; an embargo was laid on outward-bound vessels; and a fleet was assembled at Spithead. By the summer of 1739, Britain was effectively at war, even before the formal declaration.

The Declaration of War (October 1739)

On 23 October 1739, Great Britain formally declared war against Spain. The British navy at that time had eighty-four men-of-war in commission, with another thirty-two ready to be fitted out. The entire Spanish navy numbered only thirty-three ships of war, including the flota merchant vessels (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 432). The disparity in naval power was enormous, and the British public expected swift and decisive results.

Vernon’s Expedition

Among the most vocal proponents of war in the House of Commons was Edward Vernon, a blunt-speaking naval officer and MP for Ipswich. Vernon had served in the West Indies and knew the Spanish Caribbean intimately. In a parliamentary debate on Spanish depredations, he declared — with characteristic bravado — that he could take the fortified Spanish port of Porto Bello on the Isthmus of Darien with six ships only.

The boast was seized upon by the opposition, who echoed it through the country. Walpole, perhaps hoping to remove a troublesome critic from the Commons, appointed Vernon to command the West Indies squadron and gave him the opportunity to make good his word. There was a suspicion, recorded even by contemporary historians, that the ministry was “not without hopes that the admiral might disgrace himself and his party by not succeeding in the adventure” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 433).

Vernon sailed from England on 20 July 1739 with nine men-of-war. He arrived at Port Royal, Jamaica, on 23 October — the very day war was proclaimed in England. Upon arrival he had the satisfaction of seeing the Diamond man-of-war standing into harbour with two Spanish prizes, one a register ship carrying 120,000 pieces of eight and clothing for 6,000 men.

After encountering contrary winds, the squadron came in sight of Porto Bello on the evening of 20 November. Porto Bello — so called from the beauty of its harbour — was situated on the north coast of the Isthmus of Darien, in what is now Panama. The town stood at the bottom of a small bay, defended by three fortifications: the Iron Fort (Fuerte del Hierro) at the mouth of the harbour on the north side; Castle St. Jeronimo near the town, with a battery facing the bay entrance; and the Castle Gloria on the west side of the town, a massive structure garrisoned by 400 men.

On the morning of 21 November, Vernon weighed anchor and formed line of battle. The ships entered the bay in order, with the Hampton Court leading. As she came within a cable’s length of the Iron Fort, she was suddenly becalmed by the high land to windward and exposed to a smart fire before she could bring her guns to bear. Once she anchored and returned fire, she “seemed, in a moment, a cloud of perpetual thunder” — firing 400 balls in the space of twenty-five minutes (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 435).

The Norwich and Worcester came up next and engaged with “vast alacrity.” Vernon himself, aboard the Burford, perceived the Spaniards beginning to flee from parts of the fort. He ordered a landing. The boats full of sailors and marines passed the admiral, who called out to them to land immediately under the walls of the fort, though no breach had been made. The sailors scaled the wall, pulled the soldiers up after them, struck the Spanish colours, and hoisted the British ensign on the lower battery.

The garrison of the Iron Fort, originally 300 men, had been reduced to only 35 privates and 5 officers by the time they surrendered. Observing the English flag on the lower battery, the upper garrison hoisted a white flag and surrendered at discretion.

On the morning of 22 November, Vernon called a council of war to plan an attack on Gloria Castle, but the Spaniards anticipated him by sending a boat with a flag of truce. Articles of capitulation were agreed and signed on board the Burford that day between Vernon and Commodore Brown on one side, and Governor Don Francisco Martinez de Retez and Commandant Don Francisco de Abarea on the other. The British troops took immediate possession of Gloria Castle and St. Jeronimo.

Two Spanish guarda-costas of twenty guns each and an armed snow were found in the harbour. The Spanish crews, having first plundered the town and committed outrages on the inhabitants, were relieved by the disciplined behaviour of the British sailors, who received 10,000 dollars (the Spanish troop payroll, captured intact) as a reward.

Vernon spent three weeks demolishing the fortifications, using 122 barrels of Spanish gunpowder, and took on board 40 brass cannon, 10 brass field-pieces, 4 brass mortars, 18 brass pattereroes, and spiked 80 pieces of iron ordnance. He sailed for Jamaica on 13 December.

The Popular Celebration

The news of Porto Bello’s capture reached England in March 1740. Captain Rentone, dispatched in the Triumph sloop, arrived on 12 March — though news had preceded him by a day via a merchant ship from Jamaica. The nation “became frantic with joy” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 438).

Parliament voted congratulatory addresses. The cities of London, Bristol, and others followed suit. The Commons granted every demand of the crown: 28,000 land forces, 6,000 marines, and a powerful naval expansion. Vernon became the most celebrated man in Britain. Medals were struck in his honour. Inn- and tavern-keepers renamed their establishments “The Admiral Vernon” or “Porto Bello.” The most lasting tribute came in London, where the street Portobello Road (originally Porto Bello Road, later corrupted to its modern spelling) was laid out on the Portobello estate in Paddington, named to commemorate the victory. The name survives to this day as one of London’s most famous markets.

More significantly, the victory inspired the composition of “Rule, Britannia!” — the patriotic song by James Thomson with music by Thomas Arne, first performed at Cliveden in 1740 for the Prince of Wales. Though the song celebrates British naval supremacy in general, its composition in the year of Porto Bello — with the line “Britannia, rule the waves” — was unmistakably informed by Vernon’s astonishing triumph. The contemporary ballad “Hosier’s Ghost” by Richard Glover, recounting the death of Admiral Francis Hosier off Porto Bello in 1726 and contrasting it with Vernon’s success, also became immensely popular.

The praise was not unqualified. Campbell’s history notes that the victory, while honourable to the British navy, was “in part attributed to the cowardice of the Spaniards in surrendering the first fort before a breach was made, and the other two before they were attacked” — adding that Gloria Castle was so regularly fortified that it “might have sustained a long siege” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 437–438).

Aftermath: Cartagena and the Widening War

The capture of Porto Bello proved to be the high-water mark of British success in the war. Emboldened by Vernon’s victory, the government planned a far more ambitious operation: the capture of Cartagena de Indias (in modern Colombia), the most formidable Spanish fortress in the Americas.

In 1741, a massive expedition assembled at Jamaica under Vernon’s command. Sir Chaloner Ogle arrived in January with reinforcements, giving Vernon a fleet of thirty ships of the line, numerous frigates, bomb-ketches, and fire-ships, with 15,000 seamen and 12,000 soldiers (including four battalions raised in America and 500 Jamaican negroes). “This very formidable armament,” Campbell writes, “doubtless the most tremendous that ever appeared in those seas, was certainly equal to any attempt against the Spanish settlements” (Campbell, Vol. IV, p. 444).

Yet the Cartagena expedition was a disaster. The fleet had been delayed at Spithead for months by the ministry; Vernon himself delayed at Jamaica until the season was dangerously advanced; the Spanish, forewarned, reinforced the garrison under the great military engineer Blas de Lezo; disease ravaged the British troops; and coordination between Vernon and the army commander, Brigadier-General Thomas Wentworth, was catastrophic. After two months of siege and desperate fighting, the British withdrew with staggering losses — over 9,000 men dead, mostly from yellow fever and other diseases. Vernon’s reputation never fully recovered.

The war with Spain merged into the general European conflict of the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748), triggered by the death of Emperor Charles VI and the disputed succession of Maria Theresa of Austria. Britain found herself fighting France and Spain simultaneously across multiple theatres. The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) ended the war on broadly status quo terms — Porto Bello, which had been evacuated immediately after its capture, remained in Spanish hands. As one historian observed, “In the whole course of a war which had continued nine years, nothing had been lost, and nothing gained, Porto Bello excepted, which had been immediately evacuated.”

Key Figures

  • Robert Walpole (1676–1745) — Prime Minister, reluctantly pushed into war by public clamour against his pacific policy. His ministry fell in 1742, partly as a consequence of the war’s mismanagement.
  • Edward Vernon (1684–1757) — Admiral, hero of Porto Bello, inventor of “grog” (diluted rum issued to sailors), and later disgraced by the Cartagena disaster. He served as MP for Ipswich and was a fierce critic of the Admiralty.
  • Captain Robert Jenkins — Scottish merchant captain whose severed ear became the rallying cry for war. His subsequent career is obscure; he is remembered only for this single, dramatic appearance in history.
  • King George II (1683–1760) — The reigning monarch. The last British king to lead troops in person (at Dettingen, 1743), he supported the war against Spain despite Walpole’s reservations.
