Treaty of Utrecht (1713)

The Treaty of Utrecht, signed on 11 April 1713 (1 April Old Style), was the series of peace agreements that ended the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714). It was the crowning diplomatic achievement of the Harley–St John ministry and the most controversial settlement in British eighteenth-century history — denounced by the Whigs as a betrayal of the allies, defended by the Tories as a necessary release from a war that had exhausted the nation.

The treaty was not a single document but a complex of bilateral instruments between Great Britain, France, Spain, Portugal, Savoy, Prussia, and the Dutch Republic. The emperor continued the war alone until the Treaty of Rastatt (1714). The central bargain was stark: Philip V of the House of Bourbon was confirmed as King of Spain and the Indies, while Britain received Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, Acadia (Nova Scotia), Hudson’s Bay, the Asiento de Negros, and the demolition of Dunkirk — a settlement that laid the foundations of the British Empire.

Secret Negotiations: The Anglo-French Backchannel (1711–1712)

The road to Utrecht began in secrecy. By 1710, the war had become a drain on both powers. The Tory ministry that came to power in the general election of that year, under Robert Harley (created Earl of Oxford in 1711) and Henry St John (later Viscount Bolingbroke), was committed to peace from the outset. Harley, a master of clandestine diplomacy, established a secret channel to the French court through the Abbé François Gaultier, a French priest resident in London, and the poet-diplomat Matthew Prior.

The negotiations were conducted without the knowledge of Britain’s principal allies — the Dutch Republic and the Emperor. Smollett records that the articles “being privately regulated between the two courts of London and Versailles, the English plenipotentiaries at Utrecht were furnished with general powers and instructions, being ignorant of the agreement which the queen had made with the French monarch touching the kingdom of Spain, which was indeed the basis of the treaty.”

The preliminaries of peace were signed in London on 27 September 1711, but the secret was difficult to keep. In October 1711, news of the Anglo-French talks leaked, producing a storm of protest from the allies and from the Whig opposition. The crisis deepened when the Emperor Joseph I died in April 1711, leaving the Austrian claim to the Spanish inheritance in the hands of his brother Charles — the very union of the Spanish and Imperial crowns the war had been fought to prevent. Harley’s argument that a Bourbon Spain was no more dangerous than a Habsburg universal monarchy became, overnight, a plausible diplomatic position.

Opening of the Congress and the Barrier Treaty Controversy

The Congress of Utrecht opened on 29 January 1712 (Old Style). The British plenipotentiaries were Dr. John Robinson, Bishop of Bristol, and Thomas Wentworth, Earl of Strafford. The French sent the Marshal d’Uxelles, the Abbé de Polignac, and Nicolas Mesnager; the Imperialists, the Dutch, Savoyards, Portuguese, and Prussians all sent delegations. The ceremonial was deliberately simplified — the plenipotentiaries agreed to assume no character of dignity until all matters were adjusted, saving the weeks of wrangling over precedence that had plagued earlier congresses.

The French proposals, delivered in early February, were extravagantly high. Burnet describes them as “as extravagant as any that France could have made, in the most prosperous state of their affairs.” They offered Spain and the Indies to Philip, the Spanish Netherlands to the Elector of Bavaria, and demanded the restoration of Landau and other conquests. The allies responded with demands equally inflated. The congress soon degenerated into fruitless conference.

The real obstacle was the Dutch Barrier Treaty. The States-General had secured a Barrier Treaty with Britain in 1709 that gave them the right to garrison a chain of fortresses in the Spanish Netherlands against French aggression. The Harley ministry repudiated this treaty and negotiated a new one that sharply reduced the Dutch barrier and made it conditional on the States supporting the Protestant succession only when called upon by the queen, rather than automatically. Burnet notes that “the States were forced to yield in every particular” — a humiliation that poisoned Anglo-Dutch relations for years. The new Barrier Treaty was signed only after the Dutch had conceded all essential points to France.

The Crisis of the French Succession (1712)

The negotiations at Utrecht were thrown into chaos by a succession of deaths at the French court that brought the prospect of a Franco-Spanish union terrifyingly close. In February 1712, the Dauphiness, Marie-Adélaïde of Savoy, died of measles; within a week, the Dauphin (Louis, son of Louis XIV) died of the same disease. Six days later, their eldest son, the Duke of Brittany, died. Of the Dauphin’s children, only the infant Duke of Anjou — the future Louis XV — survived, and he was a sickly child.

As Smollett records, “Such a series of calamities could not fail of being extremely shocking to Lewis in his old age; but they were still more alarming to the queen of England, who saw that nothing but the precarious life of an unhealthy child divided the two monarchies of France and Spain.” Queen Anne sent the Abbé Gaultier back to Paris with a memorial demanding that Philip V renounce his rights to the French throne absolutely and irrevocably.

Philip V’s Renunciation

Philip V was initially reluctant. Harley’s ministry proposed an alternative — that Philip should surrender Spain to the Duke of Savoy in exchange for Sicily and the Savoyard hereditary dominions, which would revert to France on his accession. Louis XIV seemed inclined to accept this expedient, but Philip rejected it. He chose, as Smollett puts it, “to make the renunciation, rather than quit the throne upon which he was established.”

In November 1712, Philip summoned the Cortes of Castile and, before that assembly, made a solemn renunciation for himself and his heirs of all right of succession to the crown of France. The renunciation was registered in the parliaments of France — a form that the Marquis de Torcy frankly admitted was “in itself void, as being contrary to the fundamental laws and constitution of the French monarchy, but it was found necessary for the satisfaction of the English people.” The reciprocal renunciation, by which the Princes of France renounced their claims to Spain, followed some months later.

The Separate Armistice and Ormond’s Defection

While the diplomats manoeuvred at Utrecht, the military situation took a decisive turn. In May 1712, the Duke of Ormond, who had succeeded Marlborough as Captain-General of the allied forces in Flanders, received secret orders from London not to risk an engagement with the French. This instruction was kept from the allies for as long as possible.

Prince Eugene of Savoy, the Imperial commander, pressed for an attack on the French army under Marshal Villars. Ormond prevaricated. When the restriction became known, the Dutch and Imperial deputies protested vehemently. On 16 July 1712, Ormond formally notified the allies that he could no longer co-operate; he withdrew the British contingent and signed a separate armistice with Villars. The British troops marched to Dunkirk, which was handed over as a pledge of French good faith.

Prince Eugene, abandoned by his British allies, pressed on alone. On 24 July 1712, Villars struck at the isolated Dutch corps at Denain, commanded by the Earl of Albemarle, and inflicted a devastating defeat. The siege of Landrecies was raised; one by one, the allied conquests of the campaign — Douai, Quesnoy, Bouchain — fell to the French. Burnet observes that “these losses created a great distraction in the councils at the Hague” and forced the Dutch to accept terms they would never have countenanced before.

In Britain, the news of Ormond’s withdrawal and the disaster at Denain provoked a political firestorm. The Whigs in the House of Lords moved addresses demanding that Ormond be ordered to act offensively. In the Commons, a motion to the same effect was defeated by a large majority. Harley defended the policy as prudent — “to avoid a battle on the eve of a peace.” Marlborough, now in opposition, declared that the measures pursued were “directly contrary to her majesty’s engagements with the allies” and would “render the English name odious to all nations.”

The Treaty: Terms and Provisions

The Treaty of Utrecht was signed in the early hours of 1 April 1713 (Old Style) / 11 April 1713 (New Style), at the private house of the Bishop of Bristol, under pretence of a conference. Campbell, who gives the most detailed account of the signing, records that “the Earl of Strafford, and the Bishop of Bristol, signed first; then the ministers of the Duke of Savoy, declared King of Sicily by that treaty; those of the King of Portugal after them; then the plenipotentiaries of the King of Prussia, and those of the States-General last of all. The whole was over about two in the morning.”

The separate peace between Britain and Spain was not concluded until 13 July 1713, but the essential terms were embodied in the Anglo-French treaty. The principal provisions affecting Great Britain are summarised in the table below.

Provisions for the Allies

The treaty also provided for the other members of the Grand Alliance. The Duke of Savoy received Sicily with the title of King, together with the Alpine frontier fortresses he had long demanded. Portugal had its boundaries with Spanish America settled and received territorial compensation in South America. Prussia had its royal title recognised and received Upper Guelderland in compensation for the loss of Orange to France. The Emperor was offered the Spanish Netherlands, Milan, Naples, and Sardinia — terms he rejected, continuing the war until the Treaty of Rastatt (March 1714) gave him substantially the same settlement.

British Domestic Politics: The Tory Peace

The Treaty of Utrecht was the central political fact of the Harley-St. John ministry and the defining issue of British politics between 1711 and 1715. The peace was a party peace, made by a Tory ministry against the wishes of a Whig minority in the Lords and a substantial body of Whig opinion in the country.

Queen Anne announced the terms to Parliament on 6 June 1712, in a speech that stressed the security of the Protestant succession, the acceptance of Hanover, and the exclusion of the Pretender. She enumerated the territorial gains for Britain — Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, Hudson’s Bay, St. Christopher’s, the demolition of Dunkirk, the Asiento — and argued that France had “acknowledged the queen’s title and the Protestant succession in the strongest terms.”

The Commons voted an immediate address of thanks and approbation. The Lords were more difficult. Marlborough declared that the measures pursued “sullied the triumphs and glories of her reign.” Lord Cowper defended the allies and condemned the clandestine diplomacy. The debate was fierce, but the ministry’s majority held. Twenty peers entered a protest.

The Whig charge was twofold: first, that Britain had betrayed her allies by making a separate peace; second, that the terms themselves were inadequate — that France had been let off far too lightly after a decade of defeat. The charge of betrayal — “perfidious Albion” — would echo in Dutch and Austrian historiography for generations. The charge of inadequate terms was pressed with particular force regarding the commercial treaty with France, which Whig merchants argued would destroy English silk, linen, and paper manufactures by admitting cheaper French goods under the tariff of 1664.

The commercial treaty was defeated in the House of Commons in June 1713, a spectacular reverse for the ministry. Petitions poured in from the manufacturing towns and trading corporations. The ministerial majority — normally reliable on foreign policy — crumbled under the pressure of mercantile interest. The commercial articles were dropped; trade with France reverted to the prohibitory duties of William III’s reign.

Key Figures

Robert Harley, Earl of Oxford

The architect of the peace. Harley, as Lord Treasurer and effective head of the ministry, managed the secret diplomacy through Gaultier and Prior, navigated the parliamentary crisis of the twelve peers (December 1711), and defended the settlement against Whig attack. His style was cautious, secretive, and managerial. He was, Smollett wrote, “a man of moderate abilities, but of great industry, penetration, and address.” His impeachment after the Hanoverian accession would centre on the accusation that he had exceeded his instructions and betrayed the allies.

Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke

The fiery Secretary of State who conducted the detailed negotiations with the French. Bolingbroke was sent to Paris in secret in 1712 to finalise the terms. He was the driving force behind the rapid conclusion of the treaty, the architect of the commercial clauses, and the man who persuaded the Queen to abandon the Dutch. His relationship with Harley deteriorated into open war, splitting the ministry at the moment of its greatest triumph. He was impeached in 1715 and fled to France, where he became Secretary of State to the Pretender.

Queen Anne

The Queen was the ultimate author of the peace. She had grown weary of war, distrustful of the Whig generals and financiers, and personally devoted to Harley. Her speech from the throne on 6 June 1712 laid out the terms. Her intervention prevented Marlborough’s duel with Earl Pawlet. Her declining health — she died fifteen months after the treaty — gave the peace settlement a desperate urgency: the ministry needed peace before the Queen died, lest a Hanoverian king overturn their work.

Marlborough

The great general, dismissed from all his commands in December 1711, was the most prominent critic of the peace. From retirement and then from self-imposed exile, he denounced the terms as inadequate and the manner of their making as dishonourable. His exile in 1712 removed the one man who might have rallied opposition to the treaty. Campbell records that his departure was “variously censured” — some called it fear, others wise precaution.

Duke of Ormond

Succeeded Marlborough as Captain-General in 1712 and executed the ministry’s policy of refusing battle, leading to the separate armistice and the disaster at Denain. He was the instrument of the Queen’s most controversial command; his reputation never recovered. He was impeached in 1715 and fled to France, where he played a leading role in the Jacobite rising of 1715.

Marshal Villars

The French commander whose victory at Denain (July 1712) restored French bargaining power after a decade of defeat. Smollett notes that Villars knew of the British restraint order and “abated of his usual vigilance.” His victory broke the allied will to continue and enabled the French to extract significantly better terms than they could have hoped for in the spring of 1712.

Assessment and Legacy

The Treaty of Utrecht was, for Britain, an astonishingly favourable settlement — “larger advantages,” as Harley claimed in his defence, “than ever had been demanded before, in any treaty or negotiation between this and any other foreign state.” Britain gained a Mediterranean naval base at Gibraltar, a winter anchorage at Port Mahon in Minorca, a North American empire in Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, and Hudson’s Bay, the demolition of the great privateering base at Dunkirk, and the exclusive right to supply slaves to the Spanish Empire. The Protestant succession was guaranteed by France. The Hanoverian dynasty was safe.

Yet the manner of the peace left a stain. The secret diplomacy, the abandonment of the allies, the separate armistice, and the disaster at Denain made the treaty a byword for diplomatic duplicity in allied capitals. At home, the Whigs never forgot or forgave. The impeachment of Harley and Bolingbroke in 1715 was, in large part, a prosecution of the peace. Even the Tory historian Smollett, writing two generations later, conceded that “better might have been obtained, had not the obstinacy of the allies abroad, and the violent conduct of the Whig faction at home, obliged the ministers to relax in some material points, and hasten the conclusion of the treaty.”

The treaty defined the European state system for a generation. The separation of the French and Spanish crowns, guaranteed by renunciation, held until the French Revolution. The British Mediterranean empire endured until the twentieth century. The Asiento, channelled through the South Sea Company, produced the South Sea Bubble of 1720 — the greatest financial catastrophe of the eighteenth century and the ruin of Harley himself.

“After all the steps our court had made, to get them a treaty of their own projecting, we were not at last able to gain any one point upon them: They seemed to reckon, that now we had put ourselves in their hands, and that they might use us as they pleased.” — Gilbert Burnet, History of His Own Time

“The said earl presumes, on this occasion, to mention to your lordships, the saying of as wise a man, and as great a general as the last age produced, the duke of Parma… ‘That if France were to be got, only by reducing its towns, the world would sooner be at an end than such a war.‘” — Robert Harley, Earl of Oxford, in his answer to the articles of impeachment
