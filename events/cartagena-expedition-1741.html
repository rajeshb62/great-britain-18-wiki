Cartagena Expedition (1741)

The Cartagena Expedition (March–April 1741) was the largest amphibious operation undertaken by Great Britain in the eighteenth century and one of the most disastrous. A fleet of over 120 ships carrying some 27,000 men — including British regulars, marines, and American colonial regiments — was dispatched to capture Cartagena de Indias (modern Colombia), the richest Spanish port in the New World. After a promising opening in which the outer defences of the harbour were carried with relative ease, the expedition collapsed amid rancorous disputes between the naval and army commanders, a failed assault on Fort St. Lazar, and devastating epidemics of tropical disease. The Spanish defenders, led by the one-eyed, one-legged, one-armed Admiral Blas de Lezo, mounted one of the most brilliant defensive campaigns in colonial history, inflicting a humiliating defeat that ended British offensive operations in the Caribbean for years.

Background

The War of Jenkins’ Ear

The expedition must be understood in the context of the War of Jenkins’ Ear (1739–1748), a conflict between Britain and Spain that arose from long-simmering disputes over trade, colonial boundaries, and Spanish depredations on British shipping in the Caribbean. The immediate casus belli was the story of Captain Robert Jenkins, a British merchant captain who claimed before the House of Commons that Spanish coast guards had boarded his vessel, cut off his ear, and bade him carry it to his king. The incident became a rallying cry for the anti-Walpole opposition, which demanded war against Spain (Belsham, vol. IV, lines 1480–1485).

The administration of Sir Robert Walpole had long resisted war, preferring a policy of peace and financial retrenchment; but by 1739 the popular clamour had become irresistible. Walpole was famously heard to mutter as war was declared: “They are ringing the bells now; they will soon be wringing their hands.” The prophecy would prove uncannily accurate.

Vernon’s Triumph at Porto Bello

In the opening stroke of the war, Vice-Admiral Edward Vernon — a combative and outspoken Member of Parliament who had taunted the government in the Commons — was given command of a squadron of just six ships with which he claimed he could take the Spanish fortified town of Porto Bello on the Isthmus of Darien. On 20 November 1739, Vernon unleashed a furious attack on the town’s forts, and the Spanish garrison surrendered within twenty-four hours. The news was received in Britain with unprecedented jubilation. Vernon became the most popular man in the kingdom: medals were struck, streets and taverns were named after him (the original of “Portobello Road” in London and the nursery rhyme “Portobello”), and Parliament voted him its thanks (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 18800–18860).

The Porto Bello triumph raised expectations to a dangerous height. The ministry — still dominated by Walpole, now fighting a war he had never wanted — resolved upon a far more ambitious stroke: the capture of Cartagena de Indias, the principal Spanish treasure port on the Spanish Main and the linchpin of Spain’s American empire.

The Target: Cartagena de Indias

Cartagena, founded in 1533, was the wealthiest city in Spanish America. It served as the primary collecting point for Peruvian silver, the terminus of the annual treasure fleets, and the administrative capital of the Spanish Main. Its harbour — a large, well-protected lagoon connected to the sea by a narrow, heavily fortified channel called Boca Chica — was considered one of the strongest natural positions on the continent. The defences had been continuously improved through the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries: massive stone fortresses, batteries of heavy cannon, and the formidable fort of San Felipe de Barajas (called St. Lazar by the English) on a hill overlooking the city. The French had taken Cartagena in 1697 under Pointis only through a combined naval and land operation of great sophistication, and the Spanish had learned the lesson, strengthening the defences further.

The Expedition

Assembly and Command

The expedition assembled at Spithead under the overall command of Vice-Admiral Edward Vernon and Lieutenant-General Lord Cathcart, an experienced and respected officer. The fleet numbered some 124 vessels of all sizes — ships of the line, frigates, bomb-ketches, fireships, and transports — the largest naval force Britain had yet sent across the Atlantic. The land forces, including six regiments of marines, numbered approximately 10,000 regular troops, with a further 3,600 raised from the American colonies (notably Virginia, North Carolina, and New York), plus a large body of sailors who could be deployed for shore operations. The American regiments included the “Virginia Blues” and companies raised by Alexander Spotswood, the former governor of Virginia (Campbell, vol. VI, lines 10599–10608).

The expedition was placed under the naval command of Sir Chaloner Ogle, who sailed with the main reinforcement from England in the autumn of 1740 and joined Vernon at Jamaica on 9 January 1741. Vernon’s combined fleet now consisted of thirty-one sail of the line.

Tragedy struck before operations could begin: Lord Cathcart, the army commander, died soon after arrival in the West Indies. Command of the land forces devolved upon General Thomas Wentworth, an officer whose reputation has been the subject of fierce historical debate. According to Campbell (vol. VI, lines 10608–10617), Wentworth “was undoubtedly inferior to his predecessor in every essential requisite of a general,” an officer “without experience, resolution, or authority, utterly unqualified for the important station of commander-in-chief,” who owed his appointment entirely to his parliamentary connections. The memoir concedes, however, that the verdict may be too harsh: the two commanders were “at utter and open variance during the whole of the expedition,” and Vernon’s own fierce temper and overbearing manner may have contributed to the breakdown (Campbell, vol. VI, lines 10618–10647).

Delay at Jamaica

The fleet did not sail from Jamaica until late January 1741 — dangerously late in the season. The rainy season on the Spanish Main begins about the end of April, after which campaigning becomes all but impossible. Campbell (vol. IV, lines 19040–19046) notes Vernon’s puzzling delay: “it was near the end of January before he sailed from Jamaica, though he certainly was not ignorant that the season was already too far advanced.” The admiral’s discretionary orders allowed him to attack any Spanish settlement; some contemporary critics argued he ought to have struck at Havana, which lay to leeward and could have been reached in three days, rather than beating against the wind towards Cartagena. Vernon, however, had set his mind on Cartagena, and no argument would move him (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19051–19061).

The French governor of Hispaniola, observing the armada with alarm, sent an advice-boat to Cartagena to warn the Spanish of Vernon’s approach. The element of surprise was lost before a single shot was fired.

The Siege

Arrival and Initial Success

On 4 March 1741, the British fleet anchored in Playa Grande, a bay to windward of Cartagena. The sight of 124 sail must have been terrifying to the Spanish defenders, but the commanders, “as if determined to give the enemy time to recover from their surprise,” remained inactive in the bay until 9 March (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19066–19077).

On 9 March, the first division of the fleet, commanded by Sir Chaloner Ogle, followed by Vernon with the transports, moved forward to the entrance of the harbour at Boca Chica — a narrow, twisting channel protected by fortresses on either side. The second division, under Commodore Richard Lestock, remained at anchor as a reserve. The Norfolk, Russel, and Shrewsbury anchored close to two forts called St. Jago and St. Philip, silenced them in under an hour, and a detachment of British grenadiers took possession (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19078–19087).

On 10 March, the regiments of Harrison and Wentworth, with six regiments of marines, landed on the island of Tierra Bomba and began erecting a battery against the castle of Boca Chica. Five days were consumed in landing artillery. Wentworth’s want of military knowledge soon showed itself: the tents were pitched within range of a Spanish fascine battery on the opposite side of the harbour. Vernon detached sailors under Captain Edward Boscawen, who landed, captured the battery of fifteen 24-pounders (plus a raised battery of five guns), spiked the cannon, and returned to their ships — a feat described in Campbell’s admiring phrase as the work of “intrepid sons of Neptune” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19096–19108).

Reduction of the Outer Defences

On 22 March, Wentworth opened a battery of twenty 24-pounders against Boca Chica castle. The next day Commodore Lestock, with five ships, attacked from the sea. The bombardment continued through the 24th, costing the British the life of Lord Aubrey Beauclerk, captain of the Prince Frederic, “a very brave and experienced officer” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19110–19116). Meanwhile the Spanish remounted their fascine battery, and a second time it was destroyed by British sailors.

A small breach having been made on the landward side of Boca Chica, Wentworth resolved to storm it. Vernon, to divert the enemy’s attention, manned his boats under Captain Charles Knowles. As the grenadiers approached the breach, the garrison fled without firing a musket. The neighbouring fort of St. Joseph followed suit. The sailors boarded the Spanish admiral’s ship, the Galicia, capturing her captain and sixty men. The Spaniards themselves sank two of their six men-of-war and burnt a third. The sailors cut the boom that barred the channel, and on the morning after the fall of Boca Chica, the British fleet entered the inner harbour without molestation (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19119–19147).

Thus far, success had been complete. The castles, forts, and batteries commanding the lake were in British hands. The entrance into the lake — a channel commanded by some two hundred cannon — had been forced. Vernon was so confident that on 1 April he dispatched an express to the Duke of Newcastle announcing his progress. The news threw the British nation “frantic with joy and exultation” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19154–19160).

The Quarrel

What followed, in Campbell’s rueful phrase, was that “the demon of discord presided in their councils” (Campbell, vol. IV, line 19191). The army had secured the harbour entrance, but the town of Cartagena itself remained to be taken. Between the inner harbour and the city rose the fortress of San Felipe de Barajas — Fort St. Lazar — perched on a steep hill and commanding the only land approach. The fort was well fortified and defended by a numerous garrison, which had been reinforced by the troops driven from the outer defences.

Wentworth believed that St. Lazar could only be reduced by formal siege — regular approaches, trenches, and artillery bombardment — which would take weeks. Vernon, with the rains approaching and disease already beginning to appear, insisted that the fort could be taken by escalade — a direct assault with scaling ladders. The admiral upbraided the general for not storming the fort; the general reproached the admiral for not bombarding the town with his ships. Each man’s contempt for the other grew into open hostility, and the spirit of discord spread through the fleet and army, with officers and men taking sides (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19185–19196; vol. VI, lines 10679–10701).

Belsham (vol. IV, lines 1501–1506) summarised the situation with devastating brevity: “Ignorance, rashness, and dissension, characterised all their operations.”

The Assault on Fort St. Lazar

A council of war reluctantly resolved to attempt the storm — the season offered no time for a siege. On the night of 9 April, before dawn, Brigadier-General Guise marched with 1,200 picked men against Fort St. Lazar. The plan was fatally flawed from the start:

  • The guides were killed before reaching the walls, leaving the attackers without direction.
  • The scaling ladders, brought up in darkness and placed at random, proved too short for the walls.
  • The officers, receiving no clear orders in the chaos, could not coordinate the assault.
  • The Spanish garrison, fully prepared and sheltered behind strong fortifications, poured musket and cannon fire into the packed ranks below.

The attack failed with terrible losses. Six hundred men were killed or wounded — half the assault force — in a few hours of bloody confusion. The survivors staggered back to the British camp (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19199–19206).

Vernon would later argue that the plan was sound and its failure was due to the general’s poor execution; Wentworth pointed to the recklessness of the admiral’s insistence. The recriminations continued for the rest of their lives.

Disease and Retreat

The rains now began in earnest. They fell “very heavily,” and with them came the true enemy: yellow fever, malaria, and dysentery. Disease spread through the British camp with terrifying speed. Men who had survived the storm of St. Lazar fell sick and died in hours. The dead were buried in mass graves, and soon there were not enough healthy men to bury them. The camp became a charnel house. “Diseases, peculiar to the climate, raged with inconceivable malignity,” Campbell records, “and many brave men who had escaped the enemy died in their hammocks” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19234–19236).

A council of war on 14 April determined to abandon the enterprise. The remnant of the army was re-embarked on 16 April. Vernon, in a final gesture meant to demonstrate the impossibility of taking the place by naval bombardment alone, converted the captured Spanish flagship Galicia into a floating battery and warped her into the harbour as near to the town as possible. She fired for some hours, but at too great a distance to damage the walls; she soon drifted and struck on a sandbank. Smollett, who was present as a surgeon’s mate, later asserted that there was room for four or five men-of-war to lie within pistol-shot of the walls — suggesting that the admiral bore some responsibility for failing to use his fleet’s full power during the land assault (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19216–19232).

A few days were spent dismantling the fortifications that had been so hard won, and then the shattered fleet sailed for Jamaica.

The Spanish Defence: Blas de Lezo

The Spanish defence was orchestrated by one of the most remarkable officers in the history of Spanish America: Blas de Lezo y Olavarrieta, a veteran admiral who had lost his left eye, his left arm, and his right leg in various battles over a thirty-year career. He had fought at Vélez-Málaga (1704), Toulon (1704), and in the defence of Barcelona, and had served as commander of the Spanish South Sea fleet. Deformed and scarred, he was known to his men as Mediohombre — “Half-Man” — but his spirit was undiminished.

De Lezo’s strategy was masterful. He correctly judged that the outer fortifications at Boca Chica could not indefinitely hold against the overwhelming British force, and he husbanded his best troops — some 3,000 regulars and militia — for the defence of the inner city and Fort St. Lazar. He ordered the sinking of some of his own ships to block the channel, not so much to prevent the British from entering the harbour as to ensure that once inside, they could not bring their heavy ships close enough to bombard the city walls effectively. Every day that the British spent in the swamps and lowlands of Tierra Bomba and the inner harbour was a day that the tropical climate worked in his favour.

De Lezo’s riposte to Vernon’s vaunting was characteristically sardonic. Before the expedition, when Vernon had bombarded Cartagena in a preliminary reconnaissance (March 1740), de Lezo had sent him a message charging him with cowardice for dismantling Porto Bello and threatening retaliation. Vernon replied that the charges would “best be answered from the mouths of his mortars.” After the British withdrawal, de Lezo’s reply was simple: the guns of St. Lazar had been answer enough (Campbell, vol. VI, lines 10560–10575).

The Aftermath

The Cost

The human cost of the Cartagena expedition was staggering. Of the approximately 27,000 men who sailed from Jamaica, fewer than 10,000 returned. The great majority died not from Spanish shot but from yellow fever and other diseases. The Spanish lost perhaps 2,000 men, mostly in the outer fortifications.

The material losses were equally severe. Six British men-of-war were lost or destroyed, and the fleet returned to Jamaica in a state of dilapidation. The army was effectively annihilated as a fighting force. The dreams of capturing the Spanish treasure fleet, of seizing the gateway to Peru, of breaking the Spanish empire in America — all had evaporated in the miasmas of the Cartagena coast.

Return to Jamaica

The fleet arrived at Jamaica on 19 May 1741. The climate of the island did not improve matters: many died after their arrival, among them Lord Augustus Fitzroy, captain of the Orford. Vernon, “exceedingly dissatisfied with his colleague Wentworth,” ardently wished to return to England. But the King had taken a high opinion of his abilities, and the Duke of Newcastle’s letters were so flattering that the admiral allowed himself to be persuaded to remain (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 19361–19384).

On 26 May, a council of war resolved to attack Santiago de Cuba (St. Jago) on the island of Cuba. The expedition sailed on 1 July with eight ships of the line and some forty transports. The port of Walthenham was taken — but there the success ended. Wentworth declared it impossible to penetrate to Santiago by land; a council of war confirmed the opinion; and on 20 November the troops re-embarked. A third and even more ambitious plan — to land at Porto Bello and march across the Isthmus of Darien to attack Panama — was agreed in January 1742 but abandoned in March after the troops landed and the council of war, citing disease and inadequate forces, voted to re-embark (Campbell, vol. VI, lines 10781–10896).

In October 1742, both Vernon and Wentworth were recalled to England.

The Blame

The post-mortem in England was ferocious. The nation, which had greeted the news of Porto Bello with delirium and that of the fleet’s entry into Cartagena harbour with premature exultation, was now plunged into humiliation and rage. Someone had to be blamed.

Vernon’s supporters blamed Wentworth: the general was a coward, a fool, and a placeman who had mismanaged every phase of the land operations. The admiral wrote bitterly to the Duke of Newcastle that “no part of the disappointment was justly to be attributed to the sea forces… this unhappy event was principally owing to the command falling into the hands of General Wentworth, who had proved himself no ways equal to it.” He even claimed that “if the sole command had been in him, both in the Carthagena expedition, and in the Cuba one also, the British forces would have made themselves masters of Carthagena and St. Jago, and with the loss of much fewer men than had died through the imprudent conduct of General Wentworth” (Campbell, vol. VI, lines 10761–10780).

Wentworth’s defenders — fewer in number but not without substance — pointed to Vernon’s own faults: his delay at Jamaica, his decision to beat against the wind to Hispaniola rather than strike quickly at Havana, his refusal to bring his ships into the inner harbour to support the assault on St. Lazar, and his overbearing and contemptuous manner, which had poisoned the relationship between the services from the start. Campbell himself, though generally sympathetic to Vernon, delivers a balanced judgment: “both the admir al and general, to say no worse of them, were weak men” (vol. IV, lines 19292–19293).

The deepest responsibility, in Campbell’s analysis (vol. IV, lines 19252–19276), lay with the ministry — specifically with Sir Robert Walpole. A minister who had been forced into a war against his will could hardly be expected to prosecute it with zeal. The fleet had been “unnecessarily retarded at Spithead,” the troops “raw and undisciplined,” the expedition fatally delayed from the outset. “A bad beginning,” Campbell concludes, “commonly produces a bad ending.”

Consequences

The Cartagena expedition was a strategic disaster of the first order. It ended any possibility of major British offensive operations in the Caribbean for the remainder of the War of Jenkins’ Ear. The enormous resources poured into the expedition — estimated at over £2 million — had been utterly wasted. The Spanish empire in America, far from being broken, had demonstrated its resilience and its ability to defend its richest possessions.

The defeat accelerated the fall of Walpole. The ministry had already been gravely weakened by the outbreak of war it had not wanted; the catastrophe of Cartagena was the final blow. Walpole resigned in February 1742, after a narrow defeat in the House of Commons. The King wept at his departure (George II, lines 31–32).

For Vernon, the failure marked the end of his glory. Though he remained popular with the public — the man who had taken Porto Bello could do no wrong in the eyes of the London mob — his reputation among the Admiralty and the government was irreparably damaged. He was never again given a command of importance and was eventually struck from the Navy List in 1746 for his relentless criticism of the administration.

For Blas de Lezo, the victory at Cartagena was his crowning achievement. He died later in 1741, worn out by his exertions and his wounds, but he had cemented his place in Spanish history as the greatest naval commander of the age — the man who, with a handful of ill-equipped troops, had shattered the largest expedition Britain had ever sent across the Atlantic.

Historiography

The Cartagena expedition has been the subject of persistent historical controversy. British historians of the eighteenth century, drawing heavily on Vernon’s own version of events, tended to place the blame squarely on Wentworth. The general was portrayed as a timid, incompetent fool who had thrown away a certain victory. Later historians, beginning with Smollett (who had been present at the siege as a surgeon’s mate and whose account in his History of England is unusually vivid), offered a more nuanced view, noting Vernon’s own faults and the structural problems of divided command.

Modern scholarship has tended to rehabilitate Wentworth’s reputation to some degree, while acknowledging his limitations. The expedition was probably doomed from the start by the lateness of the season, the difficulty of the objective, and the poisoned relationship between the two commanders. The real lesson of Cartagena was that divided command in a theatre of operations as remote and challenging as the Caribbean was a recipe for disaster — a lesson that would not be fully learned until the Pitt-Granville system of unified strategic direction in the Seven Years’ War.
