Capture of Louisbourg (1758)

The Capture of Louisbourg (2 June – 26 July 1758) was the decisive British amphibious operation against the French fortress of Louisbourg on Cape Breton Island (Île Royale) during the Seven Years’ War. The fortress guarded the seaward approach to the St. Lawrence River and was the key to French Canada. Its fall after a seven-week siege opened the gateway to Quebec and set the stage for the British conquest of Canada the following year.

The expedition was the centrepiece of William Pitt the Elder’s grand strategy for 1758 — a massive combined-arms assault commanded by Major-General Jeffrey Amherst ashore and Admiral Edward Boscawen afloat. The siege demonstrated the new British doctrine of amphibious warfare, combined with the tactical brilliance of Brigadier-General James Wolfe, whose audacity at the landing and energy during the reduction made him the rising star of the British army.

Background

The Fortress

Louisbourg, constructed by the French between 1720 and 1740 at enormous expense, was considered the strongest fortress in North America. Situated on the south-east coast of Cape Breton Island, it commanded the entrance to the Gulf of St. Lawrence, through which all shipping to Quebec and Montreal had to pass. Its walls, sixty feet thick at the base in some places, enclosed a town with a fine harbour capable of sheltering a large fleet. The French called it the “Dunkirk of America” — a naval base and privateer haven that threatened British Nova Scotia and the New England fisheries.

The First Capture (1745)

Louisbourg had been captured once before. In 1745, during the War of the Austrian Succession, a force of New England militiamen under William Pepperrell, cooperating with Commodore Peter Warren’s Royal Navy squadron, had taken the fortress in a celebrated seven-week siege. The conquest was the greatest colonial military achievement of the age, and New Englanders regarded it with fierce pride.

The Return (1748)

Under the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), which ended the War of the Austrian Succession, Louisbourg was returned to France in exchange for Madras in India. The decision provoked fury in the American colonies — the New England merchant Thomas Hancock called it “giving a bone to a dog.” British colonists never forgave the ministry for surrendering a conquest won with their own blood and treasure. The fortress was restored to France, repaired, and strengthened.

The Renewed Threat

By 1756, with the outbreak of the Seven Years’ War, Louisbourg again menaced British North America. French privateers operating from its harbour preyed on New England shipping, and the fortress served as the staging point for French reinforcement of Canada. British defeats in 1756 and 1757 — the loss of Minorca, the failure of Lord Loudoun’s expedition against Louisbourg, the fall of Fort William Henry — had left the British public despondent. Pitt, who took control of war strategy in 1757, determined to break the pattern by striking at the heart of French power in North America.

The Expedition

Pitt’s Strategy

William Pitt the Elder grasped what earlier ministries had not: that France could not be defeated in Europe by land armies, but must be struck at her colonial possessions, her trade routes, and her navy. The capture of Louisbourg was the first great test of his strategy. He appointed Major-General Jeffrey Amherst — a steady, methodical professional soldier — to command the land forces, and Admiral Edward Boscawen — the aggressive “Old Dreadnought” — to command the fleet. As brigadiers under Amherst, Pitt chose three officers of proven enterprise: James Wolfe, Charles Lawrence (the governor of Nova Scotia), and Edward Whitmore.

The Fleet

Boscawen sailed from St. Helen’s on 19 February 1758 with a powerful fleet of forty-one ships of the line and eighteen frigates. His flagship was the Namur of ninety guns. The fleet included the Royal William under Sir Charles Hardy, the Princess Amelia under Philip Durell, and ships commanded by future luminaries including Captain George Brydges Rodney (later Lord Rodney). To prevent French reinforcements from reaching Louisbourg, Pitt’s strategy also deployed Admiral Osborne in the Mediterranean — who crushed Monsieur du Quesne’s Toulon squadron at the action off Cartagena on 28 February — and Sir Edward Hawke in the Bay of Biscay, who blockaded the French Atlantic ports and prevented the sailing of a second reinforcement fleet.

The Army

Amherst’s army numbered approximately 14,000 men, including regular infantry, grenadiers, light infantry, and artillery. The troops assembled at Halifax, Nova Scotia, where they trained for amphibious landings through the spring. The fleet, comprising 157 sail in total (including transports and store-ships), sailed from Halifax on 28 May 1758 and arrived in Gabarus Bay, about two leagues west of Louisbourg, on 2 June.

The Landing (8 June 1758)

The French Defences

The French governor, the Chevalier Augustin de Drucour, had prepared for the British assault with great energy. He threw up entrenchments, erected batteries, and formed a chain of redoubts stretching for two and a half miles along the coast. His garrison consisted of approximately 2,500 regular troops, supplemented by 600 armed burghers and Canadian militia. In the harbour, Drucour stationed six ships of the line and five frigates — including the seventy-four-gun Entreprenant, Prudent, and Bienfaisant — as floating batteries. Three of these ships were later sunk at the harbour entrance to block the channel.

The Delay

When the British fleet anchored on 2 June, a heavy surf was running that made landing impossible. For six days, the army chafed in the transports while the weather refused to moderate. Some officers urged Boscawen to call a council of war — the traditional refuge of indecisive commanders — but he refused, determined to land at all costs.

Wolfe’s Feint at Freshwater Cove

On 8 June, the weather moderated sufficiently for the attempt. Before dawn, the grenadiers and light infantry climbed into their boats. The frigates and sloops began a heavy bombardment of the French coastal batteries. The landing craft formed into three divisions under Wolfe, Whitmore, and Lawrence.

Wolfe’s division made for a cove known as Freshwater Cove (Anse-à-la-Cormorandiere), where the French had concentrated their strongest defences — entrenchments, abatis, and batteries covering every approach. As Wolfe’s boats rowed toward this position under heavy fire, the French defenders rushed to the spot, expecting the main assault. Wolfe pressed the feint just long enough to fix the enemy’s attention. Meanwhile, a small party of light infantry under Lieutenant-Colonel John Murray, supported by Wolfe’s own boat, had been probing further along the coast near a rocky inlet that the French had considered impassable. Wolfe himself leaped into the surf, sword drawn, and led his men ashore under heavy fire. The first men to land formed a desperate beachhead as the surf staved boats and soldiers drowned around them.

The account in Campbell’s Naval History captures the moment:

“When they approached the land they met with a warm reception from the enemy, and the surf ran so high that many of the boats were staved, and some of the soldiers drowned. General Wolfe leaped into the sea, and being followed by his whole division, formed his people on the beach, and marched intrepidly to the nearest battery. The other two divisions followed his example, and the enemy soon fled in confusion.” 1

The French, having committed their reserves to oppose the feint, could not prevent the main body from establishing a foothold. The landing cost the British three officers, four sergeants, and thirty-eight men killed or drowned, with five lieutenants and fifty-one men wounded, and about seventy boats lost. But the French abandoned seventeen pieces of cannon, fourteen swivels, two mortars, a furnace for heating shot, and large quantities of stores and ammunition.

The Siege

Investment

Amherst, having secured his beachhead, moved methodically to invest the town. He fortified his camp with redoubts and epaulements and began to drag his cannon and mortars forward through marshy ground — a laborious process that slowed the early stages of the siege. Drucour, meanwhile, withdrew his detachments from the outworks, destroyed all buildings within two miles of the ramparts, and prepared for a desperate defence.

Lighthouse Point

The British operations were hampered by fire from the Island Battery — a strong fortification on an island at the harbour mouth — as well as from the French ships of the line. Amherst detached Wolfe with a strong body of troops to march around the north-east side of the harbour and seize the Lighthouse Point on the opposite shore. Wolfe executed the march with great speed, and by 25 June he had erected a powerful battery on the heights that commanded the island. The British guns silenced the Island Battery and opened the harbour to direct fire.

The Systematic Reduction

Throughout July, the siege proceeded in a regular and scientific manner. The British approaches — trenches, parallels, and batteries — crept steadily closer to the fortress walls. The French responded with resolution, launching several sallies that were repulsed with loss. By 13 July, the besiegers had advanced to within six hundred yards of the covered way.

The British bombardment, including red-hot shot heated in furnaces, began to take its toll on the town and the ships in the harbour. On 21 July, a shell from the Lighthouse Point battery struck the seventy-four-gun Entreprenant. The ship caught fire and blew up with a tremendous explosion, and the flames spread to the Capricieux and the Celebre — both of sixty-four guns — which were also consumed. In a single afternoon, three of the six French ships of the line were destroyed, leaving only the Prudent and the Bienfaisant as vessels of force remaining in the harbour.

The Cut-Out Operation (25 July)

Boscawen, determined to eliminate the remaining French warships, ordered a daring night raid. All available boats from the fleet — a barge and pinnace from every ship — assembled under the stern of the Namur around noon on 25 July. They proceeded to Sir Charles Hardy’s squadron near the harbour mouth, where they formed into two divisions under Captains Laforey and Balfour.

The boats entered the harbour of Louisbourg at midnight, unperceived. The night was intensely dark, a thick fog adding to the concealment, while the incessant din of bombs, cannon, and musketry from the siege covered the sound of the oars. Each division surrounded its target — the Prudent and the Bienfaisant — and the crews scrambled aboard from all sides simultaneously. Resistance was feeble. The Prudent, being aground, was set on fire and burnt. The Bienfaisant was towed triumphantly out of the harbour, past the guns of the island battery, and secured as a prize. The operation was one of the most brilliant boat actions of the war.

The Surrender

With his ships destroyed or taken, his cannon dismounted, his garrison reduced and demoralised, and his ramparts battered to pieces in numerous places, the Chevalier Drucour had no choice but to capitulate. On 26 July 1758, he sent a letter to Boscawen offering to surrender on the same terms that had been granted to the British garrison at Minorca in 1756. Boscawen and Amherst rejected this proposal, insisting that the garrison must surrender as prisoners of war. Drucour, his situation hopeless, complied.

The capitulation was signed on 26 July, and British troops took possession of the fortress on 27 July. The terms extended also to the two islands of Cape Breton (Île Royale) and St. John (Prince Edward Island), which were ceded to His Britannic Majesty. The garrison — reduced from 3,100 to barely 2,000 effectives — marched out with the honours of war and became prisoners.

Aftermath

The Fortress Demolished

Pitt’s instructions had been explicit: Louisbourg was to be destroyed, not garrisoned. Britain had learned the lesson of 1745 — a fortress taken at great cost could not be held permanently at the end of a transatlantic supply line. British engineers demolished the fortifications systematically, blowing up the walls, filling the ditches, and rendering the harbour useless as a naval base. The destruction took months and was carried out with such thoroughness that Louisbourg never again served as a military installation. Only the fishing hamlets remained.

The St. Lawrence Opened

The fall of Louisbourg removed the cork from the bottle of the St. Lawrence. The river route to Quebec and Montreal — previously guarded by the guns of the fortress and the French squadron in its harbour — now lay open to the Royal Navy. Pitt immediately began planning the expedition that would culminate in the Capture of Quebec the following year. Wolfe, promoted to major-general and given command of the Quebec expedition, sailed into the St. Lawrence in June 1759 with a fleet of forty-nine ships — a passage that would have been impossible with Louisbourg in French hands.

Wolfe’s Promotion

Wolfe’s performance at Louisbourg — the daring of his landing, the speed of his march to Lighthouse Point, and his energy throughout the siege — established him as the outstanding officer of the expedition. Pitt, who had been watching Wolfe since the Rochefort raid of 1757, now had no hesitation in giving him the independent command that would make his reputation immortal. The siege had been the making of Wolfe.

Amherst’s Advance

Amherst, the cautious and methodical commander-in-chief, was rewarded with command of the forces advancing into Canada from the south. His slower but equally important campaign against Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) in 1758 was less successful — a costly repulse — but he continued to serve as commander-in-chief in North America, finally receiving the French surrender at Montreal in 1760.

Significance

The capture of Louisbourg was the first decisive British victory of the Seven Years’ War in North America. It validated Pitt’s strategy of amphibious power and established the template for the conquest of Canada. The victory brought the first good news to a British public demoralised by the reverses of 1756–1757 and unleashed a wave of celebration. Parliament voted thanks to Boscawen and Amherst, and the City of London — Pitt’s great bastion of support — voted its thanks to the fleet and army. The fall of Louisbourg was the beginning of the annus mirabilis that would make 1759 the most celebrated year in British military history.

Key Figures

  • Edward Boscawen (1711–1761) — “Old Dreadnought,” commanding admiral of the fleet. His refusal to call a council of war during the weather-delayed landing and his energetic direction of the naval blockade were critical to the victory.
  • Major-General Jeffrey Amherst (1717–1797) — Commander-in-chief of the British army. His methodical siegecraft, while slower than Wolfe might have preferred, was effective and economical of life.
  • James Wolfe (1727–1759) — Brigadier-general under Amherst. His audacious landing, seizure of Lighthouse Point, and tactical energy throughout the siege made him the hero of the operation and earned him command of the Quebec expedition.
  • Chevalier Augustin de Drucour (c. 1700–1762) — French governor of Louisbourg. He conducted a resolute defence under hopeless circumstances, refused earlier calls to surrender, and only capitulated when every ship was destroyed and his walls breached.
  • William Pitt the Elder (1708–1778) — British Secretary of State and architect of the strategy that captured Louisbourg. His choice of commanders — Boscawen, Amherst, and Wolfe — and his naval diversion operations (Osborne in the Mediterranean, Hawke in the Bay of Biscay) were essential to the victory.
  • Captains Laforey and Balfour — Commanders of the cut-out operation on the night of 25 July, who burned the Prudent and carried off the Bienfaisant in one of the most brilliant boat actions of the war.
