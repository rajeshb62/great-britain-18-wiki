Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773) and the Coercive Acts (1774)

The Boston Tea Party of 16 December 1773 — the destruction of 342 chests of East India Company tea, valued at approximately £10,000, by colonists disguised as Mohawk Indians — was the most dramatic act of defiance in the pre-Revolutionary crisis and the event that broke the fragile peace between Great Britain and her American colonies. The British response, a suite of punitive measures known as the Coercive Acts (1774), was intended to isolate and subdue Massachusetts. Instead, it unified the thirteen colonies in a common cause, led directly to the meeting of the First Continental Congress, and set the empire on an irreversible course toward civil war. The crisis revealed, in the starkest possible terms, the failure of both British coercion and American resistance to achieve their intended aims: Parliament could not govern the colonies without their consent, and the colonies could not assert their rights without severing their connection to the Crown.

Background: The Tea Act of May 1773

The Distresses of the East India Company

The East India Company in 1772–1773 was in a condition approaching financial collapse. The Company, which governed Bengal and exercised a monopoly over the eastern trade, had overextended itself: it held vast quantities of tea in its London warehouses — some 17 million pounds — which it could not sell at a profit. The American market was glutted with smuggled Dutch tea, cheaper than the Company’s product because the Company was burdened with the full British customs duty of one shilling per pound and the additional Townshend duty of threepence per pound, imposed in 1767 and retained by Lord North as a symbol of parliamentary sovereignty after the repeal of the other Townshend duties in 1770. The Company’s stock, which had traded at 280 in 1769, collapsed to 160 in 1772. It owed the Treasury £1.5 million and was petitioning Parliament for relief.1

Lord North, the Prime Minister, was determined to rescue the Company — not only because its collapse would destabilise British commerce and finance, but because he saw in the Company’s distress an opportunity to solve two problems at once: save the Company and sell its surplus tea to the American colonies. The legislation he devised, the Tea Act, received the royal assent on 10 May 1773.

The Provisions of the Tea Act

The Tea Act was not, in its technical provisions, a new tax. It did not impose any duty on tea that had not already existed. What it did was more subtle and, from the American perspective, more dangerous:

  1. It permitted the East India Company to export its tea directly to America, bypassing the London wholesale merchants through whom tea had previously been sold.
  2. It authorised the Company to appoint its own colonial consignees — favoured merchants in each colonial port — to receive and sell the tea on commission.
  3. Crucially, it remitted the one-shilling-per-pound British customs duty on tea re-exported to America, so that the Company would pay only the threepenny Townshend duty on arrival in the colonies.

The effect of these provisions was to place the East India Company’s tea on the American market at a price lower than the smuggled Dutch tea that the colonists had been drinking for years. For the first time, legally imported British tea would be cheaper than the contraband article. The colonists could buy their tea lawfully, pay the threepenny duty that represented the despised principle of parliamentary taxation, and still save money compared to what they had been paying the smugglers.2

The Colonial Perception

To the British ministry, the Tea Act was an ingenious commercial measure, a device that would rescue the East India Company, increase the revenue from the tea duty, and induce the colonists, by the irresistible logic of a lower price, to accept the principle of parliamentary taxation without ever having to acknowledge that they were doing so. Belsham records that the ministry considered the measure “a stroke of policy, which would at once relieve the Company, augment the revenue, and, by making the tea cheaper than that which the Americans smuggled from Holland, induce them voluntarily to submit to the payment of the duty.”3

The colonists saw it very differently. They perceived the Tea Act not as a commercial accommodation but as a constitutional trap. The lower price was the bait; the threepenny duty was the hook. If the colonists bought the Company’s tea, they would be — for the first time since the repeal of the Stamp Act — paying a tax imposed by Parliament for the purpose of raising revenue. The principle for which the Stamp Act Crisis had been fought, the principle for which the non-importation agreements had been maintained, the principle for which the Sons of Liberty had organised, would be surrendered — not by force, but by the quiet, daily act of buying a cup of tea.

The Committees of Correspondence, established by Samuel Adams after the Boston Massacre and the Gaspee affair, acted with remarkable speed and coordination. From Boston to Charleston, the patriots resolved that no tea from the East India Company would be permitted to land. The consignees — the merchants appointed by the Company to receive the tea — were denounced as tools of ministerial tyranny. In New York and Philadelphia, public meetings compelled the consignees to resign. In Charleston, the tea was landed but left to rot in damp warehouses, unsold. In Boston, the crisis came to a head, because in Boston the consignees — two of whom were sons of Governor Thomas Hutchinson, and another a nephew — refused to resign.4

The Boston Tea Party: 16 December 1773

The Stand-off in Boston Harbour

The first of three tea ships, the Dartmouth, arrived in Boston Harbour on 27 November 1773, commanded by Captain Hall and loaded with 114 chests of tea. Two more ships — the Eleanor and the Beaver — arrived in the following days. Under the Townshend duty regulations, the tea must be unloaded and the duty paid within twenty days of the ship’s arrival, or the ship and its cargo would be seized by the customs authorities. For the Dartmouth, that deadline fell on 17 December.5

The town of Boston was in a state of high agitation. Mass meetings were held at Faneuil Hall and, as the crowds swelled, at the Old South Meeting House. Samuel Adams, John Hancock, and the Boston Committee of Correspondence orchestrated the resistance. The meetings resolved, night after night, that the tea must not be landed, that the duty must not be paid, and that the ships must be sent back to England without unloading — a course that the law did not permit, for the customs officers had the power to seize the cargo if the duty was not paid, and the consignees, who had the legal authority to receive the tea, refused to resign.

Governor Thomas Hutchinson — who had returned to Boston after a year in England and who believed as firmly as the ministry in the necessity of enforcing the law — was determined to break the deadlock. He refused to grant the ships clearance to leave the harbour without payment of the duty. He ordered the Castle William garrison to be reinforced. He insisted that the law must take its course: the tea must be landed, the duty must be paid, and the authority of Parliament must be maintained.6

The Final Meeting

On the afternoon of Thursday, 16 December 1773 — the last day before the twenty-day deadline for the Dartmouth — a vast crowd, estimated at between five and seven thousand people, packed the Old South Meeting House. It was the largest gathering that Boston had ever seen — a crowd that represented a substantial portion of the entire population of the town, which numbered roughly 16,000. The meeting had been called to hear a final message from Governor Hutchinson, who had withdrawn to his country house at Milton, several miles from Boston.

The messenger arrived shortly after noon. Hutchinson had refused once more: the ships would not be permitted to leave without paying the duty. The law would be enforced. The crowd listened in silence. Then Samuel Adams rose and, according to the testimony of witnesses, pronounced the fateful words: “This meeting can do nothing more to save the country.”7

The Destruction of the Tea

Those words were the signal. A war-whoop sounded from the gallery of the church — a cry that was answered from the street. A group of men, variously estimated at between thirty and fifty, who had been waiting in the near-darkness of the winter afternoon, made their way to Griffin’s Wharf, where the three tea ships lay at anchor. They were disguised as Mohawk Indians, with blankets and war-paint, their faces blackened with soot and grease — a theatrical disguise that served both to conceal their identities and to make the symbolic claim that they were no longer British subjects, but men of the forest, beyond the reach of British law.8

The men divided themselves into three parties, boarded the three vessels, and set to work with systematic efficiency. They hauled 342 chests of tea from the holds, broke them open with hatchets and axes, and emptied the contents into the waters of Boston Harbour. The work took the entire night, from about seven in the evening until the small hours of Friday morning. By the light of a rising moon, the harbour was covered with a brown scum of tea leaves, driven by the tide against the wharves and the sides of the ships. The crowd on the shore watched in absolute silence. No property was damaged beyond the tea itself; no locks were forced, no other cargo disturbed, no violence offered to any person. When the work was done, the men swept the decks clean, dispersed into the night, and disappeared into the town.9

The total value of the tea destroyed was approximately £10,000 — a sum that the East India Company would later be compensated for by the British government as part of the peace settlement of 1783. The names of the participants were carefully guarded; even to this day, only a handful of the “Mohawks” have been definitively identified. Among them were men who would later distinguish themselves in the Revolutionary War, but at the time, the destruction of the tea was an act of collective defiance in which the participants were sworn to secrecy and bound by a common risk.

The British Reaction: Lord North Determined on Punishment

News Reaches London

The news of the Boston Tea Party reached London on 19 January 1774, conveyed in dispatches from Governor Hutchinson that described the destruction of the tea in the most damning terms. The ministry was already in a combative mood: the East India Company’s crisis had been aggravated by the American defiance, and the political opposition — Lord Chatham, Edmund Burke, and the Rockingham Whigs — had been warning for years that the policy of parliamentary taxation would lead to catastrophe. Now the catastrophe had arrived.

Lord North’s reaction was decisive and uncompromising. He had pursued a policy of studied patience since the partial repeal of the Townshend duties in 1770; he had allowed the tea duty to remain as a symbol of sovereignty while hoping that time and commercial self-interest would reconcile the colonists to the principle. The Boston Tea Party had shown that this policy had failed. The colonists had not merely evaded the tax; they had destroyed the symbol. The entire legal and constitutional authority of the British Parliament in America had been challenged by an act of organised, defiant violence.10

North’s position was supported by George III, who had long believed that the American crisis required firmness. The King, who had endured the humiliation of the Stamp Act repeal and the partial retreat on the Townshend duties, now saw the destruction of the tea as an intolerable affront to the dignity of the Crown. The King’s letters from this period reveal his conviction that the colonies must be subdued: “The die is now cast,” he wrote to Lord North. “The colonies must either submit or triumph.”11

The Parliamentary Debate

The debate in Parliament on the American crisis opened in March 1774, and it was among the most consequential in British history. North’s ministry laid before the Commons a series of punitive bills, collectively designed to bring Massachusetts to submission and to demonstrate that the British Parliament would not be defied. The opposition fought each measure with the full force of their considerable eloquence.

Lord Chatham — now in the House of Lords, his health failing but his intellectual powers undiminished — rose on several occasions to denounce the ministry’s policy. He had warned in 1766 that taxing the colonies without their consent would lose America; he now warned that coercing them would lose America faster. He proposed a comprehensive settlement: the repeal of all duties imposed since 1763, the recognition of the Continental Congress, the withdrawal of troops from Boston, and a solemn declaration of Parliament’s limited sovereignty over the colonies in matters of trade only. His proposals were rejected.12

Edmund Burke delivered two of his greatest speeches during these debates — the speech on American taxation (April 1774) and the speech on conciliation with the colonies (March 1775, but anticipated in his 1774 arguments). Burke’s position was the most philosophically developed of any British statesman: he did not deny Parliament’s right to tax the colonies, but he argued that the exercise of that right was inexpedient, that the American character was fundamentally resistant to arbitrary power, and that the empire should be governed by persuasion and interest rather than force. “The question with me is, not whether you have a right to render your people miserable, but whether it is not in your interest to make them happy,” Burke declared. His arguments did not prevail.13

The House of Commons — and the country — were overwhelmingly in favour of coercion. The Boston Tea Party had outraged British public opinion. The merchants of London and the commercial cities, who had opposed the Stamp Act in 1766 because it disrupted trade, now supported the ministry’s punitive measures. They saw the destruction of the tea not as a political protest but as an act of wanton vandalism against the property of a great commercial corporation. The ministry’s bills passed with large majorities.

The Coercive Acts (1774)

The four statutes that Parliament enacted to punish Massachusetts and restore imperial authority in America were known collectively in Britain as the Coercive Acts, and in America as the Intolerable Acts. They were passed between March and June 1774, and they transformed the character of the imperial crisis.

The Boston Port Act (31 March 1774)

The first and most immediately devastating of the Coercive Acts. The Boston Port Act ordered that the port of Boston should be closed to all commerce until such time as the East India Company had been compensated for the destroyed tea and the King was satisfied that peace and order had been restored in the town. The customs house was removed to Salem; the seat of government was transferred to Salem as well. No goods could be loaded or unloaded at Boston’s wharves — only coastal vessels carrying fuel and provisions were exempted.

The effect was catastrophic. Boston was a port city whose entire economy depended on maritime commerce. The closure of the port threw thousands of seamen, dockworkers, and tradesmen out of work. The town faced destitution. Belsham records that the Act was “calculated to reduce the town of Boston to a state of beggary, and to compel the inhabitants by the pressure of famine to submit to the authority of Parliament.”14 The Act was, in effect, a commercial blockade — the siege of a city by act of Parliament.

The Massachusetts Government Act (20 May 1774)

This act was a fundamental alteration of the constitutional structure of Massachusetts. It revoked the colonial charter that had been in force since 1691 and vested the appointment of the Governor’s Council — previously elected by the colonial assembly — in the Crown alone. It forbade the calling of town meetings without the Governor’s permission. It gave the Governor the power to appoint and remove all judges, sheriffs, and magistrates at his pleasure.

The effect of the Massachusetts Government Act was to destroy the institutions of representative government that had been the pride of the colony. The town meeting — the foundational institution of New England democracy, the forum in which ordinary citizens debated and decided the affairs of their communities — was effectively abolished. The Governor, armed with powers unprecedented in the colonial period, became a virtual dictator. The Act was, as Belsham observes, “a direct attack upon the liberties of the colony, which the Americans regarded with the utmost alarm and indignation.”15

The Administration of Justice Act (20 May 1774)

This act, which the colonists called the “Murder Act,” provided that any royal official or soldier accused of a capital offence committed in the course of suppressing riots or enforcing the revenue laws could be tried in another colony or in Great Britain, rather than in the colony where the offence was alleged to have occurred. The Governor was given the power to remove the venue of any trial if he believed that a fair trial could not be obtained locally.

The purpose of the Act was to protect British officials from the kind of local prejudice that had — in the view of the ministry — made a travesty of the Boston Massacre trial, in which the soldiers had been acquitted by a Boston jury but not before being subjected to a prosecution that the ministry regarded as politically motivated. To the colonists, however, the Act was a licence to murder: a British soldier could shoot an American colonist with impunity, confident that if he were prosecuted at all, he would be tried in a British court far from the scene of the crime, before a jury that had no sympathy for the victim.16

The Quartering Act (2 June 1774)

This act authorised the Governor of Massachusetts to billet British troops in “uninhabited houses, outhouses, barns, or other buildings” in the colony, if suitable barracks were not available. It was an amendment to the earlier Quartering Act of 1765, which had required colonial assemblies to provide quarters and provisions for British troops but had not authorised the Governor to commandeer private property without legislative consent.

The Quartering Act was less immediately provocative than the other Coercive Acts, but it revived the deepest American fears about the standing army and the threat it posed to civil liberty. The presence of troops in the midst of a civilian population, quartered without the consent of the local legislature, was a violation of the constitutional principle that the military must be subordinate to the civil power. The Act seemed to confirm the colonists’ darkest suspicions about the ministry’s intentions: they were to be governed not by law but by bayonets.17

The Quebec Act (22 June 1774)

Although the Quebec Act was passed separately from the four Coercive Acts, and was designed to establish a permanent government for the province of Quebec — acquired from France in 1763 — it was enacted in the same parliamentary session and was regarded by the American colonists as an integral part of the ministerial design to enslave them.

The Quebec Act extended the boundaries of the Province of Quebec southward to the Ohio River and westward to the Mississippi, encompassing the vast territory between the Great Lakes and the Ohio — lands that the American colonies of New York, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and Massachusetts claimed under their original charters. It established a government for Quebec that was entirely autocratic: an appointed Governor and Council, no elected assembly, and the introduction of French civil law while retaining English criminal law. Most alarmingly to the Protestant colonists of New England, the Act granted the Roman Catholic Church in Quebec the right to collect tithes and maintain its existing privileges — a toleration of “popery” that inflamed every anti-Catholic prejudice of the American Puritan tradition.

To the colonists, the Quebec Act was conclusive proof that the British government intended to destroy their liberties. In a single legislative stroke, Parliament had established a despotic government on the American continent, recognised the Catholic Church, and snatched from the colonies their western lands — the territory for which they had fought in the Seven Years’ War. The conjunction of the Coercive Acts and the Quebec Act, Belsham records, “excited the most alarming apprehensions in the minds of the colonists,” who saw in them “a deliberate and systematic design to destroy their liberties, both civil and religious.”18

The Result: From Coercion to Continental Union

The Intolerable Acts and Colonial Unity

The effect of the Coercive Acts was the precise opposite of what the British ministry had intended. North and George III had expected that isolating Massachusetts — punishing the ringleader while leaving the other colonies untouched — would divide the Americans, that the other colonies would abandon Massachusetts to its fate rather than share in its punishment. In this expectation, they were catastrophically wrong.

The Acts were published in the American newspapers within weeks of their passage, and the response was a convulsion of indignation from New Hampshire to Georgia. The Committees of Correspondence — the network that Samuel Adams and his allies had been building since the Gaspee affair — carried the news from colony to colony with extraordinary speed. The other colonies did not abandon Massachusetts; they rallied to its defence. The cause of Boston became the cause of America. The Port of Boston was closed? Then the other colonies would send provisions to feed Boston. The Massachusetts charter was abrogated? Then every colony’s charter was at risk. The Administration of Justice Act threatened trial by jury? Then no colonist was safe from arbitrary power.19

The colonial argument, from this point forward, ceased to be about particular acts of Parliament and became a general claim that the British government was engaged in a systematic conspiracy against American liberty. The Quebec Act transformed a constitutional dispute into something deeper: a struggle for the survival of Protestant, English liberty against the forces of popery and despotism. The language of the American protests after 1774 is notably harsher and more apocalyptic than the language of the Stamp Act crisis — the colonists were no longer petitioning for redress but arming themselves against a perceived tyranny.

The First Continental Congress (September–October 1774)

The colonial response to the Coercive Acts was an act of political coordination without precedent in American history. At the call of the Massachusetts House of Representatives, and after a series of inter-colonial exchanges, delegates from twelve colonies — all except Georgia — assembled in Philadelphia in September 1774. This was the First Continental Congress, and it marked, for all practical purposes, the birth of a unified American political nation.20

The Congress met at Carpenters’ Hall from 5 September to 26 October 1774. The delegates included many of the most eminent men in America: George Washington, Patrick Henry, and Richard Henry Lee of Virginia; John Adams, Samuel Adams, and John Hancock of Massachusetts; John Dickinson of Pennsylvania; John Jay of New York. The Congress was divided between radicals — who argued for a complete cessation of trade with Britain, the preparation of military defences, and an uncompromising assertion of American rights — and moderates — who hoped for a negotiated settlement that would restore the relationship on the basis of the colonial position before 1763.

The result was a compromise. The Congress adopted a Declaration of Rights and Grievances that asserted the colonists’ entitlement to “life, liberty, and property” and their exclusive right to tax themselves. It approved a Continental Association — an agreement to boycott British goods, to cease all exports to Britain, and to enforce the boycott through locally elected committees. It addressed a petition to the King, the Olive Branch Petition, expressing loyalty to the Crown and asking for the repeal of the Coercive Acts. And it resolved that if the Acts were not repealed by September 1775, the colonies would cease all exports to Great Britain.21

The Congress was not yet a revolutionary body. It did not declare independence. It did not establish a government. But it created the machinery of a united colonial resistance: a system of committees that would enforce the boycott, a network of correspondence that would keep the colonies informed, and a precedent for collective action that would, within two years, produce the American Declaration of Independence.

The Appointment of General Gage

In April 1774, as the Coercive Acts were passing through Parliament, the British ministry took a further step that underscored the new trajectory of imperial policy. General Thomas Gage, the commander-in-chief of British forces in North America, was appointed Governor of Massachusetts in place of Thomas Hutchinson, who had been recalled. Gage was a competent soldier who had served in America for nearly twenty years and had married an American woman. He knew the country and the people. But his appointment signalled that the ministry had abandoned the attempt to govern Massachusetts by civil means and was preparing to govern it by military force.

Gage arrived in Boston in May 1774 to find a town already under military occupation. Additional troops were being dispatched from England and Ireland — reinforcements that would bring the garrison in Boston to 4,000 men by the autumn of 1774. Gage’s instructions were to enforce the Coercive Acts and to maintain order. He attempted to do so with a mixture of firmness and moderation, forbidding provocative troop movements while insisting on the execution of the law. But he was caught between an inflexible ministry at home and an increasingly defiant population in Massachusetts. By the autumn of 1774, the colonial militia were drilling on village greens from Lexington to Worcester, and the powder and arms of the province were being removed from British control. Gage wrote to London requesting 20,000 additional troops. The request was refused. The war that would lose Britain the American colonies was now only months away.22

Key Figures

Frederick North, Lord North (1732–1792)

Prime Minister of Great Britain from 1770 to 1782. North was the architect of the Tea Act and the Coercive Acts, though he acted throughout in close consultation with George III. He was a man of considerable intelligence and personal amiability — witty, hardworking, and deeply loyal to the Crown. But he was not a strong minister. His policy of conciliation through commercial concession (the Tea Act) and his subsequent resort to coercion (the Coercive Acts) revealed a fundamental strategic incoherence: he could not decide whether to buy off the colonies or crush them, and he ended up doing neither. His decision to punish Massachusetts collectively, and his failure to anticipate that the other colonies would rally to Boston’s defence, was the greatest single political miscalculation of his career.

George III (1738–1820)

King of Great Britain and Ireland. George III was, from the beginning of the American crisis to its disastrous conclusion, the most consistent advocate of coercion. He regarded the Boston Tea Party as an act of rebellion that must be met with overwhelming force. His letters to Lord North during the Coercive Acts crisis reveal a monarch determined to uphold the authority of Parliament at any cost. “The colonies must either submit or triumph,” he wrote — and he was prepared for triumph to be impossible. The King’s personal responsibility for the Coercive Acts and the subsequent war was substantial: he was not a figurehead but an active, interventionist monarch who shaped the direction of government policy at every critical juncture.23

Samuel Adams (1722–1803)

The political organiser of the American resistance in Massachusetts. Adams was the master of revolutionary mobilisation: he orchestrated the mass meetings at the Old South Meeting House, coordinated the Committees of Correspondence, and shaped the narrative that turned the destruction of the tea from an act of vandalism into a heroic defence of liberty. His words at the decisive meeting — “This meeting can do nothing more to save the country” — were the signal for the Tea Party. Adams understood, as the British ministry did not, that the cause of Boston would become the cause of all America. He was, in Belsham’s judgment, a man of “inflexible resolution and uncommon sagacity” — a radical whose vision of American independence, still unspoken in 1773, was already guiding his actions.24

John Hancock (1737–1793)

Boston merchant and political leader. Hancock was one of the wealthiest men in Massachusetts, a merchant-prince whose fortune was built on the maritime trade that the Boston Port Act would destroy. He was a prominent member of the Sons of Liberty, the chairman of the Boston Committee of Correspondence, and the president of the Massachusetts Provincial Congress. His role in the Tea Party crisis was that of an organiser and financier: he presided over the mass meetings at the Old South Meeting House and was one of the leaders of the Boston resistance. A British effort in 1774 to arrest Hancock and Samuel Adams on charges of high treason — an effort that was abandoned — underscored his prominence in the revolutionary movement.

Thomas Hutchinson (1711–1780)

Governor of Massachusetts Bay from 1769 to 1774, and the man who bore the full brunt of the Tea Party crisis. Hutchinson was a native Bostonian, a scholar and historian who had written the history of Massachusetts, and a man of undoubted integrity. But he was also an unwavering believer in parliamentary supremacy. His refusal to allow the tea ships to leave Boston Harbour without paying the duty — a refusal based on his understanding of the law and his duty as Governor — made him the target of a fury he could not withstand. He was the last civil Governor of Massachusetts. Recalled to England in the summer of 1774, he spent the remainder of his life in exile, writing his history and corresponding with the ministry about the colony he would never see again. He died in London in 1780, a figure of genuine tragedy — a good man destroyed by events he could neither control nor understand.25

General Thomas Gage (1719–1787)

Commander-in-chief of British forces in North America and, from May 1774, Governor of Massachusetts. Gage was a professional soldier of considerable experience, having served in America during the Seven Years’ War and married an American woman. He understood the colonists better than most of his contemporaries in London. But his appointment as Governor marked a decisive shift from civil to military government in Massachusetts. Gage’s attempt to enforce the Coercive Acts while avoiding armed conflict was doomed from the start: the colonists were arming faster than he could receive reinforcements, and his requests for 20,000 troops were rejected by a ministry that refused to believe how serious the situation had become. He commanded the British forces at the Battles of Lexington and Concord (April 1775) and at the Battle of Bunker Hill (June 1775), and was recalled to England in October 1775.

Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790)

The great American polymath and statesman, who was living in London as the agent of several American colonies during the Tea Party crisis. Franklin had been deeply involved in the prelude to the crisis: his leaking of the Hutchinson letters in 1772 had inflamed the Massachusetts controversy, and he had been subjected to a humiliating public dressing-down at the Privy Council in January 1774 by Solicitor-General Alexander Wedderburn. The Tea Party and the Coercive Acts transformed Franklin from a conciliator into a revolutionary. He left England in March 1775, returned to Philadelphia, and within months was sitting in the Second Continental Congress and helping to draft the Declaration of Independence.

  5. The Dartmouth (Captain Hall), the Eleanor (Captain Coffin), and the Beaver (Captain Heed) were the three tea ships. A fourth ship, the William, was wrecked off Cape Cod before reaching Boston.   6. Hutchinson’s position was legally correct but politically suicidal. Under the Navigation Acts and the customs regulations, the Governor had no authority to permit the ships to depart without unloading and paying the duty. The ships could be seized by customs officers, the cargo confiscated, and the captains held liable. Hutchinson’s choice was between enforcing the law and being seen as complicit in its violation. ↩

The ships were otherwise untouched — a point of pride among the participants, who regarded themselves as disciplined protesters against property, not looters. ↩

  11. George III to Lord North, January 1774.   13. Burke’s Speech on American Taxation was delivered on 19 April 1774. The speech was published in London and circulated widely in America, where it did much to sustain the colonists’ faith that they had allies in the British Parliament. ↩

  15. Belsham, Memoirs of the Reign of George III, Vol. II, on the Massachusetts Government Act, 14 Geo. III, c. 45. The Act was denounced in the colonies as a violation of the fundamental compact between the Crown and the people of Massachusetts, as embodied in the charter of 1691. ↩

  16. The Administration of Justice Act, 14 Geo. III, c. 39, was the most controversial of the Coercive Acts in America and the one that provoked the most bitter denunciations. The colonists’ name for it — the “Murder Act” — reflected their conviction that it was designed to protect British soldiers from punishment for killing Americans. ↩

  17. The Quartering Act, 14 Geo. III, c. 54, was an amendment to the Quartering Act of 1765, which had been a source of friction since its passage. The new Act’s explicit authorisation of quartering in private buildings — even uninhabited ones — was regarded as a violation of the spirit, if not the letter, of the Third Amendment to what would become the United States Constitution. ↩

  18. Belsham, Memoirs of the Reign of George III, Vol. II, on the cumulative impact of the Coercive Acts and the Quebec Act. The Quebec Act, 14 Geo. III, c. 83, was the most consequential piece of imperial legislation of the eighteenth century after the American Declaration of Independence itself — not only for its immediate effect on the American crisis but for its long-term consequences for the government of Canada. ↩

  19. Belsham records that “the other colonies, instead of abandoning Massachusetts to her fate, united with her in opposing the Acts, and sent her supplies of provisions and money.” The speed and solidarity of the colonial response — within weeks of the publication of the Boston Port Act, relief shipments were on their way from towns as far south as South Carolina — was a revelation to the ministry and a vindication of the Committees of Correspondence. ↩

  20. Belsham provides an extended account of the proceedings of the First Continental Congress, noting that the delegates were “men of the first abilities in the colonies,” that their debates were conducted with “great order and solemnity,” and that their resolutions were adopted with a unanimity that astonished the British ministry. ↩

  21. The Declaration of Rights and Grievances, adopted on 14 October 1774, asserted the rights of the colonists to “life, liberty, and property” — the language of John Locke — and declared that the colonists were “entitled to all the rights, liberties, and immunities of free and natural-born subjects within the realm of England.” The Continental Association, adopted on 20 October 1774, was a detailed plan for a complete boycott of British trade, enforced by locally elected committees. ↩

  22. Gage’s dispatches to the ministry during the summer and autumn of 1774 are among the most important documents of the pre-Revolutionary period. He reported that “the people of the country are arming with all possible expedition,” that the militia were “perfectly disciplined,” and that the rebellion was “general throughout the whole continent.” His request for 20,000 troops was dismissed in London as exaggerated and alarmist. ↩

  23. George III’s role in the Coercive Acts crisis is one of the most contested questions in the historiography of the American Revolution. The traditional Whig view — that the King was a meddlesome tyrant who drove the colonies to rebellion — has been modified by more recent scholarship, which emphasises that George III was acting within the constitutional conventions of the eighteenth-century monarchy. But the King’s consistent advocacy of coercion, his refusal to countenance any compromise, and his personal direction of the ministry’s American policy made him, in effect, the architect of the Coercive Acts and the war that followed. ↩

  24. Belsham’s characterisation of Samuel Adams reflects the British Whig view of him as a formidable and dangerous opponent, a man of “inflexible resolution and uncommon sagacity” who understood that the destruction of the tea would produce consequences far beyond the immediate crisis. ↩

  25. Hutchinson’s tragedy was that of a man caught between incompatible loyalties. He loved Massachusetts and believed he was serving its true interests by upholding the rule of law. But the colonists saw him as a traitor, and the British ministry, having used him as its instrument, discarded him when he became a liability. His History of the Province of Massachusetts Bay, completed in exile, is a work of genuine scholarship and a poignant record of a world destroyed by forces that its author could neither control nor fully understand. ↩
