Regency Crisis (1788–1789)

The Regency Crisis of 1788–1789 was the most dangerous constitutional crisis of George III’s reign — a three-month period in which the King was incapacitated by mental illness, the machinery of government was thrown into paralysis, and the Pitt ministry confronted the prospect of being swept from power by a Regent hostile to its policies. The crisis tested the unwritten British constitution to its limits, raised fundamental questions about the location of sovereignty, and exposed the deep partisan fissures beneath the surface of Hanoverian politics. It was resolved only by the King’s unexpected recovery in February 1789, just as the opposition prepared to take power.

Background: The King’s Illness

The Onset (Summer 1788)

George III had enjoyed generally robust health through the first twenty-eight years of his reign. In the summer of 1788, however, he began to suffer from what his physicians described as a “billous disorder.” He was advised to try the mineral waters at Cheltenham, which he was believed to drink in too profuse a quantity. His health appeared to improve during his residence there; he amused himself and gratified his people by excursions in the vicinity, displaying much condescension and affability, and was everywhere received with loud acclamations.

But the improvement was illusory. On his return to Windsor Castle late in the summer, his illness returned with new and alarming symptoms. By the end of October 1788, it could no longer be concealed that the malady of the King was of a nature peculiarly afflictive and dreadful. A mental derangement had taken place which rendered him totally incapable of public business. He suffered from violent fits of delirium, incessant talking, hallucinations, and physical agitation so severe that he had to be placed in a straitwaistcoat.

Modern historians have diagnosed the King’s condition as porphyria — a metabolic disorder of the blood that can produce the symptoms of acute delirium, abdominal pain, and a racing pulse. At the time, his physicians were at a loss: Dr. Francis Willis, a clergyman turned mad-doctor, and Dr. Richard Warren, the leading physician of the age, disagreed fundamentally about the nature of the illness and the likelihood of recovery. Willis was cautiously optimistic; Warren pronounced the case hopeless.

The Constitutional Problem

The King’s incapacity created a problem for which the constitution had made no explicit provision. The sovereign was the linchpin of the British executive — he opened and closed Parliament, gave the royal assent to bills, appointed ministers, created peers, and commanded the armed forces. If the King could not perform these functions, who could? The heir apparent, the Prince of Wales, was the natural candidate — but by what authority would he act, and with what powers?

The Political Battle

The Stakes

The political dimension of the crisis was what made it explosive. William Pitt the Younger had been Prime Minister since December 1783, having come to power through the King’s decisive intervention against the Fox-North Coalition. Pitt’s majority in the House of Commons was solid but dependent on the Crown’s support — and on the King’s personal confidence in him.

The Prince of Wales, by contrast, was a devoted ally of Charles James Fox. He had long chafed at his father’s refusal to grant him a role in government or settle his debts; his court at Carlton House was a Whig salon where Fox, Edmund Burke, and Richard Brinsley Sheridan were the leading figures. If the Prince became Regent with full royal powers, Pitt’s dismissal was all but certain. The Whig opposition, led by Fox and the Duke of Portland, stood ready to form the ministry they had been denied since 1783.

Fox himself had been travelling on the continent when news of the King’s illness reached him. He returned to England with “incredible expedition” — a journey that contemporaries noted as evidence of his eagerness for the impending change. A new administration, consisting of the principal members of the late coalition ministry (with Lord North alone excepted), was believed already to be formed.

The Opening of Parliament (November 1788)

Parliament had been prorogued to the 20th of November. A few days before that date, a circular letter was issued by the ministers signifying the impracticability of a further prorogation and requesting the attendance of the members. When Parliament assembled, the state of the King’s health was formally notified to the House of Peers by the Lord Chancellor, and to the Commons by Pitt. Since the session could not be opened in the regular mode (the King’s speech from the throne being impossible), an adjournment of fourteen days was recommended.

When Parliament reassembled on 4 December 1788, a report of the Board of Privy Council was presented to both houses, containing an examination of the royal physicians. It was suggested that Parliament should rest satisfied without any more direct information, especially since the examinations of council had been taken upon oath — which the Commons had no power to administer.

Fox’s Argument: The Prince’s Inherent Right

The decisive moment came when Pitt moved for the appointment of a committee to search the journals for precedents of proceedings adopted in former instances when the sovereign authority was suspended by sickness or infirmity. Charles James Fox rose to oppose the motion, arguing that it was calculated only for delay.

Fox advanced what he claimed was a proposition deducible from the principles of the constitution and the analogy of the law of hereditary succession: that whenever the sovereign was incapable of exercising the functions of his high office, the heir apparent, if of full age and capacity, had as indisputable a claim to the exercise of the executive authority — in the name and on behalf of the sovereign during his incapacity — as in the case of his natural demise.

This was a bold and, as it turned out, a disastrous argument. By asserting an inherent right in the Prince of Wales, Fox handed Pitt the most powerful weapon he could have desired.

Pitt’s Counter: The Right of Parliament

William Pitt the Younger immediately rose with much apparent warmth and declared that the assertion made by Fox was “little short of TREASON against the CONSTITUTION.” He pledged himself to prove that the heir apparent had no more right to the exercise of the executive power than any other person — and that it belonged entirely to the two remaining branches of the legislature to make such provision for supplying the temporary deficiency as they might think proper.

Pitt’s argument was masterfully framed. He invoked the popular and primary axioms of government:

“When the regular exercise of the powers of government was from any cause suspended, to whom could the right of providing a remedy for the existing defect devolve, but to the people, from whom all the powers of government originated? To assert an inherent right in the Prince of Wales to assume the government was virtually to revive those exploded ideas of the divine and indefeasible authority of princes which had so justly sunk into contempt and almost into oblivion.”

The irony was not lost on contemporaries. Fox — the great advocate of the rights of the people — had become the defender of prerogative. Pitt — who had been loudly accused of deserting the principles of liberty — appeared in the advantageous light of their intrepid and zealous assertor.

The Debate in the Lords

The same doctrine was debated in the House of Lords, where Lord Camden reprobated Fox’s argument with great severity. It was defended with eloquence and ability by Lord Loughborough and Lord Stormont, who concluded his speech by recommending an immediate address to the Prince of Wales, entreating him to assume the exercise of the royal authority.

But the ministry’s advantage was becoming apparent. The abstract question of right — by aligning the Whigs with an indefeasible hereditary claim — was proving politically damaging to the opposition. The Duke of York, speaking in the Prince’s name, sought to disengage his brother from Fox’s doctrine: “No claim of right had been advanced by the Prince of Wales; and he was confident that his brother too well understood the sacred principles which seated the House of Brunswick upon the throne, ever to assume or exercise any power, be his claim what it might, that was not derived from the will of the people expressed by their representatives.”

The Resolutions (December 1788)

On 16 December 1788, in the committee on the state of the nation, Pitt moved two declaratory resolutions: first, that the royal authority was interrupted; and second, that it was the duty of the two houses of Parliament to provide the means of supplying that defect. After vehement debate, the resolutions were carried by 268 votes to 204.

On 23 December, a third resolution passed, empowering the Lord Chancellor to affix the Great Seal to such bill of limitations as might be necessary to restrict the power of the future Regent. This mode of procedure was opposed with great animation by Lord North, who called it “a pageant, a fictitious being” set up to give the force of law to the decisions of two houses. It was styled in the heat of debate “an insulting fraud,” “a mockery of legislation,” and “a legal forgery.” An amendment requesting the Prince of Wales to take upon himself the administration of the government was negatived by 251 to 178.

The resolutions passed the Lords on a division of 66 to 99.

The Regency Bill and Pitt’s Restrictions

On 16 January 1789, Pitt introduced the plan of limitations in the House of Commons. The proposed restrictions were severe:

  • The Prince could not create peers
  • The Prince could not grant any office, reversion, or pension for any term other than during the King’s pleasure
  • The Prince could not dispose of Crown property
  • The care of the King’s person and the disposition of the royal household — including the patronage of some four hundred places, among them the great offices of Lord Steward, Lord Chamberlain, and Master of the Horse — were to be committed to Queen Charlotte

This last provision was the most controversial. By separating the court from the state, Pitt’s plan ensured that the patronage network of the Crown — the lubricant of Hanoverian politics — would remain in hands friendly to the outgoing ministry, not available to the incoming Regent. The opposition denounced it as a scheme to establish a weak government and a strong opposition: a fortress from which Pitt, when out of office, could counteract the measures of the administration he had left behind.

Edmund Burke condemned the plan as “a solemn mockery of royalty” that proposed to strip the executive of all its essential powers while the King “was the object of compassion to the meanest of his subjects.” Sheridan declared that the whole of Pitt’s conduct was governed by party considerations and the impulse of personal ambition: had Pitt expected to retain his place, the limitations would never have been heard of.

Fox spoke in terms of indignation and abhorrence of a plan “most artfully framed on a perfect knowledge of the weakness of human nature, to introduce discord and endanger affection between persons most nearly connected by the ties of blood and in the bonds of duty.”

The Prince’s Response

Pitt had written to the Prince of Wales on 30 December 1788, informing his Royal Highness of the plan. The Prince’s reply, dated 2 January 1789, was firm, dignified, and temperate. He professed his deep regret at perceiving “a project for introducing weakness, disorder, and insecurity into every branch of political business — for separating the court from the state, and depriving government of its natural and accustomed support — a scheme for disconnecting the authority to command service from the power of animating it by reward.”

Nevertheless, declaring his conviction that the evils which might otherwise arise outweighed every other consideration, the Prince announced his determination to undertake “the painful trust imposed upon him by that melancholy necessity which of all the King’s subjects he deplored the most.”

The Committee of the Whole and the Protest

Despite the opposition’s rhetorical power, the resolutions passed the Commons by a large majority. In the Lords, the contest was equally obstinate. A protest was entered on the journals of the House on 25 January 1789, signed by the Dukes of York and Cumberland and fifty-five other peers, expressing their highest indignation at the restrictions “thus arbitrarily imposed.”

A committee of both houses then presented the resolutions in form to the Prince, who accepted them in somewhat indignant though not unguarded terms.

The Commission and the Bill

Lord Camden then moved — with a view, as he expressed it, to restore “the efficacy of legislation” — that the Lord Chancellor be directed, by authority of the two houses, to issue a commission in the name of the sovereign for opening the session. This finally passed, but the princes of the blood expressly refused to let their names appear on the commission. The session was opened in form by the Lords Commissioners on 3 February 1789.

The bill itself — founded on the propositions already agreed — passed the House of Commons on 12 February, with some amendments (the peerage clause was limited to three years). In the course of the succeeding week, the bill had advanced, after encountering much unavailing opposition, to the stage of commitment — amidst very just and general apprehension as to the consequences.

The King’s Recovery (February 1789)

Just as the Regency Bill was about to pass into law — and with Pitt’s ministry hours from likely dismissal — the crisis was resolved by the King’s recovery.

These extraordinary and unprecedented measures were at length “happily for the public arrested in their progress by an official information from the Lord Chancellor that the King was declared by his physicians to be in a state of convalescence.” An adjournment of all further proceedings in the bill immediately took place. For nearly three weeks, a total suspension of all parliamentary business ensued.

On 10 March 1789, it was declared that His Majesty, being perfectly recovered from his indisposition, had ordered a commission to be issued for holding the Parliament in the usual manner.

The National Rejoicing

The tidings of the King’s recovery diffused “the most general and heartfelt satisfaction.” A national thanksgiving was appointed. The King himself went in solemn procession to St. Paul’s Cathedral to offer up his grateful devotions — an event celebrated throughout the kingdom by splendid illuminations and all the other accustomed demonstrations of joy.

The contrast with the French monarchy, whose financial crisis was at that very moment spiralling into revolution, was not lost on contemporaries. Where Louis XVI was being dragged back to Paris by a revolutionary mob, George III was processing through the streets of London to the cheers of his people.

The Irish Parliament’s Role

The conduct of the Irish Parliament in the regency business formed a striking contrast to that of the English. On ascertaining the fact of the King’s incapacity, the Irish House of Commons on 11 February 1789 carried without a division an address to the Prince of Wales requesting him immediately to take upon himself the government of that kingdom as Regent. The Lords acceded.

The Lord-Lieutenant — the Marquis of Buckingham, formerly Lord Temple — refused to transmit this address to England, in what Belsham calls “a rash and hazardous exercise of discretion.” Commissioners were therefore appointed by both houses to present the address in person to the Prince. The rapid and unexpected recovery of the King happily superseded the object of their commission — but the Prince in his reply declared “that nothing could obliterate from his memory the sentiments of gratitude which he felt for their generous kindness.”

This divergence between the two kingdoms raised a dangerous constitutional question: whether Ireland, under the political compact subsisting between the two countries, could constitutionally separate itself from the executive government established in England. The Prince’s acceptance of the regency “under all the concomitant humiliating restrictions of Parliament fortunately precluded not only the investigation, but even the mention” of this perplexing question.

Political Consequences

The Survival of Pitt’s Ministry

The immediate consequence of the King’s recovery was the preservation of Pitt’s ministry. Had the Regency Bill passed and the Prince of Wales taken office as Regent with reduced powers, the new government would almost certainly have dismissed Pitt and his colleagues. But the King’s return to health meant that the Prince’s regency never took effect — and Pitt remained in power, strengthened by his victory.

Indeed, the crisis actually strengthened Pitt’s position. He had defended the authority of Parliament against the claim of indefeasible hereditary right. He had appeared as the champion of the popular element in the constitution against the pretensions of the heir apparent. The tide of popular opinion, Belsham records, “ran with no less violence at this crisis in favour of Mr. Pitt than at the commencement of his ministerial career.”

The Damage to Fox

Fox, by contrast, emerged from the crisis badly wounded. His assertion of the Prince’s inherent right to the Regency — however legally defensible — had been politically catastrophic. It allowed Pitt to paint the Whigs as defenders of divine right, a charge that was deeply damaging in an age still shaped by the constitutional settlement of 1688.

More durably, Fox’s alliance with the Prince of Wales — which had seemed so promising on the eve of the crisis — now became a liability. The King never forgot that his eldest son had been ready to take his throne while he lay incapacitated. The Prince’s association with Fox and the opposition Whigs was a wound that would never fully heal, poisoning the relationship between George III and the Prince of Wales for the remainder of their lives.

Fox’s role in the crisis also deepened the divisions within the Whig party. The more conservative Whigs — the Rockinghamites around the Duke of Portland — were uneasy with Fox’s intimacy with the Prince and with the recklessness of his constitutional doctrines. Edmund Burke, though he supported Fox during the crisis, was increasingly troubled by the direction of his party. The French Revolution, which erupted just months later, would complete the rupture.

The Constitutional Legacy

The Regency Crisis established an important constitutional precedent: that in the event of the sovereign’s incapacity, it was for Parliament — not the heir apparent — to determine who should exercise the royal authority, and with what limitations. This principle was followed in the Regency Acts of 1811 (when the Prince of Wales finally became Regent during the King’s permanent illness) and has governed all subsequent regency arrangements in the United Kingdom.

The crisis also demonstrated the fragility of the eighteenth-century constitution. The entire machinery of government could be paralysed by the illness of one man. The flexibility of the unwritten constitution — the fact that it could be adapted by parliamentary resolution to meet an unprecedented emergency — was both its greatest strength and its most alarming vulnerability.

The King and the Prince of Wales

The personal consequences of the crisis were enduring. George III never again trusted his eldest son. The Prince, for his part, never forgot the restrictions that Pitt had proposed to impose on him — restrictions that would have made him the most powerless Regent in British history. The relationship between father and son, already strained by the Prince’s debts, his secret marriage to Maria Fitzherbert (a Roman Catholic, and therefore in violation of the Royal Marriages Act), and his Whig sympathies, was now poisoned beyond repair.

When the King suffered a final, permanent breakdown in 1810 — after the death of his beloved youngest daughter, Princess Amelia — the Prince of Wales became Regent under the terms of the Regency Act of 1811. But by then, the political circumstances had changed. The King was no longer governing, the Whigs were no longer in alliance with the Prince, and the restrictions that Pitt had proposed in 1789 had become a historical curiosity rather than a practical blueprint. The regency of 1811–1820 — the period known as the Regency era — bore little resemblance to the regency that had been planned in the desperate winter of 1788–1789.

Key Figures

  • George III (1738–1820) — The King. Afflicted by what modern historians believe was porphyria, his first serious episode of mental illness created the constitutional crisis. His unexpected recovery in February 1789 saved Pitt’s ministry and preserved the political status quo.

  • William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) — Prime Minister. Faced the prospect of immediate dismissal if the Prince of Wales became Regent. His masterful handling of the crisis — asserting Parliament’s right to appoint and limit the Regent, and proposing severe restrictions on the Prince’s powers — preserved his ministry and strengthened his position.

  • Charles James Fox (1749–1806) — Leader of the Whig opposition. His assertion of the Prince’s inherent right to the Regency was a catastrophic political miscalculation that allowed Pitt to pose as the defender of parliamentary sovereignty. The crisis permanently linked Fox to the Prince of Wales in the King’s mind.

  • George, Prince of Wales (1762–1830) — The heir apparent. His close alliance with Fox and the opposition Whigs made him the natural alternative to Pitt. His dignified acceptance of Pitt’s restrictions under protest preserved his constitutional position, but his relationship with his father was permanently damaged.

  • Edmund Burke (1729–1797) — Whig MP and Fox’s ally. Participated in the Commons debates, denouncing the restrictions as a “solemn mockery of royalty.” His break with Fox over the French Revolution was still in the future, but the Regency crisis revealed growing tensions within the Whig party.

  • Richard Brinsley Sheridan (1751–1816) — Whig MP and orator. One of the sharpest critics of Pitt’s restrictions, arguing that the whole scheme was governed by party considerations and personal ambition. His eloquence in the crisis matched his performance in the Impeachment of Warren Hastings.

  • Lord Thurlow (1731–1806) — Lord Chancellor. Initially appeared to be preparing to join the Prince’s administration, but dramatically shifted course when signs of the King’s recovery emerged, declaring with theatrical piety: “When I forgot my king, may God forget me.”

  • Queen Charlotte (1744–1818) — The Queen. Pitt’s plan vested the care of the King’s person and the patronage of the royal household in her hands — a provision that made her, in effect, the political ally of the ministry against her own son.
