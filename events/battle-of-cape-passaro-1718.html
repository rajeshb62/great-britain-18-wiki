Battle of Cape Passaro (1718)

The Battle of Cape Passaro (11 August 1718) was a decisive naval engagement fought off the southeastern coast of Sicily, in which a British fleet under Sir George Byng destroyed the Spanish Mediterranean fleet under Don Antonio de Castaneta. The battle was the principal naval action of the War of the Quadruple Alliance (1718–1720) and constituted the most complete British naval victory since Barfleur in 1692. Byng captured or destroyed sixteen Spanish ships of war without losing a single vessel of his own, a feat that effectively extinguished Spanish naval power in the Mediterranean for a generation.

Background

The War of the Quadruple Alliance

The Treaty of Utrecht (1713), which ended the War of the Spanish Succession, had stripped Spain of its Italian possessions — Sicily was awarded to the Duchy of Savoy, Sardinia to the Holy Roman Empire under Emperor Charles VI, and Naples, Milan, and the Spanish Netherlands to Austria. The settlement was deeply resented at the Spanish court of Philip V, the first Bourbon king of Spain, who had been confirmed on the throne by the treaty but at the cost of Spain’s traditional Mediterranean empire (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 13500–13535).

The driving force behind Spain’s revanchist ambitions was Cardinal Giulio Alberoni, an Italian-born statesman of extraordinary energy and ambition who had risen from humble origins to become the effective prime minister of Spain. Alberoni’s grand design was to overturn the Utrecht settlement, recover the lost Italian territories, and restore Spain to the rank of a first-class European power. To this end, he set about rebuilding the Spanish navy — neglected and reduced to a shadow of its former strength by the War of the Spanish Succession — and raising a formidable army (Smollett, vol. X, lines 9340–9390).

In 1717, Alberoni judged the moment opportune. Emperor Charles VI was embroiled in the Austro-Turkish War (1716–1718) and could spare few troops for Italy; the new British king George I was preoccupied with the aftermath of the Jacobite Rising of 1715 and the consolidation of his Hanoverian dynasty; and France, under the Regency of the Duke of Orléans, was itself unstable. Spain struck.

Spanish Invasions of Sardinia and Sicily

In August 1717, a Spanish expeditionary force landed on Sardinia and swiftly overran the island, meeting only token Austrian resistance. The European powers watched with alarm but did not immediately intervene. Emboldened, Alberoni prepared an even larger expedition against Sicily, the strategic key to the central Mediterranean.

On 1 July 1718, a Spanish army under the Marquis de Lede landed near Palermo and rapidly conquered most of Sicily, laying siege to the citadel of Messina, which held out for the Emperor. The speed and scale of the Spanish offensive precipitated a diplomatic crisis. On 2 August 1718, the Treaty of the Quadruple Alliance was signed in London between Great Britain, France, the Holy Roman Empire, and the Dutch Republic. The alliance demanded Spain’s immediate withdrawal from Sardinia and Sicily and offered an exchange: Spain’s recognition of the Utrecht settlement in return for the eventual reversion of the Duchies of Parma, Piacenza, and Tuscany to Philip V’s son, Don Carlos (the future Charles III of Spain). Spain rejected the terms (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 13550–13600).

Before the ink was dry on the treaty, the British government had already acted. The Cabinet, dominated by the Whig ministry of Lord Stanhope and Charles Townshend, resolved to strike pre-emptively at the Spanish fleet. Stanhope’s instructions to the Admiralty were unequivocal: the first blow should, if possible, be decisive.

The Campaign

Byng’s Squadron

Sir George Byng, then aged fifty-five and one of the most experienced admirals in the Royal Navy, was appointed to command the Mediterranean squadron with orders to prevent Spanish aggression against the Emperor’s Italian possessions. Byng sailed from England in June 1718 with a fleet of twenty-two ships of the line, frigates, and fire-ships — a force of about 9,000 men and some 1,390 guns. His second-in-command was Vice-Admiral Sir John Norris (who remained in home waters), but Byng’s immediate subordinates included Rear-Admiral Sir George Delaval commanding the van and Captain George Walton as one of his most aggressive frigate captains.

Byng called at Gibraltar and Port Mahon (Minorca, captured in 1708 and now a British naval base) to take on provisions and intelligence, then sailed for the coast of Calabria. On arrival, he landed German auxiliaries at Reggio to reinforce the Imperial forces, and sent a flag of truce to the Marquis de Lede, proposing a two-month suspension of arms pending diplomatic resolution. De Lede replied that he had no authority to treat and would follow his orders, which were to take Sicily for the King of Spain (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15053–15065).

The Pursuit

Byng learned that the Spanish fleet had sailed from Malta to avoid an encounter. Determining to bring them to battle, he weighed anchor and stood through the Strait of Messina. On 10 August 1718, Byng’s lookouts sighted Spanish scouts in the Faro; by noon, the entire Spanish fleet was in view, drawn up in line of battle: twenty-seven sail of men-of-war, large and small, besides two fire-ships, four bomb-vessels, seven galleys, and numerous store-ships and transports — the fruit of Alberoni’s naval rebuilding programme.

The Spanish commander, Don Antonio de Castaneta, having the weather gauge and a clear numerical advantage in total vessels (though his largest ships carried fewer guns than Byng’s), chose not to engage directly. Instead, he stood away large but in good order, hoping either to draw Byng into a trap or to reach the shelter of the Sicilian coast. Byng pursued through that day and through the following night, with light north-easterly winds and occasional calms.

The Battle (11 August 1718)

The Action Begins

At dawn on 11 August, the British had closed the distance. Byng observed that the Marquis de Mari, commanding the Spanish rear-admiral’s division, had separated from the main body with six men-of-war, the galleys, fire-ships, bomb-vessels, and store-ships, standing in towards the Sicilian shore. Byng instantly detached Captain George Walton in the Canterbury with five ships to pursue this division, while he himself pressed on after the Spanish main body under Castaneta (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15107–15112).

Walton’s detachment closed rapidly. The Argyle fired a warning shot; when it was disregarded, a second shot; and when the Canterbury drew near, a third. The Spanish ship replied with her stern-chase guns, and the engagement became general.

The Main Engagement

Around ten o’clock, the leading British ships — the Orford, Captain Edward Falkingham, and the Grafton, Captain Nicholas Haddock — came up with the Spanish rear. Byng sent orders not to fire unless the Spaniards fired first, but the Spanish stern-chase guns removed any ambiguity. The Orford attacked the Santa Rosa (64 guns) and took her. The St. Carlos (60 guns) struck to the Kent, Captain Thomas Matthews. The Grafton engaged the Prince of Asturias (70 guns, the former British Cumberland, sold to Spain), carrying Rear-Admiral Chacon’s flag; Haddock mauled her severely and left her for the Breda and Captain to secure, pressing ahead after another Spanish ship of sixty guns (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15148–15164).

The hardest fighting centred on Castaneta’s flagship, the Real San Felipe (Royal St. Philip, 74 guns), a magnificent three-decker. The Kent and the Superbe, Captain Streynsham Master, engaged her from about one o’clock. She fought a running battle for two hours, giving broadside for broadside. The Superbe finally laid herself alongside the Spanish admiral, and Castaneta — his rigging shot away, his decks slippery with blood, two hundred men killed or wounded, and he himself wounded in the left leg and right heel by the same shot — struck his colours. As the Spanish historian recorded, he surrendered only after a cannonball had cut a man in two beside him, the pieces leaving him half-dead (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15498–15543).

Byng in the Barfleur (90 guns) had meanwhile tacked to pursue Rear-Admiral Guevara and another sixty-gun ship that had stood to windward, but they hauled out of reach as night fell. The Essex took the Juno (36 guns); the Montague and Rupert jointly captured the Volante (44 guns); and Rear-Admiral Delaval in the Dorsetshire took the Isabella (60 guns). The action was fought off Cape Passaro, the southeastern promontory of Sicily, about six leagues from the shore.

Walton’s Famous Dispatch

Captain Walton’s pursuit of Mari’s division proved equally devastating. He chased the Spanish ships onto the Sicilian coast, where Mari ran his vessels aground to save his men. On 16 August, Byng received a dispatch from Walton so laconic that it became one of the most celebrated documents in naval history:

“SIR, We have taken and destroyed all the Spanish ships and vessels which were upon the coast, the number as per margin. I am, &c. G. WALTON.” Canterbury, off Syracusa, Aug. 16, 1718.

The margin listed the prizes: four Spanish men-of-war captured (including Rear-Admiral Mari’s flagship of 60 guns, plus one of 54, one of 40, and one of 24) and four more burnt (one of 54, two of 40, and one of 30), together with a fire-ship and a bomb-vessel (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15304–15367). The dispatch became a byword for British phlegm in victory and was widely reprinted in the London press.

The Result

By nightfall, Byng had captured or destroyed sixteen Spanish vessels — including the flagship Real San Felipe, three rear-admirals’ flagships, and numerous other ships of the line — without losing a single British ship. British casualties were remarkably light; the ship that suffered most was the Grafton, which had pressed so far ahead that she had engaged several Spanish ships in succession. The Grafton’s captain, Nicholas Haddock, was later knighted for his valour.

The Spanish loss in men was severe: estimates range from 2,000 to 4,000 killed, wounded, or taken prisoner. The Spanish admirals Castaneta, Chacon, and Mari were all captured; Guevara alone escaped with a handful of ships into the night.

Significance

Destruction of Spanish Naval Power

Cape Passaro stands as one of the most complete fleet actions in naval history. Byng’s victory was not merely a tactical success but a strategic catastrophe for Spain. In a single day’s fighting, Alberoni’s four-year programme of naval reconstruction was undone. The Spanish navy, which had been built up to challenge British Mediterranean supremacy, was shattered, and Spain would not possess a battle fleet capable of contesting the Mediterranean for another generation. As Campbell observed, the principal point of the expedition was “the entire destruction of the Spanish maritime power” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15387–15389).

British Mediterranean Dominance

The victory confirmed Britain’s unchallenged control of the Mediterranean, a dominance founded on the capture of Gibraltar (1704) and Minorca (1708) and now enforced by the destruction of the only remaining rival fleet. The Royal Navy could henceforth operate in the interior sea as an instrument of British policy rather than as a combatant. Cape Passaro thus forms a direct link in the chain of British Mediterranean ascendancy that runs from Rooke through Byng to Hawke and Nelson.

Legal and Diplomatic Controversy

The battle was fought without a formal declaration of war — a circumstance that provoked heated debate in the British Parliament and throughout Europe. Spain accused Britain of acting as “guardians of the neutrality of Italy” while secretly resolved to destroy Spain’s maritime revival. The Spanish government published an account in Holland, circulated throughout Europe, alleging that the British had attacked without the notice required by the laws of nations, and that the action was indistinguishable from “piratical violence” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15369–15382).

Byng’s conduct was vindicated by the publication of his instructions and a letter from Lord Stanhope, then Secretary of State, dated from Bayonne on 2 September 1718, which declared: “The two great objects… are, to destroy their fleet, if possible, and to preserve such a footing in Sicily as may enable us to land an army there.” Stanhope had earlier written that “the first blow you give should, if possible, be decisive” (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 15122–15140). The pre-emptive strike doctrine thus received one of its earliest and most successful demonstrations.

Aftermath

Austrian Landings and the Siege of Messina

Having cleared the sea of the Spanish fleet, Byng turned his attention to supporting the Imperial land campaign. He sailed to Naples to concert measures with the Imperial viceroy, then proceeded to Sicily, where Austrian troops under General Mercy were landed under the protection of Byng’s guns. The Marquis de Lede’s army, now cut off from reinforcement and supply by sea, was forced to raise the siege of Messina and fall back on Palermo.

Byng also oversaw the execution of the Quadruple Alliance treaties in Sardinia, ensuring that Spanish troops were evacuated and that the Duke of Savoy was put in possession of his new kingdom. The Spanish king himself was later reported to have expressed satisfaction at Byng’s conduct to the British court, while the Duke of Savoy presented the admiral with his portrait set in diamonds — a measure of the respect Byng had earned even from his opponents.

The Fall of Alberoni and the Treaty of The Hague

The destruction of the Spanish fleet fatally undermined Cardinal Alberoni’s position at the Spanish court. The war on which he had staked everything was lost. Besieged in Palermo, his army isolated, his navy at the bottom of the Ionian Sea, Alberoni faced a coalition that now included France and the Empire in active military operations against Spain. A French army invaded the Basque Country in 1719, while British ships raided the Spanish coast at Vigo and Santander.

In December 1719, Philip V, yielding to allied pressure, ordered Alberoni to leave Spain within three weeks. The Cardinal’s fall was as sudden as his rise had been meteoric: he fled to Italy, where he survived the disgrace and lived on until 1752, but his grand design was in ruins. The peace was concluded at the Treaty of The Hague on 17 February 1720. Spain acceded to the Quadruple Alliance, evacuated Sardinia and Sicily, and renounced all claims to the former Spanish possessions in Italy. Sicily was transferred from Savoy to Austria, and the Duke of Savoy was compensated with the island of Sardinia — a territorial exchange that created the Kingdom of Sardinia, a state that would itself play a pivotal role in the unification of Italy a century and a half later.

Key Figures

  • George Byng, 1st Viscount Torrington (1663–1733) — The victor of Cape Passaro. A veteran of the Battle of Beachy Head (1690), the War of the Spanish Succession, and the suppression of the Jacobite Rising of 1715, Byng was by all contemporary accounts a commander of exceptional judgment, courage, and diplomatic skill. After Passaro, he was recalled to England by special command of George I, appointed Treasurer of the Navy, sworn to the Privy Council, and in 1721 created Viscount Torrington and Baron Byng of Southill, Bedfordshire. He served as First Lord of the Admiralty from 1727 to 1733. His son, Admiral John Byng, was executed in 1757 for failing to relieve Minorca — a tragic contrast to his father’s brilliant career.

  • Cardinal Giulio Alberoni (1664–1752) — The architect of Spain’s revival and the instigator of the war. An Italian of humble birth who rose to become Philip V’s chief minister, Alberoni rebuilt the Spanish navy and army, recovered Sardinia and most of Sicily, and came within sight of overturning the Utrecht settlement. His fall after Cape Passaro was total: dismissed, exiled, and his policies in ruins, though he later played a role in the election of Pope Innocent XIII.

  • Charles VI, Holy Roman Emperor (1685–1740) — The Habsburg emperor whose Italian possessions were the target of Spanish aggression. The War of the Quadruple Alliance secured Sicily and Sardinia for Austria, though Charles VI’s attention was divided between the Mediterranean and the ongoing war with the Ottoman Empire, which concluded with the Treaty of Passarowitz just weeks after Cape Passaro. Charles’s preoccupation with the Pragmatic Sanction (to secure the succession of his daughter Maria Theresa) would dominate the remainder of his reign.

  • Captain George Walton — Byng’s aggressive frigate captain, whose destruction of the Spanish rear-admiral’s division was recorded in one of the most famous dispatches in naval history. Walton’s terse report — “We have taken and destroyed all the Spanish ships and vessels which were upon the coast, the number as per margin” — became a byword for the understated professionalism of the Georgian navy.

  • Don Antonio de Castaneta — The Spanish admiral who commanded the Mediterranean fleet. Though wounded and forced to surrender after a gallant defence of the Real San Felipe, he conducted the retreat in good order before the British closed, and his flagship fought for hours against overwhelming odds before striking.

  • Marquis de Lede — Spanish general commanding the invasion of Sicily. After Byng’s victory isolated his army, he was forced to abandon the siege of Messina and was eventually compelled to evacuate Sicily under the terms of the Treaty of The Hague.
