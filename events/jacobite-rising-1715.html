Jacobite Rising of 1715

The Jacobite Rising of 1715 (also known as the Fifteen Rebellion) was an armed attempt by James Francis Edward Stuart (the Old Pretender) to recover the British throne for the Stuart dynasty. It broke out in September 1715, barely a year after the Hanoverian succession placed George I on the throne, and was the first major Jacobite rising of the eighteenth century. The rebellion was characterised by the Earl of Mar’s rapid raising of a large Highland army, the indecisive Battle of Sheriffmuir, the collapse of an English rising at Preston, and the Pretender’s belated and futile arrival in Scotland before fleeing to France in February 1716.

Background

Hanoverian Succession and Whig Ascendancy

The accession of George I upon the death of Queen Anne on 1 August 1714 was deeply unwelcome to the Jacobite party. The new king was a German Lutheran who spoke no English, had little interest in British affairs, and arrived determined to break the power of the Tory Party, which had dominated the last years of Anne’s reign. The Whigs, led by Robert Walpole, James Stanhope, and Charles Townshend, seized the opportunity to cement their ascendancy, impeaching the former Tory ministers Oxford and Bolingbroke for their role in the Treaty of Utrecht negotiations. Bolingbroke, fearing impeachment and execution, fled to France in March 1715 and entered the service of the Pretender, an act that both encouraged and embarrassed the Jacobite cause (Smollett, vol. X, lines 6773–6827).

The Jacobites had placed great hopes in Oxford and Bolingbroke, expecting that a Tory ministry would favour a Stuart restoration. Instead, George I’s first government was an exclusively Whig administration, and the Septennial Act was eventually passed to secure Whig dominance. The Pretender’s manifesto, issued from Lorraine, appealed to the disaffected and was circulated covertly in England, though it provoked a diplomatic incident with the Duke of Lorraine (Smollett, vol. X, lines 6780–6810).

The Earl of Mar’s ‘Hunting Party’

John Erskine, the 22nd Earl of Mar, had been a leading Scottish politician under Queen Anne and had initially paid court to George I after the succession. Snubbed by the new king and excluded from office, he turned to the Jacobite cause. Under the pretext of a hunting party, Mar assembled a large gathering of Highland chiefs at Braemar on 6 September 1715 (O.S. 27 August). There he proclaimed James Francis Edward Stuart as King James III and VIII, raised the Stuart standard at Castletown, and began recruiting an army (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1588–1619; Smollett, vol. X, lines 8380–8410).

The standard itself bore the ill-omened inscription “No Union,” a reference to Mar’s own role in negotiating the Union of 1707, which many Highlanders resented. Within weeks, Mar had assembled an army of some 10,000 men from the Highland clans — Camerons, MacDonalds, MacLeans, Frasers, and others — making the rising the most formidable Jacobite force ever gathered in Scotland.

Military Campaign

Operations in Scotland (September–October 1715)

Mar’s initial strategy was to secure the Scottish Lowlands and open a route into England. He occupied Perth without opposition and established his headquarters there. The government response was led by John Campbell, 2nd Duke of Argyll, the commander-in-chief in Scotland. Argyll, a veteran soldier and a Campbell — traditional enemy of many Jacobite clans — had only a small regular force of some 3,500–4,000 men, mostly raw recruits and a few regiments of dragoons recently arrived from Ireland. He established his base at Stirling, the key to controlling the Forth crossings.

In October, Mar made an abortive attempt to cross the Forth and was checked by Argyll’s forces. Meanwhile, Simon Fraser, Lord Lovat, who had professed Jacobite sympathies, opportunistically seized Inverness for the government in October, driving out Sir John Mackenzie and securing the northern communications for the Crown (Smollett, vol. X, lines 8574–8580; Belsham, vol. II, lines 1728–1732).

The English Rising and the Surrender at Preston

In England, the Jacobite rising was led by Thomas Forster (a Northumbrian MP and Protestant Jacobite) and James Radclyffe, 3rd Earl of Derwentwater. With a force of English Jacobites, they appeared at the head of a considerable armed force in Northumberland in October 1715, hoping to surprise Newcastle upon Tyne where they expected ample supplies of arms and stores. However, both churchmen and dissenters in Newcastle formed a town guard of 700 men and a corps of 700 keelmen (mostly dissenters) who embodied for the town’s defence, rendering the attack abortive (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1640–1655).

Foiled at Newcastle, Forster and Derwentwater altered their route northward to Kelso, where they were joined by a body of Highlanders under Viscount Kenmure. Reinforced, they re-entered England by the western border, proclaiming the Pretender at Warkworth, Penrith, Lancaster, and other towns as they marched south. On their arrival at Preston on 12 November 1715 (O.S. 1 November), they were attacked by government forces under generals Charles Wills and George Carpenter, who invested the town on all sides and compelled the rebels to surrender at discretion (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1656–1666; Smollett, vol. X, lines 8490–8510).

Battle of Sheriffmuir (13 November 1715)

On the very same day as the Preston surrender, the main Scottish armies clashed at Sheriffmuir, near Dunblane in Stirlingshire. Mar, learning of Argyll’s position, decided to force a crossing of the Forth to join his southern friends. He marched to Auchterarder and reviewed his army before advancing. Argyll, apprised of Mar’s intention, crossed the Forth at Stirling on 12 November and took position on the heights of Sheriffmuir, his left anchored at the village of Dunblane and his right stretching towards the muir (Smollett, vol. X, lines 8520–8532).

The Highland army, numbering about 9,000 effective men (horse and foot), greatly outnumbered Argyll’s force of 3,500. The clans that formed part of the centre and right wing of the Jacobite army, with Clanranald and Glengarry at their head, charged the left wing of the king’s army sword in hand with such impetuosity that in seven minutes both horse and foot were totally routed with great slaughter. General Whetham, who commanded the government left, fled at full gallop to Stirling declaring the royal army totally defeated (Smollett, vol. X, lines 8534–8541; Belsham, vol. II, lines 1676–1698).

However, on the other wing, the Duke of Argyll personally led the right, charging the enemy’s left with Stair’s Dragoons and Evans’s Dragoons, and drove them two miles before him as far as the Water of Allan. Though the Jacobite left attempted to rally ten times, Argyll pressed them hard. Meanwhile, the victorious right wing of the rebels, having pursued Whetham, returned to the field and formed in the rear of Brigadier Wightman’s three supporting battalions, now numbering some 5,000 men. Argyll, returning from his pursuit, joined Wightman who had faced about and taken possession of enclosures and mud-walls (Smollett, vol. X, lines 8542–8559; Belsham, vol. II, lines 1699–1709).

In this posture both armies fronted each other till evening, when Argyll drew off towards Dunblane and the rebels retired to Ardoch without mutual molestation. Next day, Argyll marched back to the field of battle, carried off his wounded with four pieces of cannon left by the enemy, and retreated to Stirling. Casualties were about 500 men on each side. Though tactically indecisive — each general claimed the victory — the battle was a strategic success for the government: Argyll, with an inferior force, had entirely disconcerted Mar’s plans and prevented him from crossing the Forth to link up with the English Jacobites. As Belsham records, “all the honour as well as the advantage of the fight rested with the duke of Argyle” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1715–1727; Campbell, vol. IV, lines 13960–13963).

Arrival of the Old Pretender

Despite the reverses at Sheriffmuir and Preston, the Pretender resolved to land in Scotland, arriving at Peterhead on 26 December 1715 (O.S. 15 December). He immediately issued proclamations summoning a convention of the estates, ordering all fencible men to repair to his standard, and fixing a day for his coronation. His reception was lukewarm: even his own supporters were dismayed by his lack of martial spirit and his rigid Catholic piety. In council, when presented with the draft of a declaration to be circulated in England, he objected to passages promising to secure the established churches of England and Scotland, declaring he could not in conscience make such promises (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1775–1787). Lord Bolingbroke, who had joined the Pretender’s service, was so alarmed by the king’s religious obstinacy that he refused to countersign the declaration.

When intelligence arrived of Argyll’s advancing army, the Pretender’s resolution collapsed. “For me,” he told his council, “it will be no new thing if I am unfortunate: my whole life, even from my cradle, has shown a constant series of misfortunes” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1740–1746).

Suppression by the Duke of Argyll and Flight to France

Argyll, now heavily reinforced, advanced towards Perth. The Pretender, accompanied by the Earl of Mar and the Earl of Melfort, embarked on board a French snow lying in the harbour of Montrose on 4 February 1716 (O.S. 23 January 1716). The vessel stretched over to Norway to avoid pursuit, then coasted along the shores of Germany and Holland, arriving in five days at Gravelines in France. This escape was narrowly managed — English men-of-war were in the vicinity but the French snow kept so close alongshore that pursuit proved impossible (Campbell, vol. IV, lines 13965–13971; Belsham, vol. II, lines 1749–1760).

The rebel army, left without leadership, retreated northward and quietly dispersed without making any further effort or receiving molestation from the government forces (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1760–1762).

Aftermath

Trials and Executions

The government’s response to the rebellion combined swift military suppression with a campaign of exemplary punishment. The lords Derwentwater, Nithsdale, and Nairne, with divers other noblemen, were tried in Westminster Hall for high treason, with Earl Cowper presiding as Lord High Steward. Despite earnest intercession from their families — the Countesses of Derwentwater and Nithsdale and Lady Nairne threw themselves at the king’s feet, pleading for mercy — George I remained implacable (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1865–1878).

A motion in the House of Lords, supported by the Earl of Nottingham, to address the throne requesting a reprieve for such of the condemned lords as should deserve mercy, was carried by a majority of only five voices. But George I replied haughtily that “on this and all other occasions he would do what he thought most consistent with the dignity of the crown and the safety of his people.” Nottingham was so far incurring the displeasure of the court that he was within a few days removed from his office as President of the Council (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1880–1898).

Earl of Derwentwater was executed on Tower Hill on 24 February 1716. A young nobleman of considerable virtue and accomplishment, he had been persuaded, though with difficulty, to plead guilty in the hope of obtaining a pardon. In his last declaration, he acknowledged the disingenuousness of this expedient but professed that, had he been granted life, he would never have taken up arms again against the king (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1899–1911).

Viscount Kenmure was also executed.

Earl of Nithsdale famously escaped from the Tower of London on the eve of his scheduled execution, aided by his wife. Lady Nithsdale, with the assistance of a female servant and another woman, smuggled him out of his cell disguised in women’s clothing — a feat that became one of the most celebrated prison breaks of the century.

Many of the lower classes also suffered execution. One Paul, a clergyman and vicar of Orton-upon-the-Hill in Leicestershire, who had joined the rebels at Preston, was tried and convicted of high treason and suffered the utmost rigour of the law with undaunted resolution. On the Sunday before his departure he preached a sermon at his own parish church from Ezekiel xxi. 26–27: “Thus saith the Lord God: Remove the diadem, and take off the crown. Exalt him that is low, and abase him that is high” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1916–1925).

Political Fallout

Lord Bolingbroke, reflecting on the rebellion’s failure, observed that “if milder measures had been pursued, the tories would never have universally embraced Jacobitism; the violence of the whigs forced them into the arms of the Pretender, and dyed the royal ermines in blood” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1931–1936).

The Disarming Act (1716)

In the wake of the rising, Parliament passed the Disarming Act 1716 (1 Geo. I, st. 2, c. 54), which sought to pacify the Scottish Highlands by prohibiting the possession of weapons by unauthorised persons and by disarming the clans that had participated in the rebellion. The Act authorised government forces to search for and seize arms and punished non-compliance with severe penalties. Though unevenly enforced — many Highland clans hid their weapons or were protected by sympathetic local officials — it marked the beginning of a sustained government campaign to break the military power of the clan system.

Legacy

The failure of the Fifteen demonstrated that a Jacobite restoration would require substantial foreign assistance. The rising’s deficiencies were laid bare: Mar’s cautious generalship, the Pretender’s personal inadequacy, the lack of coordination between northern and southern forces, and the impossibility of defeating regular troops with Highland charges alone. Bolingbroke’s damning verdict on the Pretender — “his religion is not founded on the love of virtue… the spring of his whole conduct is fear; he has all the superstition of a capuchin” (Belsham, vol. II, lines 1800–1811) — underscored the fatal incompatibility between Stuart Catholicism and British Protestant sensibility.

Yet the rebellion also revealed the stability of the new Hanoverian regime: the rising was suppressed within five months despite widespread disaffection, and the institutions of the state — Parliament, the army, the navy under Sir George Byng, and the financial system — held firm under Robert Walpole’s steady management. The Fifteen was a prelude to the more serious Forty-Five, but its failure confirmed that the Stuarts could not reclaim their throne without a fundamental shift in British politics or a major European war.

Key Figures

Jacobite Command

  • John Erskine, Earl of Mar — The ‘Bobbing John’ of Scottish politics, a former secretary of state for Scotland who turned Jacobite after being spurned by George I. Raised the largest Highland army of any Jacobite rising but proved a hesitant and ineffective general. After the rising, he fled to France with the Pretender and died in exile.
  • James Francis Edward Stuart (the Old Pretender) — Son of James II, recognised by Louis XIV as James III. Landed in Scotland too late and too half-heartedly to revive the rebellion; his rigid Catholicism and personal indecisiveness fatally undermined the cause. Bolingbroke described him as having “all the superstition of a capuchin” and “the native narrowness of his understanding.”
  • Thomas Forster — Northumbrian MP and Protestant Jacobite who led the English rising. Captured at Preston.
  • James Radclyffe, 3rd Earl of Derwentwater — Young, personable, and wealthy English Catholic nobleman who raised men in Northumberland and was executed for high treason. His execution provoked widespread sympathy.
  • William Gordon, Viscount Kenmure — Scottish Jacobite peer who joined Forster at Kelso and was captured at Preston. Executed.

Government Command

  • John Campbell, 2nd Duke of Argyll — Commander-in-chief in Scotland. With a greatly inferior force, he fought the indecisive Battle of Sheriffmuir but strategically prevented Mar from crossing the Forth. His leadership, local influence, and the loyalty of the Campbell interest were decisive in suppressing the rising.
  • General Henry Whetham — Commanded the left wing at Sheriffmuir; his wing was routed in seven minutes by the Highland charge, and he fled to Stirling declaring the army destroyed.
  • General William Cadogan — Served as Argyll’s second-in-command; later commanded the pursuit of the retreating Jacobites.
  • General George Wade — A rising military figure during the Fifteen; Wade’s later road-building programme in the Highlands (the Wade roads) was a direct consequence of the 1715 rebellion’s demonstration that the government lacked the means to move troops rapidly into the interior of Scotland.
  • General George Carpenter — Commanded at Preston, investing the town and compelling the surrender of the English Jacobites.
  • Simon Fraser, Lord Lovat — Former Jacobite who opportunistically seized Inverness for the government in October 1715, securing northern communications. His later Jacobite career in the Forty-Five ended on the block.
  • Sir John Norris — Admiral who commanded naval forces in Scottish waters during the rising; kept watch for French intervention.
