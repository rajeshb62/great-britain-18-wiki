Glorious First of June / Battle of Ushant (1 June 1794)

The Glorious First of June — known in French as the Bataille du 13 prairial an II — was the first major naval engagement of the French Revolutionary Wars and the largest fleet action fought in the Atlantic since Hawke’s triumph at Quiberon Bay thirty-five years earlier. Fought some four hundred miles west of the French coast, it pitted the British Channel Fleet under Admiral Lord Howe against the French Atlantic Fleet under Rear-Admiral Louis Thomas Villaret-Joyeuse. In a single day of ferocious close-range combat, Howe captured or destroyed seven French ships of the line, inflicted over three thousand casualties, and gave Britain its first great naval victory of the war. Yet the strategic prize — a vast French grain convoy from America — escaped untouched, leaving the battle’s significance disputed between tactical glory and strategic ambiguity.

Background

The War and the Grain Convoy

By the spring of 1794, the War of the First Coalition had been raging for sixteen months. On land, the coalition had fared disastrously: the Duke of York’s Flanders campaign was unravelling, the French Republic had mobilised the largest army Europe had ever seen through the levée en masse, and the Reign of Terror under the Committee of Public Safety was driving the revolutionary war effort with fanatical intensity. The one area in which Britain held unquestioned supremacy was at sea.

The immediate strategic crisis that produced the Glorious First of June was one of logistics, not territory. Revolutionary France, having mobilised its entire male population for war, had stripped its countryside of labour and suffered a catastrophic harvest. The country was starving. Its salvation lay across the Atlantic: a vast convoy of over 130 merchant ships, laden with grain, flour, naval stores, coffee, sugar, and other colonial produce valued at some £5 million (an immense sum for the period), had been assembled in Chesapeake Bay and was sailing for France. If it reached Brest safely, it would feed the army, relieve the famine, and replenish the treasury. If it were intercepted, the French Republic might collapse.

Pitt’s government understood the stakes perfectly. The Admiralty ordered the Channel Fleet — the largest and best-equipped force the Royal Navy could assemble — to put to sea, intercept the convoy, and cripple French naval power in a single decisive stroke.

The Commanders

Admiral Lord Richard Howe (1726–1799), then in his sixty-eighth year, was the most respected commander in the British service. Known to the seamen as “Black Dick” for his swarthy complexion and saturnine expression, Howe was a tactical innovator of the first rank. He had served with distinction under Edward Hawke at Quiberon Bay (1759), commanded in North America during the American Revolutionary War, and performed the most masterly convoy operation of the century at the relief of Gibraltar in 1782. He was, in the words of his biographer John Campbell, “an officer long held in the first degree of respect and esteem in the British navy, and personally beloved by all that had served under him.”

Rear-Admiral Louis Thomas Villaret-Joyeuse (1748–1812) was a very different figure. A former naval officer who had served in the American Revolutionary War, he had been a member of the Breton Parlement and had been elected to the National Convention as a deputy. He was a republican and a patriot, but he was also a political appointee — the Committee of Public Safety had ordered him to sea with explicit instructions: protect the grain convoy at all costs; do not risk the fleet in a general engagement unless necessary.

The Campaign: The Hunt (28–31 May)

Howe sailed from Spithead on 2 May 1794 with a fleet of thirty-four ships of the line, organised as follows: the main body under his own command (twenty-five sail), a detached squadron under Rear-Admiral Montagu (five ships, stationed to intercept the convoy on the Western Approaches), and a reconnaissance force under Captain Rainier.

The French Atlantic fleet under Villaret-Joyeuse — twenty-six ships of the line — had sailed from Brest on 16 May, after the Committee of Public Safety had reluctantly authorised him to put to sea. His orders were explicit: rendezvous with the convoy, escort it into Brest, and avoid a decisive battle if possible. To the south, the convoy itself — 130 merchantmen under the protection of a small squadron commanded by Contre-Amiral Vanstabel — was approaching the French coast.

For two weeks, the two great fleets groped for each other in fog and heavy weather. On 28 May, lookouts on both sides sighted the enemy. Howe immediately gave chase. The French, adhering to Villaret-Joyeuse’s orders to avoid a decisive action, formed a line of battle and sailed westward, drawing the British away from the convoy’s likely track. It was a calculated sacrifice: Villaret-Joyeuse was willing to risk the destruction of his fleet if it meant the grain convoy could slip past unmolested.

On 29 May, Howe caught the French rear and a sharp action followed. The British van, under Rear-Admiral Sir Alexander Hood in the Royal George, engaged the French rear division commanded by Contre-Amiral Nielly. Howe attempted to break the French line but was only partially successful; the French maintained their formation and continued westward through the night.

On 30 and 31 May, the fleet manoeuvred in thick fog, each side preparing for the decisive encounter. By the evening of 31 May, Howe had the wind gauge — the weather-gage — and was closing rapidly. Villaret-Joyeuse, seeing that escape was impossible, formed his line of battle and prepared to fight.

Howe’s Tactical Plan

Howe’s plan for the battle was the most aggressive tactical innovation attempted by a British admiral since Hawke’s pursuit into Quiberon Bay. In the standard line-of-battle tactics of the period, fleets fought parallel to one another, exchanging broadsides at moderate range, and the battle was decided by gunnery and endurance. Howe rejected this entirely.

His design was to break through the enemy’s line at every point — each British ship was to steer directly for its opposite number in the French line, pass through the gaps between them, and then engage from leeward, cutting off the French from their line of retreat to Brest. This was far more aggressive than conventional tactics, for it meant that Howe’s ships would be raked by French broadsides as they approached, would have to accept whatever damage came before they could reply, and would then find themselves on the leeward side — the disadvantageous position — once they had broken through.

But the psychological effect was intended to be devastating. A fleet whose line had been pierced, whose ships were engaged from both sides, and whose line of retreat had been cut, would lose all cohesion. Howe was aiming not merely to defeat the French fleet but to annihilate it.

The danger of this plan was that it depended entirely on the skill and resolution of his captains. If a captain failed to push through, or hung back, the whole scheme would collapse, and Howe’s ships would find themselves isolated and outnumbered. Howe issued his signals with the confidence of a commander who knew every officer under his flag: “Each ship will take the nearest opportunity to break through the enemy’s line, and then engage the ships opposed to her on the lee side.”

The Battle (1 June 1794)

The Approach

Dawn of 1 June 1794 broke clear and fair over a grey Atlantic, the fog having lifted to reveal the two fleets drawn up in order of battle. The French line extended for perhaps six miles, stretching north to south, with Villaret-Joyeuse’s flagship La Montagne (120 guns) in the centre. The British line lay to windward, Howe in the Queen Charlotte (100 guns) at its head.

At about 8:00 a.m., Howe made the signal for general chase, and then, as the fleets closed to within a mile, the signal to break the line. The British ships bore down in a long, irregular line, each captain steering for his appointed mark in the enemy’s formation. The French opened fire as the British approached, and the sea became a chaos of shot, smoke, and shattered timber.

The Breakthrough

Howe in the Queen Charlotte led the way. He steered directly for the gap between the fifth and sixth ships of the French line — the Montagne and the Républicain, the two largest ships in the French fleet. Passing through at pistol-shot range, the Queen Charlotte poured a devastating broadside into the Montagne as she passed, then emerged on the lee side and engaged the French flagship at close quarters.

Throughout the line, the same scene was repeated. The Brunswick (74 guns), commanded by Captain John Harvey, became entangled with the French Vengeur du Peuple (74 guns) and the Achille. For over three hours, the Brunswick fought a triple-handed duel in which Harvey was mortally wounded — a cannonball carried off his leg — and the Brunswick was reduced to a wreck, but never struck.

The Invincible (98 guns), under Captain Thomas Pakenham, broke through the French line and engaged the Juste at a range so close that the muzzles of the guns almost touched. Pakenham, a commander of the old school, is said to have removed his coat, called for a bowl of punch, and directed the action from his quarterdeck with a sang-froid that became legendary. “We’ll give them a good drubbing, lads!” was his reported rallying cry.

Sir John Jervis, in the Boyne (98 guns), broke through in the centre with characteristic precision and disciplined his fire with that cold efficiency that would later make him famous at Cape St Vincent. The Boyne’s gunners were drilled to fire on the down-roll, striking the French hulls at the waterline.

The Crisis

By noon, the battle had broken down into a series of individual ship-duels, each fought with extraordinary ferocity. The French republican crews, inspired by the cry of “Vive la République!”, fought with a tenacity that surprised the British — these were not the demoralised royalist crews of the old regime. The Vengeur du Peuple, surrounded by British ships and sinking from damage below the waterline, continued to fire until the sea reached her gunports. Captain Lacoudrais and half his crew went down with her, the tricolour still flying. The sinking of the Vengeur became a set-piece of French revolutionary propaganda — dramatised by the artist Pierre Ozanne and celebrated in verse as the supreme example of republican heroism.

The Sans Pareil (80 guns), the Juste (84 guns), the America (74 guns), the Achille (74 guns), the Impétueux (74 guns), and the Northumberland (74 guns) all struck their colours at various points during the afternoon. The Républicain escaped only through the desperate exertions of her crew and the fact that she was too damaged to pursue.

Howe’s Conduct

Howe himself, early in the action, was wounded — a splinter struck the calf of his leg — but he remained on the quarterdeck of the Queen Charlotte throughout, directing the battle with that silent, imperturbable calm that had earned him the devotion of his men. At one point, his flag-captain, Sir Andrew Douglas, was standing close to him when a round shot carried off a sailor at the admiral’s side. Howe, according to Campbell, showed no more emotion than if the man had fallen from fatigue. “He was not a man to waste words or feeling in the heat of action.”

The Result

By 4:00 p.m., the fighting was over. The French fleet was shattered: seven of its finest ships of the line — six captured and the Vengeur du Peuple sunk — lay in British hands. The remainder, including Villaret-Joyeuse’s battered Montagne, were in retreat to the east, making for Brest. The British fleet had suffered severe damage — most ships were dismasted or badly holed — but had lost not a single vessel.

The British dead included two post-captains — John Harvey of the Brunswick and John Hutt of the Queen — as well as numerous lieutenants and warrant officers. The French lost three flag officers and a large proportion of their commissioned ranks, many of whom were killed by the concentration of fire that followed Howe’s breakthrough.

The Grain Convoy

But the grain convoy — the object of the entire campaign — had escaped. While Howe had been pursuing and fighting Villaret-Joyeuse’s fleet, the convoy under Vanstabel had slipped past Montagu’s detached squadron in the fog and arrived safely in Brest on 3 June. The French Republic had its bread. The Committee of Public Safety could feed the army, and the Revolution would survive.

Howe had won a tactical victory of the first magnitude, but he had lost the strategic campaign.

Aftermath

”Lord Howe’s Day” in London

When news of the victory reached London, the capital erupted in celebration. The London Gazette published Howe’s dispatch — a model of understated professional brevity — and the King expressed his satisfaction in terms of warm personal gratitude. Parliament voted its unanimous thanks to Howe and his officers. Lloyd’s of London opened a subscription for the widows and orphans of the slain, which raised an enormous sum within days.

Howe was awarded the Order of the Garter — the highest honour in the King’s gift — and was presented with a magnificent service of plate by the City of London. The captured French colours were paraded through the streets and laid at the foot of the throne in St Paul’s Cathedral at a service of national thanksgiving. The day of the battle — 1 June — would be commemorated for years afterwards as “Lord Howe’s Day,” a patriotic anniversary second only to Trafalgar in the national calendar.

The Strategic Controversy

But even as the celebrations unfolded, a sober question was being asked in the Admiralty, in Parliament, and among the naval profession: had Howe actually won?

The grain convoy had arrived. France would not starve. The French fleet, though badly battered, had not been destroyed — most of Villaret-Joyeuse’s ships got back to Brest, where the dockyards repaired them within weeks. And Howe’s choice to pursue the French fleet rather than the convoy — the very aggressiveness that made the victory so glorious — was criticised by those who argued that his first duty had been to intercept the grain.

The controversy reflected a deeper tension in British naval strategy. The traditional purpose of the fleet in wartime was the destruction of the enemy’s naval forces — the guerre d’escadre — a doctrine that Hawke had perfected at Quiberon Bay. But the strategic purpose of the fleet in the French Revolutionary Wars was increasingly understood as the protection of British commerce and the destruction of French trade, the guerre de course. Had Howe’s victory been a spectacular irrelevance?

John Campbell, writing in his Lives of the Admirals, defends Howe on two grounds. First, the French fleet had to be defeated before the convoy could be safely intercepted — a fleet in being could always have intervened to protect the grain. Second, the moral effect of the victory was incalculable. “It taught the French nation,” Campbell writes, “that their admirals could not, with impunity, put to sea in the face of the British navy; and it demonstrated to the world that the naval superiority of Great Britain, which had been so glorious in the last war, had not been impaired or lost.”

Belsham, writing from a Whig perspective that was sceptical of the war’s conduct, is more critical. He notes that the government’s financial and strategic position remained as precarious after the victory as before it — the war was not shortened by a day, and the cost of equipping the Channel Fleet for the campaign consumed resources that might have been better spent on the land war.

The French Perspective

In France, the battle was presented as a strategic victory despite the tactical defeat. The Convention decreed that the grain convoy had been saved; that the French Republic had demonstrated its maritime capacity to challenge the British; and that the crew of the Vengeur du Peuple, who had gone down firing rather than strike their colours, had shown the world the indomitable spirit of republican seamen. Villaret-Joyeuse, though he had lost a third of his fleet, was not disgraced — he had carried out his orders.

The Wider Naval War

The Glorious First of June did not decide the naval war, but it established a pattern that would persist for the next decade. The Royal Navy had proved that it could seek out and defeat the French fleet in a pitched battle. The French had proved that they could accept the loss of a battle if the strategic objective — the safe arrival of a convoy — was achieved. The Channel would remain contested, but the psychological ascendancy now lay with the British.

Significance

The Glorious First of June marked a turning point in three distinct senses:

First, it established that the British naval supremacy of the 18th century — won at Quiberon Bay and Lagos in 1759 — had survived the disruptions of the American Revolutionary War and the financial strains of the peace. The Channel Fleet, under Howe’s command, was the most powerful single concentration of naval force the world had yet seen. The French Republic could not match it in a straight fight.

Second, it inaugurated the style of warfare that would characterise the next twenty years: the British seeking the decisive fleet action, the French seeking to avoid it while their commerce raiders and convoys slipped through the gaps. This was the pattern that a young captain named Horatio Nelson — then serving in the Mediterranean — would study and eventually perfect, culminating at the Battle of the Nile (1798), Copenhagen (1801), and Trafalgar (1805).

Third, the battle demonstrated that the old “formalist” style of naval tactics — parallel lines, long cannonades, gentlemanly battles — was giving way to something far more aggressive. Howe’s breakthrough was a direct precursor to Nelson’s famous “Nelson touch” — the division of the fleet and the concentration of force on a part of the enemy’s line. Howe showed that the line could be broken; Nelson would show that it could be destroyed.

Key Figures

  • Richard Howe, 1st Earl Howe (1726–1799) — “Black Dick,” the British commander. At 68, the oldest active flag officer in the service. His tactical plan to break the French line and engage from leeward was the most aggressive fleet action since Quiberon Bay. Rewarded with the Garter, but his reputation remains tied to the strategic ambiguity of the victory.

  • Admiral Louis Thomas Villaret-Joyeuse (1748–1812) — The French commander. A political appointee of the Convention, he carried out his orders with skill and tenacity: he drew Howe away from the convoy, sacrificed no more ships than necessary, and preserved the fleet as a fighting force. His conduct was, in its way, as creditable as Howe’s — though posterity has not remembered him.

  • Captain Thomas Pakenham (1757–1836) — Commander of HMS Invincible. One of the most colourful captains in the fleet, known for his bravery and his eccentricity. His sang-froid during the battle — removing his coat, calling for punch, and fighting his ship through the French line — became a staple of naval anecdote.

  • Sir John Jervis (1735–1823) — Captain of HMS Boyne. Already a distinguished officer, he would three years later win the Battle of Cape St Vincent (1797) and serve as reforming First Lord of the Admiralty. The Glorious First of June was his apprenticeship in fleet command.

  • Captain John Harvey (1740–1794) — Commander of HMS Brunswick. Mortally wounded in the action, he became one of the iconic British deaths of the battle. His ship, locked in combat with the Vengeur du Peuple, was reduced to a wreck but never surrendered. He died of his wounds on 30 June.

  • William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) — The British prime minister, who received news of the victory with satisfaction but recognised that the strategic problem — France’s survival and the war’s immense cost — had not been solved.
