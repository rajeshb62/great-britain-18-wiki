Capture of Quebec / Battle of the Plains of Abraham (1759)

The Capture of Quebec (June–September 1759) was the decisive campaign of the Seven Years’ War in North America and the most celebrated feat of British arms in the eighteenth century. A combined amphibious expedition under Major-General James Wolfe and Admiral Charles Saunders ascended the St. Lawrence, laid siege to the fortress-capital of New France, and — after months of stalemate and a repulse that verged on disaster — scaled the supposedly impassable cliffs above the river by night to bring the French to battle on the Plains of Abraham on 13 September 1759. The battle lasted barely fifteen minutes. Both Wolfe and the French commander, the Marquis Louis-Joseph de Montcalm, were mortally wounded. Quebec capitulated five days later. The victory, together with Edward Hawke’s triumph at Quiberon Bay, made 1759 the annus mirabilis of the British Empire and set the stage for the complete conquest of Canada the following year.

Background

Pitt’s Grand Strategy

The campaign against Quebec was the centrepiece of William Pitt the Elder’s grand design for the conquest of Canada. Pitt, who had returned to power in June 1757 at the head of a coalition with the Duke of Newcastle, brought to the war a strategic vision unlike anything his predecessors had conceived. Where earlier British ministries had pursued a defensive, maritime war against France, Pitt saw that the key to victory lay in the coordinated application of naval power and amphibious assault — what the historian William Belsham called “a magnificent and daring project” that “would doubtless have appeared romantic and impracticable to his predecessors.”1

The plan for 1759 envisioned three converging offensives:2

  1. The Quebec expedition, the most difficult and dangerous branch of the plan, was entrusted to the thirty-two-year-old Major-General James Wolfe, who had distinguished himself at the Capture of Louisbourg in 1758. He was to ascend the St. Lawrence with a strong naval squadron and a body of land forces, and undertake the siege of Quebec, the capital of French America.2

  2. General Jeffrey Amherst, commanding the principal army of regulars and provincials amounting to some twelve thousand men, was ordered to reduce the forts of Ticonderoga and Crown Point, cross Lake Champlain, and march along the Richelieu River to effect a junction with Wolfe under the walls of Quebec.3

  3. Brigadier-General John Prideaux, with a separate corps reinforced by Indians of the Five Nations assembled under Sir William Johnson, was to invest the French fortress of Niagara, which commanded the navigation of the Great Lakes and the communication of the St. Lawrence with the Mississippi. After the reduction of Niagara, the forces were to embark on Lake Ontario, descend the St. Lawrence, and undertake the siege of Montreal before joining the grand army before Quebec.4

Pitt also appointed the veteran Admiral Edward Boscawen to command in the Mediterranean — his action at Lagos in August would destroy a French squadron — and Admiral Edward Hawke to command the Channel Fleet, whose victory at Quiberon Bay in November would annihilate the French Atlantic fleet and secure British naval supremacy for the remainder of the war. The concentration of naval effort was unprecedented. Never before had Britain committed so many ships and men to a single year’s operations.

John Campbell, the naval historian, observed that the scheme, “though too refined and complicated to be put in execution in all its parts,” was nevertheless “sufficient to perform far more than had been expected, and to overcome obstacles of art and nature that, at first sight, appeared insurmountable.”5

The Armament

The fleet that assembled at Louisbourg in the spring of 1759 under the command of Vice-Admiral Charles Saunders was the largest that had ever been collected for operations on the North American station. Saunders, who had served as second-in-command to Boscawen at Louisbourg the previous year, commanded a squadron of twenty-two ships of the line, including some of the most powerful vessels in the Royal Navy. The Neptune, the Royal William, and the Princess Amelia each carried ninety or eighty guns; the Northumberland, the Vanguard, the Terrible, and the Trident mounted seventy-four guns each; and a further dozen ships of sixty or sixty-four guns completed the line of battle. Beyond the capital ships, the fleet included numerous frigates, bomb vessels, sloops of war, and transports — some 150 sail in all, carrying approximately 8,500 troops of the regular army and 13,500 seamen and marines.6

The army under James Wolfe consisted of three brigades commanded by Brigadier-General Robert Monckton, Brigadier-General George Townshend (son of the late Lord Townshend), and Brigadier-General James Murray. The regiments included the 15th Foot (Amherst’s), the 28th Foot (Bragg’s), the 35th Foot (Otway’s), the 43rd Foot (Kennedy’s), the 47th Foot (Lascelles’), the 48th Foot (Dupre’s), the 58th Foot (Anstruther’s), and the 60th Foot (the Royal Americans), together with detachments of grenadiers, light infantry, and the Louisbourg Grenadiers, a composite battalion formed from the grenadier companies of regiments that had served at the siege of Louisbourg.

The fleet weighed anchor from Louisbourg on 4 June 1759, but the harbour being still blocked with ice, they bore away for Halifax, where final preparations were made. From Halifax, Saunders detached Rear-Admiral Philip Durell with a small squadron to the Île de Coudres in the St. Lawrence, in the hope of intercepting a fleet of French transports and victuallers destined for Quebec. Durell took two store-ships, but seventeen sail of French transports had already reached Quebec before he could take his station.7

The Navigator’s Feat

One of the least celebrated but most essential elements of the expedition was the navigation of the St. Lawrence River itself. The river, already difficult in its lower reaches, was notoriously treacherous above the confluence of the Saguenay, where the channel narrowed, the tides ran strong, and the shoals and islands multiplied confusion. French pilots had assured the governor of Quebec that the river could not be ascended by large ships — that the British would run aground on the traverse below the Île d’Orléans, where the channel was narrow and the current ran at six knots.

The British had, however, captured French vessels carrying excellent charts of the river, and Saunders placed his trust in Captain James Cook, the master of HMS Pembroke, who had made a meticulous survey of the St. Lawrence approaches during the Louisbourg campaign and who now piloted the fleet through the traverse without the loss of a single ship. Campbell records that Saunders, “having discovered some excellent charts of the river St. Lawrence, in vessels taken from the enemy, he experienced none of those difficulties with which the navigation of this immense stream is said to be attended.”8

The armament arrived at the Île d’Orléans, a few leagues below Quebec, on 26 June 1759.

The Siege

The Landing and the Positions

The island of Orleans extends for some twenty miles in the St. Lawrence directly opposite Quebec, dividing the river into two channels. Its westernmost point advances to a high promontory on the south shore called Pointe-Lévis. Both these positions were occupied by the French at the outset of the campaign, but not in sufficient force to resist the British landing.

Wolfe’s first operation was to secure both posts. On the night of 29 June, he detached Brigadier Monckton with four battalions across the south channel to Pointe-Lévis. Monckton drove the French irregulars from the promontory and possessed himself of the position after a brief skirmish. Colonel Guy Carleton — the future Lord Dorchester, who would later defend Quebec against the American invasion — marched with a detachment to the westernmost point of the island of Orleans, from which the British operations would proceed.9

The possession of these two points was absolutely necessary, as Wolfe explained to Pitt. From Île d’Orléans, the British could observe the entire French position; from Pointe-Lévis, batteries of cannon and mortars could bombard Quebec across the river. Without command of these posts, “the enemy might make it impossible for any ship to lie in the bason of Quebec, or even within two miles of it.”10

The city of Quebec now appeared full in view — “at once a tempting and discouraging sight,” Campbell wrote. “No place is more favoured by nature, and there is none of which nature seems more to have consulted the defence.”11 The Upper Town stood atop a lofty rock that ran with a bold and steep front along the western banks of the St. Lawrence, presenting a face that seemed absolutely inaccessible to assault. The Lower Town was built upon the strand at the base of this rock. To the east, the city was protected by the St. Charles River, its channel rough and broken, its borders intersected by ravines. Beyond the St. Charles, the French army under Montcalm was posted in a strongly fortified camp that extended for five miles along the Beauport shore to the river Montmorenci.12

Montcalm’s army — which Wolfe estimated at 12,000 to 14,000 men — consisted of the regular troupes de terre and troupes de la marine, together with Canadian militia and contingents of Indian allies from the pays d’en haut. Montcalm himself, a Gascon of tested bravery and tactical skill, had held the post since 1756 and knew the ground intimately. He had chosen his positions with the eye of a soldier: the entrenchments were protected by abatis of felled trees, redoubts, and gun batteries, and every accessible approach was covered by fire. His plan was simple: to defend Quebec behind its shield of earthworks and the cliffs of the St. Lawrence, let the British exhaust themselves against his fortifications, and wait for the northern winter to drive their fleet from the river.

The Bombardment

Throughout July, the British artillery on Pointe-Lévis maintained a constant fire upon Quebec. Wolfe’s men erected batteries of cannon and mortars with great dispatch on the heights commanding the south shore, and the effect was devastating. “The upper town is considerably damaged,” Wolfe reported to Pitt on 2 September, “and the lower town entirely destroyed.”13 The bombardment set fire to the cathedral of Notre-Dame de Québec and to a considerable part of the Upper Town; the inhabitants fled the city in terror, and Montcalm’s army was forced to camp in the open.

The French responded with a desperate attempt to destroy the Pointe-Lévis batteries. On the night of 12 July, Montcalm sent a force of 1,600 men across the river to attack the British works. The enterprise miscarried in confusion — the French soldiers, advancing in the dark, fired upon one another by mistake and withdrew in disorder, “by which we lost an opportunity of defeating this large detachment,” as Wolfe drily observed.14

A second French attempt to break the siege came on the night of 18 July, when Montcalm launched seven fire-ships from Quebec — old vessels packed with combustibles and gunpowder, set alight and sent down the current in the hope of destroying the British fleet at anchor in the basin. The fire-ships drifted down on the tide at midnight, presenting a spectacle of tremendous magnificence. But the British sailors, far from panicking, boarded the drifting infernos with extraordinary resolution, ran them fast aground on the shore of the island of Orleans, and prevented them “from doing the smallest damage to the British squadron.”15 The disaster of the fire-ships was a mortifying blow to French morale.

The Stalemate

Wolfe, despite the success of the bombardment, soon recognised that the reduction of Quebec by siege alone was impossible. The city was well supplied with provisions and ammunition, the fortifications were strong, and the French army that defended it was larger than his own besieging force. The British could not storm the walls from the river side — the cliffs forbade it. They could not attack the Beauport entrenchments without crossing the Montmorenci River or the St. Charles, both of which were strongly defended. And Montcalm, a cautious and able general, would not be drawn out of his fortifications.

Wolfe resolved, therefore, to try another method. On 9 July, he passed the north channel of the St. Lawrence and encamped on the north-eastern shore, opposite Montcalm’s left flank, with the river Montmorenci between the two armies. His intention was to cross the Montmorenci below the falls and attack the French entrenchments obliquely, or to force them to abandon their positions by threatening their flank. “The ground to the eastward of the falls seemed to be, as it really is, higher than that on the enemy’s side,” he wrote, “and to command it in a manner which might be made useful to us.”16 He also discovered a ford below the falls that could be crossed at low tide.

The Repulse at Montmorenci (31 July 1759)

On 31 July, Wolfe launched his first major assault. The plan was a combined operation by land and sea: Admiral Saunders would bring ships of the line close inshore to bombard the French entrenchments; a brigade under Brigadier Monckton would land on the beach below the heights and assault the French positions directly; while a second force, crossing the Montmorenci upstream, would attack the French left flank.

The operation miscarried in a manner that was to haunt Wolfe for the rest of his life. The British grenadiers — the elite companies of the army, big men in tall mitre caps who prided themselves on their élan — were ordered to form on the beach after landing before advancing. But instead of awaiting the order to advance in regular formation, “they rushed with an impetuous ardour toward the enemy’s intrenchments in the most tumultuous confusion.”17 The French, standing firm behind their earthworks, poured a steady and well-aimed fire into the disordered British ranks. The grenadiers were thrown into confusion, unable to form under the storm of musket balls, and were forced to take shelter behind a redoubt that the French had abandoned on their approach.

Wolfe, advancing in person with the remaining brigades, managed to rally the fugitives and restore some order behind the front. But the plan of attack was effectually disconcerted. The British had lost the momentum; the tide was rising, cutting off the beach; and the general was compelled to order a retreat. The British withdrew across the Montmorenci under a steady French fire, having lost 210 killed and 230 wounded.18

The repulse at Montmorenci was a serious reverse, though not a disaster. Wolfe’s casualties were heavy but not crippling; the army remained intact. But the moral effect on the young commander was profound. Belsham, who had access to Wolfe’s correspondence, records that “the disappointment of general Wolfe must have been inexpressibly great, at the failure of the concerted plan of co-operation on the part of general Amherst.”19 The expected junction with Amherst’s army — which had taken Ticonderoga and Crown Point but had then been stalled on Lake Champlain by a lack of boats and a continued succession of storms — had never materialised. Wolfe found himself alone before Quebec, his army dwindling from sickness, his supplies running short, and the northern winter approaching with inexorable speed.

His dispatches to Pitt, penned with “remarkable perspicuity and elegance,” were pervaded by a tone of depression that demonstrated his sense of the embarrassments of his situation:

“We have almost the whole force of Canada to oppose. In such a choice of difficulties I own myself at a loss how to determine. The affairs of Great Britain I know to require the most vigorous measures; but the courage of a handful of brave men should be exerted only where there is some hopes of a favourable event. However, you may be assured that the small part of the campaign which remains, shall be employed, as far as I am able, for the honour of his majesty, and the interest of the nation.”20

To his intimate friends, Wolfe spoke more freely. He declared his determination — as Belsham puts it — “to die rather than endure the censure and reproach which invariably attend the want of success.”21 He was observed often to sigh; his consumptive cough grew worse; his frame, never robust, seemed on the point of collapse.

The Decision: The Heights of Abraham

Through August and into September, Wolfe’s situation grew more desperate. The French had received reinforcements: Bougainville had returned from a foraging expedition with fresh provisions and additional troops, and Montcalm’s army was now at its full strength. Winter would close the St. Lawrence within a matter of weeks; the fleet could not remain after October, and if the army withdrew without taking Quebec, the entire campaign would be accounted a failure.

Wolfe, in consultation with Admiral Saunders, conceived a plan of transcendent boldness — “an effort transcendently bold, and approaching in other circumstances to rashness and desperation.”[^belsham-12008] He would land his army on the northern shore of the St. Lawrence, several miles above Quebec, and scale the cliffs that had always been considered impassable. Once on the heights, he would seize the Plains of Abraham — the open ground at the back of the Upper Town, where Quebec was only slightly fortified — and force Montcalm to fight a pitched battle on ground of his choosing.

The risk was extraordinary. If the French discovered the plan and moved a substantial force to the landing site, Wolfe’s army would be destroyed on the beach or on the face of the cliff. If the ascent proved impracticable, the boats would be caught in the current and the army exposed to artillery fire from the guns of Quebec. If Montcalm refused to leave his entrenchments — as he had refused throughout the siege — the British would be isolated on the heights without supplies, cut off from the fleet, and destroyed at leisure.

But Wolfe understood that in his situation, audacity was the only alternative to failure.

The Feint

The execution was a masterpiece of co-ordination and deception. Throughout the first week of September, the British fleet under Saunders and Commodore Samuel Holmes made a conspicuous demonstration above Quebec. Holmes’s squadron — consisting of several ships of the line and a number of transports — moved up the St. Lawrence past the city, as if to threaten a landing far above Quebec, drawing the attention of the French.22 Bougainville was detached with 1,500 men to watch the motions of the British admiral, constantly marching his troops up and down the riverbank to counter the apparent British movements.

Meanwhile, Saunders, who remained in the first position below the town opposite the Beauport shore, “was ordered to make a feint with every appearance of reality, as if the troops had intended to land below the town, and attack the French intrenchments on the Beauport shore.”23 The ships of the line below Quebec opened a heavy bombardment on the Beauport entrenchments, and boats were lowered and manned as if preparing for a landing — an elaborate deception that kept Montcalm’s attention fixed on his fortified camp.

The Night Ascent

At one in the morning of 13 September 1759, the troops were embarked on board the transports and flat-bottomed boats under the direction of Commodore Holmes. They proceeded three leagues farther up the river than the intended place of landing, then fell down silently with the tide, observed by the French sentinels posted along the shore. The ships of war followed them, “and, by a well conducted navigation, arrived exactly at the time concerted, to cover their landing.”24

The landing point was a narrow cove a mile and a half above Quebec — the Anse-au-Foulon (Foulon’s Cove). Here a steep, winding path — so narrow that two men could not go abreast — climbed the precipitous cliff face to the Plains of Abraham. The path was defended at the summit by a captain’s guard of perhaps one hundred men, with a battery of guns.25

The first wave of the landing force consisted of twenty-four volunteers of the light infantry under the command of Colonel William Howe (the future general of the American Revolution, brother of Admiral Lord Howe and of the fallen Brigadier George Howe). They scrambled up the cliff in the darkness, sustaining themselves by the rugged projections of the rock and the branches of the trees and shrubs that sprang from the clefts into which the precipice was everywhere broken.26 The French picket at the summit was surprised and overpowered after a brief resistance — the small body was “speedily dislodged by the English light infantry” — and the whole army ascended the hill, hauling themselves up by roots and branches, using bayonets and musket butts to gain purchase on the rock.27

By daybreak, Wolfe had formed his army in order of battle on the heights of Abraham.

The Battle (13 September 1759)

The Dispositions

When Montcalm received the news that the English army was in actual possession of the heights of Abraham, “he declared himself unable to express his astonishment.”28 The position, he had believed, was absolutely impregnable. He immediately comprehended the necessity of risking an engagement: if he allowed Wolfe to entrench on the heights, the batteries would command Quebec and the city would be lost without a fight. He abandoned his fortified camp at Beauport, passed the river St. Charles with his army, and advanced to the attack with “great intrepidity.”29

Montcalm’s decision to fight — rather than to wait for Bougainville’s detachment to descend upon Wolfe’s rear — has been debated by military historians ever since. The French general, whose troops were experienced in irregular warfare but less practiced in European linear tactics, had perhaps 4,500 men — a mixed force of regular battalions (the Régiment de la Sarre, the Régiment de Languedoc, the Régiment de Béarn, the Régiment de Guyenne), Canadian militia, and Indians. Wolfe’s line consisted of about 4,500 regulars: the 28th Foot on the left, the 35th Foot and the Louisbourg Grenadiers in the centre, the 47th Foot and the 58th Foot on the right, with the light infantry protecting the flanks.

Wolfe drew up his army in the classic British line of battle — two ranks deep, the better to deliver a concentrated fire, rather than the three ranks of continental European practice. The men had been ordered to load their muskets with two balls each. The line stretched across the width of the Heights of Abraham, its flanks anchored on the broken ground of the escarpment.

The Action

The French advanced in column, three ranks deep, their officers at the front. The Canadian militia and Indians screened the advance, opening fire from behind bushes and rocks, picking off the British officers who stood conspicuous on the line. The British, schooled in the disciplined fire of the Prussian drill, held their fire under Wolfe’s express orders. The general strode up and down the line, calm and collected, encouraging his men by his presence.

Wolfe was wounded early in the action. A musket ball struck him in the wrist; he wrapped a handkerchief around it and continued walking the line, “without the least emotion.”30 A second ball struck him in the breast. He staggered, but did not fall. The wound was mortal. He was carried to the rear by four soldiers, his head resting on the arm of an officer, where he lay in agony, his life ebbing with his blood.

The French advanced, firing as they came, but their volleys were ragged and poorly aimed. Men fell in the British ranks, but the line held. The British battalions, their officers calling the range — “Steady! Steady! Wait for the word!” — held their muskets levelled at the French front rank.

At forty yards — some accounts say thirty — the order came.

The volley crashed out like a single discharge of a great cannon. Every musket in the British line spoke at once. The front ranks of the French column dissolved in a single terrible instant — “a sheet of flame and a storm of bullets” that swept away the French officers and the front rank as if they had been hit by a scythe.

The British line advanced two paces, reloaded, and fired again.

Then the British advanced with the bayonet.

The French line — already shattered, its officers dead or wounded, its ranks broken by the storm of fire — gave way in confusion. The crisis of the battle had passed. The engagement had lasted barely fifteen minutes.31

The Death of Wolfe

As the French streamed back towards the city walls, Wolfe, lying at the point of death, was roused by the distant sound of “They fly! they fly!”

“Who fly?” the dying general demanded.

Told that it was the French — that the enemy was completely broken — he murmured, “Then I depart content,” turned on his side, and almost immediately expired in the arms of victory.32

Belsham, who writes with evident admiration, describes the moment in phrases that have echoed through two centuries of British historiography: “A death more glorious, and attended with circumstances more picturesque and interesting, is no-where to be found in the annals of history. The death of Epaminondas only, to which that of Wolfe has frequently been compared, seems to dispute the pre-eminence.”33

The Death of Montcalm

Montcalm, too, was mortally wounded in the action. As the French line shattered and his men fled through the city gates, the gallant Gascon was struck by a musket ball, probably from the volley of the 28th or 43rd Foot. He was carried into the city, supported by his officers, and laid in the Château St. Louis. Informed that his wound was mortal, he replied, with the dignified composure of a soldier: “So much the better; I shall not live to see the surrender of Quebec.” He died the following morning, 14 September 1759.

The command of the army devolved upon Brigadier-General George Townshend and Brigadier-General Robert Monckton (who had himself been wounded). The pursuit of the French was continued to the walls of the city, while the Canadian militia, abandoned by their officers, dispersed into the woods. The shattered remains of the French army — perhaps 3,000 men — retreated under the Chevalier de Lévis to Montreal, marching through the night in a column of desperate men.

The Surrender (18 September 1759)

The city of Quebec, “struck with consternation at this event, almost immediately capitulated, though still provided with the means of a vigorous defence.”34 The Marquis de Vaudreuil, the governor-general, and the French military command, uncertain of the state of their defences and fearful that the British fleet would bombard the Lower Town into rubble, opened negotiations on 15 September. The articles of capitulation were signed on 18 September.

The garrison was permitted to march out with the honours of war — drums beating, colours flying — and was to be transported to France at British expense. The civil population was guaranteed the protection of their property, their religion, and their laws. The militia was to be disbanded; the regulars were prisoners of war. On 18 September, British troops entered Quebec, and the Union Jack was raised over the Château St. Louis.

The Aftermath

The Winter of 1759–1760

General James Murray was appointed governor of Quebec, with a garrison of perhaps 5,000 men — a force that would have to endure a winter of desperate hardship in a city that had been half-destroyed by the bombardment. The fleet under Admiral Saunders, “lest it should be locked up by the frost in the river St. Lawrence,” sailed for England at the end of October, leaving Murray isolated in his shattered citadel.35

The winter was appalling. The British troops, many of them raised in the mild climate of southern England, had never experienced a Canadian winter. Scurvy and typhus swept through the garrison; by April 1760, Murray had barely 3,000 fit soldiers.

The Battle of Sainte-Foy (28 April 1760)

In the spring of 1760, the Chevalier de Lévis, who had succeeded Montcalm as commander of the French forces, assembled with great diligence whatever troops remained in Canada and began his march from Montreal in April, hoping to retake Quebec before the British fleet could arrive.36

Murray, though much inferior in numbers, refused to remain within the walls. He determined to risk an engagement, knowing that a defeat would not prevent his retiring into the city, but that a victory would secure his position. On 28 April 1760, he led his garrison out of Quebec to meet Lévis on the Plains of Abraham — the same field where Wolfe had triumphed seven months before.

The battle that followed — the Battle of Sainte-Foy — was a savage and confused affair fought in a spring snowstorm. The ground was wet with melting snow; the muskets misfired; the visibility was poor. Murray’s troops fought with desperate valour, but the disparity in numbers was overwhelming. Lévis had 7,000 men; Murray had 3,000. After several hours of intense fighting, in which the British lost 1,100 killed and wounded — more than a third of their strength — Murray was forced to order a retreat into the city. Lévis had won the field.37

But Lévis had won only the field. He had no siege train capable of reducing the walls of Quebec, and his army was already short of provisions and ammunition. He invested the city, but he could not storm it.

The Relief

The fate of Quebec hung in the balance through the first three weeks of May. Inside the walls, Murray’s garrison, reduced to the verge of starvation, waited for a relief that had to come by sea. Lévis pressed the siege with desperate haste, knowing that every day’s delay brought the British fleet closer.

On the morning of 9 May 1760, the lookouts on the heights of Quebec sighted a sail in the Saint-Laurent. It was the frigate Lowestoffe, the vanguard of a British relief squadron under Lord Colvill. By 12 May, the main body of the fleet — ten ships of the line — was anchored in the basin. Lévis, who had no naval force to oppose them, raised the siege with great precipitation, abandoning his provisions, stores, and artillery, and retreated with his army to Montreal.38

The Surrender of Montreal (1760)

The relief of Quebec sealed the fate of New France. Throughout the summer of 1760, three British armies converged upon Montreal: General Amherst advancing from Lake Ontario down the St. Lawrence; General Murray marching from Quebec up the river; and Colonel Haviland, after the reduction of the Isle-aux-Noix, approaching from the south.

The Marquis de Vaudreuil, perceiving himself completely invested and despairing of relief, demanded a capitulation. On 8 September 1760, Montreal surrendered, and with it all of New France. The conquest of Canada was complete.39

The Significance

The capture of Quebec was the decisive event of the Seven Years’ War in North America. It broke the power of France on the continent, secured the British dominion over Canada, and established the conditions for the Peace of Paris (1763), under which France formally ceded Canada to Great Britain.

For the British Empire, Quebec was the most celebrated victory of the war — the triumph that made 1759 the annus mirabilis. The death of Wolfe, immortalised in Benjamin West’s painting The Death of General Wolfe (1770), became one of the founding myths of the British imperial tradition: the young hero who gave his life for his country at the moment of victory, dying “content” that his duty had been done.

For New France, the fall of Quebec was a catastrophe from which the French empire in America never recovered. The French-speaking population of Canada — the habitants, the seigneurs, the clergy — were left to make their accommodation with a Protestant British Crown. Their rights of religion, language, and civil law were guaranteed by the articles of capitulation and later confirmed by the Quebec Act of 1774, but the political and military structures of New France were swept away forever.

The campaign also demonstrated — in the most spectacular terms — the power of combined amphibious operations under determined leadership. The partnership of Wolfe and Saunders — the army commander of reckless audacity and the naval commander of calm professionalism — became the model for future British amphibious warfare. Without the fleet, Wolfe could not have ascended the St. Lawrence, landed his army, or maintained his communications; without the army, the fleet could not have taken the city. It was, as Campbell wrote, “a design so bold and adventurous, that even the sanguine temper of General Wolfe began to despair of its success”; and it was carried through by the cooperation of sea and land forces “with a unanimity, ardour, and perseverance, that can never be enough celebrated.”40
