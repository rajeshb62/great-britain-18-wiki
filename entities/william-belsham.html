William Belsham

William Belsham (1752–1827) was an English political writer and historian, the author of two major historical works — Memoirs of the Reign of George III (5 vols., covering 1760–1793) and History of Great Britain from the Revolution, 1688, to the Conclusion of the Treaty of Amiens, 1802 (12 vols., 1805) — that together constitute the most detailed Whig narrative of eighteenth-century British political history produced in the generation after David Hume. Belsham was a dissenter in religion (a Unitarian), a supporter of parliamentary reform, sympathetic to the American colonists and the French Revolutionaries in their earlier phases, and a critic of British ministerial policy across the board. His histories are distinguished by their exhaustive parliamentary reporting, their careful attention to foreign affairs, and their consistent Whig-liberal interpretive framework.

Of the four major historians whose works are source texts in this wiki — Gilbert Burnet, Tobias Smollett, Belsham, and John Campbell — Belsham is chronologically the latest and intellectually the closest to a modern historian. He wrote with the advantage of retrospect, drawing on the accumulated literature of a century of British historiography, and he had access to parliamentary materials — the published debates, the state papers, the memoirs of participants — that his predecessors lacked. His works are the most extensive single narrative source in the wiki, covering from the Glorious Revolution to the early nineteenth century in unmatched detail.

Life and Career

Early Life and Formation (1752–c. 1790)

Remarkably little is known of Belsham’s early life. He was born in 1752, probably in Bedfordshire or London, into a dissenting family of moderate means. His precise parentage is obscure, but his intellectual formation appears to have been in the circle of rational Dissent — the Unitarians and radical Whigs who clustered around Joseph Priestley, Richard Price, and the Warrington Academy. He was educated in the dissenting tradition, which gave him a grounding in classical languages, modern history, and the principles of civil and religious liberty, but he did not attend university (the English universities being closed to Nonconformists by the Test Acts).

His choice of a literary and political career — rather than the ministry, which was the usual destination of educated Dissenters — suggests that he was temperamentally drawn to the world of print and public debate. By the late 1780s he had established himself in London as a political writer, contributing to the periodical press and publishing essays on public affairs. The outbreak of the French Revolution in 1789, which divided the English radical movement into those (like Burke) who turned against the Revolution and those (like Belsham) who continued to defend its principles, provided the context for his first substantial publications.

Political Writings and Principles (1790–1805)

Belsham’s early writings were in defence of civil and religious liberty. He wrote against the Test Acts, which excluded Dissenters from public office; against the slave trade, which he condemned on both moral and economic grounds; and against the repressive legislation of the 1790s — the Treason and Sedition Bills of 1795 (the “Two Acts”) — by which Pitt’s government suppressed the radical societies. His defence of the French Revolution, carefully qualified to avoid the excesses of the Jacobins, placed him in the circle of Charles James Fox and the radical Whig opposition.

His political principles can be summarised as: a belief in representative government and parliamentary reform; a conviction that civil liberty and religious toleration were inseparable; a suspicion of executive power, especially when exercised by the crown and its ministers; a sympathy for the principles of 1688 interpreted in a progressive, rather than merely conservative, sense; and a Whig interpretation of history that saw the progress of British liberty as the central theme of the national story. These principles inform every page of his historical works.

The Historical Works (1790s–1805)

Belsham began his historical work in the 1790s with a history of the reign of George III. The Memoirs of the Reign of George III was published in four volumes (later a fifth was added), covering from the accession of the king in 1760 to the opening of the French Revolutionary War in 1793. It was a work of contemporary history — Belsham was writing about events that had occurred within his own lifetime, many of which he had followed as a politically engaged observer.

In 1805, Belsham published his magnum opus: the 12-volume History of Great Britain, from the Revolution, 1688, to the Conclusion of the Treaty of Amiens, 1802. This was a comprehensive national history that covered the entire sweep of British affairs from the Glorious Revolution to the early nineteenth century. It was intended as a Whig corrective to Hume’s History of England, which had presented a Tory interpretation that dominated the late eighteenth-century historical market. Belsham wrote in the preface that he sought to show “the progress of liberty and of the national prosperity” — the steady expansion of constitutional government, the growth of commerce and empire, and the improvement of the condition of the people — as the central theme of post-1688 British history.

Later Years and Death (1805–1827)

After the completion of the History in 1805, Belsham continued to write on political and philosophical subjects. He published essays on liberty, toleration, and the progress of civilisation, and he contributed to the reviews. His later writings show a deepening pessimism: the French Revolution, which he had initially welcomed, had descended into military dictatorship under Napoleon; the British government, under Pitt and his successors, had become more repressive rather than more liberal; and the cause of parliamentary reform, which he had championed for forty years, seemed no closer to success.

He died in 1827, at the age of seventy-five, having outlived nearly all of the political generation with which he had been associated. Charles James Fox, his political hero, had died in 1806; the radical movement of the 1790s had dispersed; and the Reform Act of 1832, which would have vindicated many of his positions, was still five years in the future. His history, however, survived as a monument of Whig scholarship — the most detailed narrative account of eighteenth-century British politics produced in the generation between Hume and the Victorian historians.

Major Works

History of Great Britain (12 vols., 1805)

Belsham’s History of Great Britain covers the period 1688–1802 in twelve volumes. The volumes held in this wiki cover:

  • Volume II — The reign of George I (1714–1727): the Jacobite rising of 1715, the Septennial Act, the South Sea Bubble, the Atterbury plot, and the rise of Walpole
  • Volume IV — The reign of George II (1727–1760): the fall of Walpole, the War of Jenkins’ Ear, the War of the Austrian Succession, the Jacobite rising of 1745, and the Seven Years’ War
  • Volume VI — The early reign of George III (1768–1778): the American Revolutionary crisis from the Boston Massacre through Saratoga and the French alliance
  • Volume X — The French Revolutionary War (1795–1798): the Two Acts, Bonaparte’s Italian campaign, the Irish Rebellion of 1798, and the Battle of the Nile
  • Volume XI — The French Revolutionary War (1798–1800): the Income Tax, the siege of Acre, the Irish Union debates, the fall of Tippoo Sultan
  • Volume XII — The final years of the war (1800–1802): the Armed Neutrality, the Battle of Copenhagen, the conquest of Egypt, and the Peace of Amiens

Memoirs of the Reign of George III (5 vols., c. 1790s)

  • Volume I — The accession of George III, the end of the Seven Years’ War, the Bute ministry, the Peace of Paris (1763), the Wilkes affair, the Stamp Act and its repeal (1760–1766)

Historiographical Method

Belsham’s method differs significantly from those of his predecessors in this wiki. Where Gilbert Burnet wrote as a participant, Belsham wrote as a retrospective scholar. Where Tobias Smollett wrote as a journalist-historian, producing a continuous narrative at high speed, Belsham wrote with deliberate pace and careful organisation. Where John Campbell wrote a specialised naval history, Belsham wrote a comprehensive political history.

The distinguishing features of Belsham’s historical work are:

Exhaustive parliamentary reporting. Belsham devotes more space to the debates of the House of Commons than any previous historian. He reproduces speeches in extenso — the arguments of Pitt, Fox, Burke, North, and their contemporaries — and he follows the legislative process with an attention to detail that makes his history a primary source for the history of parliamentary politics.

Whig-liberal interpretive framework. Belsham writes from a consistently Whig perspective. The Glorious Revolution is the foundation of British liberty; the growth of parliamentary power and the extension of civil liberty are the central themes of national history; the American colonists were justified in their resistance to British taxation; the French Revolution, in its early phases, was a legitimate assertion of human rights. His history is openly argumentative, a sustained polemic against the Tory interpretation of Hume.

International scope. Belsham integrates British history into the broader context of European and global affairs. His accounts of the Seven Years’ War, the American Revolution, and the French Revolutionary Wars are attentive to the diplomatic, military, and commercial dimensions of international conflict. He writes with particular authority on the American war, to which he devotes extensive coverage.

Critical assessment. Belsham is not an uncritical Whig. He criticises the corruption of Whig ministries under Walpole and the Pelhams, the repressive policies of Pitt’s wartime government, and the failure of British statesmen to reconcile the American colonies. His heroes — Fox, Burke (in his American phase), the Rockingham Whigs — are not immune to criticism.

Historiographical Significance

Belsham’s History of Great Britain was the most comprehensive Whig narrative of the post-1688 period published in its generation. It was intended as a direct alternative to Hume’s History of England, which had dominated the historical market since the 1760s with its Tory interpretation of the Stuart and Hanoverian reigns. Belsham did not displace Hume — Hume’s elegance, irony, and narrative power made him irreplaceable — but he provided the materials for a Whig counter-narrative that later historians would draw upon.

His influence on the Victorian Whig historians — Henry Hallam, Thomas Babington Macaulay, and William Stubbs — was indirect but real. Macaulay’s History of England (1848–1861), the supreme literary achievement of Whig historiography, covered only the period 1685–1702 (where Belsham begins) and adopted an interpretive framework that Belsham had helped to establish. The detailed parliamentary reporting that is one of Belsham’s distinctive features became a hallmark of Macaulay’s method as well.

For the historian of the eighteenth century, Belsham’s value lies in his comprehensiveness. He covers the political, military, diplomatic, and commercial history of a period that most historians treat only in fragments. His works are the fullest single narrative source for the politics of the Hanoverian era, supplementing the specialised naval histories of Campbell and the general history of Smollett.

Relationships

  • Gilbert Burnet — Belsham’s History begins where Burnet’s History of His Own Time ends (1688–1715); together they provide a continuous Whig narrative from the Revolution through the early nineteenth century
  • Tobias Smollett — Belsham’s period overlaps with Smollett’s (1688–1748) but with a very different political sensibility; Belsham is a Whig where Smollett is a Tory
  • John Campbell — Belsham’s general political history complements Campbell’s specialised naval history; Belsham covers the politics and diplomacy that Campbell’s admirals served
  • Charles James Fox — Belsham’s political hero; the Whig opposition to Pitt is the dominant theme of Belsham’s coverage of the French Revolutionary period
  • Edmund Burke — Belsham admired Burke’s American speeches but parted company with him over the French Revolution
  • William Pitt the Younger — Belsham’s antagonist; he criticises Pitt’s repressive domestic policies and his conduct of the war
