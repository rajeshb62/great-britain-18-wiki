George III

George William Frederick (4 June 1738 – 29 January 1820) was King of Great Britain and Ireland from 25 October 1760 until the union of the two kingdoms in 1801, and thereafter King of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland until his death. His reign of fifty-nine years — the longest of any British monarch until that of his granddaughter Queen Victoria — encompassed the end of the Seven Years’ War, the loss of the American colonies, the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, and the Union with Ireland. He was the third Hanoverian monarch and the first to be born in Britain and to speak English as his first language.

Early Life and Accession

Born at Norfolk House in London, George was the eldest son of Frederick, Prince of Wales, and Princess Augusta of Saxe-Gotha. His father’s death in 1751 brought him to the front of the succession at the age of twelve. He was raised in a deeply protective household at Leicester House under the tutelage of his mother and Lord Bute, her confidant — a circle that instilled in him a strict moral code, a reverence for the established Church, and a suspicion of the Whig grandees who had dominated politics under his grandfather.

George succeeded the throne on 25 October 1760 upon the death of George II. He was twenty-two years old. His accession speech to Parliament declared that he “gloried in the name of Britain” — a pointed contrast to his German-speaking predecessor, and an immediate signal of his desire to govern as a patriot king.

Belsham records the auspicious opening of the reign: the new Monarch’s “manners were in the highest degree decorous, his morals unblemished, and his personal accomplishments corresponded with the elevation of his rank and station.” The nation, fresh from the triumphs of the Annus Mirabilis, greeted him with transports of joy.

The End of the Seven Years’ War and the Bute Ministry

The King’s first act was to appoint his former tutor, the Earl of Bute, as Secretary of State and later First Lord of the Treasury. Bute’s rapid rise — and his Scottish background — excited immediate opposition. The Treaty of Paris (1763), which ended the Seven Years’ War, was bitterly attacked by William Pitt the Elder as too generous to France, though it secured Canada, the Mississippi valley, and Florida for Britain.

Bute, temperamentally unsuited to parliamentary combat, resigned in April 1763 after less than a year as Prime Minister. His departure did not end his influence: the “Cabinet of Carlton House,” centred on the King’s mother and Bute, continued to exert a secret and malign influence over affairs — what Pitt would later call “something behind the throne greater than the throne itself.”

The Grenville Ministry and the Wilkes Affair

George Grenville succeeded Bute as First Lord of the Treasury in 1763. The King found Grenville personally difficult — stiff, lecturing, insistent — but the ministry was competent. Its great crisis came with the prosecution of John Wilkes, whose paper The North Briton No. 45 had attacked the King’s speech from the throne, charging His Majesty with uttering a direct falsehood. Grenville’s government issued a general warrant for the arrest of the authors, printers, and publishers, and Wilkes was committed to the Tower.

The general warrant controversy — a warrant that named no specific person — became a cause célèbre. Lord Chief Justice Pratt ruled the warrant illegal, and Wilkes was released. The affair made Wilkes a popular hero and dealt lasting damage to Grenville’s reputation.

The ministry fell in July 1765 after Grenville, learning of the King’s attempts to replace him, demanded the dismissal of Bute’s allies as the price of remaining. George, thoroughly exasperated, dismissed him.

The Rockingham Ministry and the Stamp Act Crisis

The Marquis of Rockingham formed a ministry in July 1765. Its great achievement was the repeal of the Stamp Act (1765) — Grenville’s tax on the American colonies — which had provoked an unprecedented wave of colonial resistance. Rockingham coupled repeal with the Declaratory Act, asserting Parliament’s authority to legislate for the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.”

George III assented to the repeal reluctantly. He had supported Grenville’s American policy and believed the colonies ought to contribute to the costs of empire. The repeal was carried by a narrow majority in the Commons, with Pitt delivering powerful speeches in favour of colonial rights.

The Chatham Ministry and the Townshend Acts

The King’s next experiment was a ministry formed around Pitt, now Earl of Chatham. It was a catastrophic failure. Chatham’s health collapsed — he suffered from severe gout and what contemporaries described as a nervous disorder — and he withdrew from active business for months at a time. Into the vacuum stepped Charles Townshend, the brilliant but erratic Chancellor of the Exchequer, who in 1767 pushed through the Townshend Acts — duties on glass, lead, paper, paint, and tea imported into the colonies. The Acts rekindled American resistance at the very moment reconciliation seemed possible.

The Years of Lord North (1770–1782)

After Chatham’s collapse and Townshend’s sudden death in 1767, the Duke of Grafton led a weak ministry until 1770, when Lord North — a man of mild temper, pleasant wit, and unmistakable loyalty to the Crown — became First Lord. North would remain in power for twelve years, the longest continuous ministry of the reign.

North’s ministry was dominated by the American crisis. The Boston Tea Party (1773), the Coercive Acts (1774), the battles of Lexington and Concord (1775), and the Declaration of Independence (1776) followed in grim succession. The King threw his full support behind the policy of coercion. He read despatches from America with obsessive attention, wrote personally to his ministers, and refused all compromise. “I am fighting the battle of the legislature,” he declared. “The colonies must either submit or triumph.”

The war went catastrophically wrong. Burgoyne’s surrender at Saratoga (1777) brought France into the war. The loss of the West Indies and Minorca followed. The final blow came with Cornwallis’s surrender at Yorktown in October 1781. North received the news “as he would have taken a ball in the breast.” The King drafted a letter of abdication before accepting the reality of defeat.

The Fall of North and the Ministry of Shelburne

North resigned in March 1782, ending twelve years at the helm. The King, humiliated and furious, was forced to accept first the Rockingham ministry — whose leader he distrusted — and then the Shelburne administration. The Peace of Paris (1783) recognised American independence. George III lamented the loss but accepted it as an accomplished fact.

The Fox-North Coalition and the India Bill Crisis

The King’s greatest domestic battle came in 1783, when the Fox-North coalition — an unnatural alliance of his two most hated opponents — brought forward Charles James Fox’s India Bill. The bill proposed to transfer control of the East India Company’s territorial possessions to commissioners appointed by Parliament. The King considered it an unconstitutional usurpation of the royal prerogative.

Using the private influence of Lord Temple, the King made it known — through a card written and circulated — that “whoever voted for the India Bill was not only not his friend, but would be considered by him as his enemy.” The message was carried to the House of Lords, where the bill was defeated. The coalition fell immediately.

This intervention — the most direct of his reign — was fiercely criticised by Fox and Burke as a violation of constitutional propriety. The King defended it as a necessary defence of the Crown’s legitimate authority.

Pitt the Younger

The King’s victory over the coalition brought William Pitt the Younger to power at the age of twenty-four. Pitt would dominate British politics for two decades. Unlike North, he was not a creature of the court; unlike Fox, he was not the King’s enemy. The relationship between monarch and minister was professional rather than warm — Pitt kept the King at a careful distance — but it was stable.

The great achievement of Pitt’s first ministry was the India Act (1784), which placed the Company’s affairs under a Board of Control answerable to the Crown — a more moderate version of the defeated Fox bill. The King, having destroyed the original, accepted the revision.

The Regency Crisis of 1788

In the autumn of 1788, George III suffered his first major attack of what contemporaries called “madness.” Modern historians believe he suffered from porphyria, a metabolic disorder that causes delirium, hallucinations, and violent physical symptoms. The King raved for hours, could not be restrained, and was placed in a straitwaistcoat.

The crisis threw the political world into chaos. If the King was permanently incapacitated, a regency would be necessary — and the heir apparent, the Prince of Wales, was a close ally of Charles James Fox and the opposition. Pitt and his ministers moved cautiously, introducing a Regency Bill that would strictly limit the Regent’s powers. Before the bill could pass, the King recovered in February 1789 — a recovery celebrated with nationwide rejoicing.

The Regency crisis revealed the fragility of the constitution in an age of monarchical incapacity, and it deepened the enmity between the King and his eldest son.

The French Revolution and War

The French Revolution erupted in 1789. At first, Pitt’s government remained neutral, but the execution of Louis XVI in January 1793, followed by French declarations of war, drew Britain into a conflict that would last — with a brief interlude — until 1815.

The King, as head of state and Supreme Governor of the Church of England, was a natural symbol of resistance to revolutionary atheism and regicide. His piety, his domestic virtue, and his very plainness — he was known as “Farmer George” for his enthusiastic interest in agriculture — became emblems of British stability. His annual messages to Parliament, his proclamations for fasts and thanksgivings, and his constant presence at Windsor gave the nation a sense of continuity.

Yet the war was a heavy burden. The national debt soared. Food prices rose. The Irish rebellion of 1798, inspired by French revolutionary principles, was suppressed with severity.

The Union with Ireland

The rebellion convinced Pitt that the only permanent solution to the Irish problem was a legislative union between Great Britain and Ireland, with Catholic emancipation as its accompaniment — the removal of the remaining penal laws that excluded Catholics from Parliament and public office.

The Act of Union passed in 1800, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland on 1 January 1801. Ireland was granted 100 seats in the House of Commons and 28 representative peers in the Lords. But when Pitt attempted to deliver Catholic emancipation as the corollary of union, the King refused point-blank. He considered Catholic emancipation a violation of his coronation oath to defend the Protestant establishment.

Pitt resigned rather than abandon the measure, and the King took personal charge of the negotiations to form Addington’s ministry. Catholic emancipation was shelved for a generation.

The King’s Final Years

George III’s health deteriorated after 1801. He suffered a relapse in 1804, recovered, and then — in 1810 — suffered a final, permanent breakdown after the death of his beloved youngest daughter, Princess Amelia. The Regency Act of 1811 placed the Prince of Wales in power as Regent for the remaining nine years of the King’s life.

The King spent his final years at Windsor Castle, blind, deaf, and intermittently lucid. He died on 29 January 1820, at the age of eighty-one.

Character and Assessment

George III was the most conscientious of monarchs. He rose early, worked methodically through despatches, presided at Cabinet meetings with careful attention, and maintained a household of exemplary domestic virtue — in stark contrast to his profligate sons. He was a devoted husband to Queen Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz, whom he married in 1761, and fathered fifteen children.

His interest in agriculture — he wrote pamphlets on farming under the pseudonym “Ralph Robinson,” corresponded with Arthur Young, and transformed Windsor Great Park into a model farm — earned him the affectionate nickname “Farmer George.” He was a patron of the arts: he supported Handel’s memory, founded the Royal Academy in 1768, and assembled the library that became the foundation of the British Library.

His political record is more contested. He was stubborn and often inflexible — qualities that served him well in resisting the French Revolution but badly in American policy. His personal intervention in the India Bill crisis of 1783 remains controversial. Some historians see him as a constitutional monarch defending his prerogative; others as a meddling executive who corrupted the constitution.

On the central question of America, George III was intransigent. He believed from first to last that Parliament’s authority over the colonies was absolute. Belsham, writing in the 1790s, captured the tragic dimension: a King who came to the throne amid universal rejoicing, whose reign opened with the greatest victories in British history, and who lived to see his empire sundered by a war that his own policies had done so much to provoke.

“He is a noble and magnificent prince; but he has a most unhappy temper for a king.” — The Duke of Grafton
