Quebec

Quebec — the capital of French Canada, the fortress at the gates of the St. Lawrence, the prize over which the empires of France and Britain contended for mastery of North America. From its foundation in 1608 to its capture in 1759, it was the seat of French power on the continent. Its fall to General James Wolfe in the most celebrated battle of the 18th century decided the fate of Canada and transformed the strategic balance of the New World.

The City

Quebec occupied one of the most formidable natural positions in North America. It stood at the confluence of the St. Lawrence and St. Charles rivers, on the northern shore of the St. Lawrence, some 400 miles from the Atlantic. The city was divided into two parts. The Lower Town — a jumble of warehouses, wharves, merchants’ houses, and narrow streets — clung to the strand at the base of a steep cliff. The Upper Town, crowned by the fortress of St. Louis and the cathedral of Notre-Dame, stood atop a sheer rock escarpment that rose 60 metres above the river. The rock continued with a bold and steep front far to the westward, running parallel to the St. Lawrence, presenting a face that seemed absolutely inaccessible to assault.

To the east, the city was protected by the St. Charles River, its channel rough and broken, its borders intersected by ravines. Beyond the St. Charles, the ground rose to the Beauport shore, where the French had constructed a formidable line of entrenchments extending eastward to the Montmorenci River. To the north, the thickly wooded hinterland offered no obvious line of approach for a European army.

The fortifications, though not of the first European class, were strong enough to repel any direct assault. The walls of the Upper Town were of stone, mounting heavy cannon; the Lower Town, though more exposed, was protected by the elevation of the guns above and by the batteries that commanded the river approach.

French Canada

When the British resolved upon the conquest of Canada in the Seven Years’ War, Quebec was both the military objective and the political prize. New France — stretching from Acadia to the Great Lakes, from Hudson Bay to the Mississippi — had its heart in the St. Lawrence valley. The city of Quebec was its capital, its administrative centre, its ecclesiastical seat, and its commercial emporium.

The population of New France numbered perhaps 70,000 souls at the outbreak of the Seven Years’ War — a small number set against the million and a half British colonists to the south, but a population united by language, religion, and military tradition. The French regular forces in Canada, under the command of the Marquis de Montcalm, were supplemented by the militia of the parishes — hardy farmers and woodsmen whose knowledge of the forest and the canoe made them formidable irregulars.

Quebec itself was a city of perhaps 8,000 inhabitants — government officials, clergy, officers of the garrison, merchants of the fur trade, artisans, and the families of the seigneurs. The society was hierarchical, Catholic, and intensely French. The Bishop of Quebec was a prince of the Church; the Intendant managed the civil government; the Governor-General commanded the military. Below them, the seigneurs held their estates in long narrow strips along the river; the habitants, the freeholding farmers, tilled the land in exchange for modest feudal dues.

The economy rested on the fur trade — beaver pelts from the interior, carried by canoe to Montreal and then to Quebec for shipment to Europe — and on the fisheries of the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Agriculture in the St. Lawrence valley supplied the colony’s subsistence; the forests yielded timber for shipbuilding; but the colony was never self-sufficient, and its dependence on French subsidies was a constant vulnerability.

The Siege of Quebec (1759)

The expedition against Quebec was the centrepiece of William Pitt the Elder’s grand strategy for the year 1759 — the annus mirabilis of British arms. While Hawke destroyed the French fleet at Quiberon Bay, while Boscawen drove the French from Lagos, while Amherst reduced Ticonderoga and Crown Point, the most difficult and dangerous branch of the plan was allotted to the young Major-General James Wolfe.

Wolfe had distinguished himself at Louisbourg in 1758. He was now thirty-two years old, tall, thin, red-haired, and afflicted with a constitution that seemed perpetually on the verge of collapse. His orders were to ascend the St. Lawrence, invest Quebec, and reduce it.

The Approach

The British armament — some 9,000 soldiers of the regular army, supported by a powerful naval squadron under Admiral Charles Saunders — arrived at the island of Orleans on 26 June 1759. The sight that greeted them was daunting: the cliffs of Quebec rising sheer from the river, the French entrenchments stretching for miles along the Beauport shore, the fleur-de-lis floating defiantly above the fortress.

Wolfe established his headquarters on the island of Orleans, directly opposite the city. From there, he could study the French positions: Montcalm’s main army of perhaps 14,000 men — including regulars, militia, and Indians — entrenched from the St. Charles River eastward to the Montmorenci falls. The position was immensely strong — the entrenchments were protected by abatis, redoubts, and gun batteries, and the ground before them was swept by the fire of the French artillery.

The Repulse at Montmorenci

Wolfe resolved to strike directly at Montcalm’s entrenchments. On 31 July 1759, he launched a major assault on the French position near the falls of Montmorenci. The plan was for a brigade to cross the river above the falls and attack the French left, while the main force landed on the beach below the entrenchments and assaulted the French centre.

The operation went wrong from the start. The grenadiers — the elite companies of the army — rushed to the attack with irregular impetuosity before the main body had formed. The French poured volleys into the exposed British ranks; the grenadiers were thrown into confusion and suffered severely in the retreat. Wolfe, advancing in person with the remaining brigades, managed to restore order, but the attack had been fatally disconcerted. He was compelled to order a withdrawal. The British lost over 400 men; the French, barely 200.

The repulse affected Wolfe more deeply than his officers knew. He was observed often to sigh; to his intimate friends he declared that he would die rather than endure the censure of failure. His dispatches to England were penned in a tone of depression that barely veiled his desperation:

“We have almost the whole force of Canada to oppose. In such a choice of difficulties I own myself at a loss how to determine. The affairs of Great Britain I know to require the most vigorous measures; but the courage of a handful of brave men should be exerted only where there is some hopes of a favourable event.”

The Heights of Abraham

Through August and into September, Wolfe’s situation grew more desperate. His army was dwindling from sickness; his supplies were running short; winter would close the St. Lawrence within weeks. Montcalm, content to remain behind his fortifications, showed no intention of risking a general engagement.

Wolfe conceived a plan of transcendent boldness — a plan that approached, in other circumstances, to rashness and desperation. He would land his army on the northern shore of the St. Lawrence, several miles above Quebec, and scale the cliffs that had always been considered impassable. Once on the heights, he would seize the Plains of Abraham — the open ground at the back of the Upper Town — and force Montcalm to fight.

The execution was a masterpiece of co-ordination and deception. Admiral Saunders, commanding the fleet, moved a division of ships several leagues upriver, as if to threaten a landing far above the city, drawing the attention of the French. Meanwhile, during the night of 12–13 September 1759, Wolfe’s army embarked in flat-bottomed boats and dropped silently downstream.

The landing point was a narrow cove — later called the Anse au Foulon — where a steep, wooded path climbed the cliff face to the Plains of Abraham. The first wave, commanded by Colonel William Howe (the future admiral’s brother), scrambled up the precipice, sustaining themselves by the rugged projections of the rock and the branches of the trees and shrubs that sprang from the clefts. A French picket at the summit was surprised and overpowered. By dawn, Wolfe had 4,500 men drawn up in line of battle on the Plains of Abraham.

The Battle of Quebec (13 September 1759)

When Montcalm received the news that the English army was in actual possession of the heights of Abraham, his astonishment was complete. He immediately understood the necessity of risking an engagement: if he allowed Wolfe to entrench, Quebec was lost.

Abandoning his fortified camp at Beauport, Montcalm marshalled his forces and advanced with great intrepidity across the plain. He had perhaps 4,500 men — a mixed force of regulars, militia, and Indians — against Wolfe’s 4,500 regulars. The British were drawn up in the classic line: two deep, muskets loaded and bayonets fixed, the red coats a bright splash of colour against the green meadow.

The French advanced in column, firing as they came. The British battalion companies, schooled in the disciplined fire of the Prussian drill, held their fire until the French were within forty yards. Then the volley crashed out — a single rippling crash of sound. The front ranks of the French column dissolved. Before the French could reform, the British advanced with the bayonet.

Wolfe, standing conspicuous in the front of the line, was shot through the wrist. He wrapped a handkerchief around the wound and continued giving orders without the least emotion. A second ball struck him in the breast. He was carried to the rear, where he lay in agony, his head resting on the arm of an officer.

The battle was decided within fifteen minutes. The French line broke; Montcalm himself, struck by a musket ball, was carried from the field mortally wounded. As the French streamed back towards the city walls, Wolfe was roused by the distant sound of “They fly! they fly!” He asked who fled. Told that it was the French, he murmured: “Then I depart content.” He died almost immediately — the most celebrated death in the annals of British military history.

Quebec capitulated five days later.

The Winter Siege (1759–1760)

The fall of Quebec did not end the war in Canada. The remnants of Montcalm’s army, under the Chevalier de Lévis, retreated to Montreal and prepared for a counter-stroke. In the spring of 1760, Lévis descended upon Quebec with an army of 7,000 men, determined to recapture the city before the British fleet could arrive.

General James Murray, who had been appointed governor of Quebec, had only 3,000 fit soldiers. He sallied forth to give battle at the Plains of Abraham on 28 April 1760, but was driven back into the city by overwhelming numbers. The Siege of Quebec began in earnest.

But Lévis had no siege train, and the British fleet — thanks to Hawke’s victory at Quiberon Bay — arrived at the end of May. The first British ship to appear, the frigate Lowestoffe, signalled that the fleet was close behind. Lévis raised the siege and retreated to Montreal, where he surrendered in September 1760. The conquest of Canada was complete.

British Rule

Under British rule, Quebec was transformed. The Treaty of Paris (1763) formally ceded Canada to Great Britain, and the city ceased to be the capital of a French colony and became the seat of a British military government. The civil population — French-speaking, Catholic, and deeply attached to its customs — faced an uncertain future.

The British government, guided by a pragmatic understanding that the French Canadians would not be easily assimilated, pursued a policy of conciliation. The Quebec Act of 1774 guaranteed the free practice of the Catholic religion, restored French civil law for private matters, and preserved the seigneurial system of land tenure. The traditional elites — the seigneurs and the clergy — were confirmed in their authority. In return, the French Canadians remained loyal to the Crown during the American Revolutionary War — a loyalty that would be tested in 1775.

The American Invasion (1775–1776)

In the autumn of 1775, as the American Revolutionary War spread northward, two American armies converged on Quebec. The main force, under General Philip Schuyler and later Richard Montgomery, advanced via Lake Champlain and the Richelieu River, capturing Fort St. John and Montreal. A second force, under Colonel Benedict Arnold, marched through the wilderness of Maine — a harrowing journey through swamps and forests that reduced his 1,100 men to barely 600.

The two armies met before Quebec in December 1775. The American force, ragged, half-starved, and suffering from smallpox, numbered perhaps 1,200 effectives. The British garrison, commanded by Governor Guy Carleton, was scarcely more numerous — a mix of regulars, Canadian militia, and sailors from the few ships that had wintered in the river.

On the night of 31 December 1775, in a driving snowstorm, the Americans launched their assault. Montgomery led the attack on the Lower Town from the west; Arnold attacked from the north. Montgomery was killed by a cannon shot at the head of his column; Arnold was wounded in the leg. The attacks stalled in the narrow streets of the Lower Town, where the American columns were cut to pieces by grape-shot and musket fire from the barricaded houses.

The survivors, now commanded by Arnold, maintained a blockade of Quebec through the winter. But the garrison held firm, and when British reinforcements arrived by river in May 1776 — the vanguard of a fleet of thirty ships — the Americans withdrew in disorder. Carleton pursued them up the Richelieu, inflicting a final defeat at Trois-Rivières.

The defence of Quebec preserved Canada for the British Crown. Had Quebec fallen in 1775, the American Revolution might have achieved what the French and Spanish empires had not — the expulsion of British power from the St. Lawrence.

Legacy

Quebec at the end of the 18th century was a transformed city — British in government and garrison, French in language and faith, caught between two worlds. It had been the stage for the most dramatic episode of the Seven Years’ War, the death of two generals in a single morning, and the deliverance of a continent. In the generations that followed, the image of Wolfe expiring in the arms of victory on the Plains of Abraham would become the founding myth of British Canada — and the wound from which French Canada’s long memory of conquest would never fully heal.
