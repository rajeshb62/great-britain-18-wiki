Robert Clive, 1st Baron Clive

Robert Clive — later Baron Clive of Plassey (29 September 1725 – 22 November 1774) — was the soldier and statesman who laid the foundation of British rule in India. Known as “Clive of India” , he won the decisive Battle of Plassey in 1757, became the effective ruler of Bengal through the acquisition of the Diwani in 1765, and died by suicide under the weight of parliamentary persecution. No single figure did more to transform the East India Company from a trading corporation into the paramount power on the Indian subcontinent.

Early Life and Arrival in India

Clive was born at Styche, the family estate in Shropshire, into a modest gentry family of modest means but ancient lineage. His father, Richard Clive, was a lawyer of limited success; his mother, Rebecca, was the daughter of a Manchester merchant. The boy was restless, headstrong, and quarrelsome — the leader of a gang of local children who terrorised the town of Market Drayton. Sent to a succession of schools (Merchant Taylors’ among them), he proved an indifferent scholar. At the age of eighteen, in 1743, he was packed off to Madras as a “writer” — a clerk — in the Company’s civil service.

India broke him at first. The climate, the isolation, the tedium of ledgers in a sweltering counting-house — he sank into a profound depression. Twice he attempted suicide, and twice the pistol failed to fire. His friends, disarmed by the story, took it as an omen: the hand of Providence had preserved him for greater things. When war with France broke out in the Carnatic, Clive exchanged the quill for the sword and received a commission as ensign in the Company’s European infantry.

Arcot (1751)

The Carnatic — the coastal region of southeastern India — had become the cockpit of Anglo-French rivalry. The French governor Dupleix had placed his own candidate on the throne of the Carnatic, and the British candidate, Mohammed Ali, was besieged in Trichinopoly. Desperate for a diversion, the British command authorised the young Captain Clive — then twenty-six years old — to strike at Arcot, the capital of the Carnatic, with a force of barely 200 Europeans and 300 sepoys.

Clive marched on Arcot through the monsoon rains, arriving on 31 August 1751. The garrison, taken utterly by surprise, fled. Clive occupied the fortress without firing a shot.

The French response was immediate. An army of 10,000 — including 150 French soldiers and 4,000 well-armed sepoys, supplemented by crowds of irregulars — surrounded the fort. Clive had provisions for sixty days and a garrison reduced by sickness to 120 Europeans and 200 sepoys. For fifty days he held out. His sorties were devastating: he led a raid against the enemy’s guns in the dead of night, spiking the cannon and driving the besiegers back in confusion. His sepoys, awed by his calmness under fire, fought with a devotion that would become legendary. When the relief column under Colonel Kirkpatrick finally arrived, Clive sallied forth and routed the besieging army in the open field.

The defence of Arcot made Clive’s reputation overnight. It was the first demonstration that European discipline and Indian troops, welded together by determined leadership, could defeat vastly superior numbers. It also broke the French grip on the Carnatic. Over the following months Clive fought a brilliant campaign of manoeuvre and assault — the Battle of Arni, the capture of Covelong and Chingleput — that reduced the French influence in southern India to ruins.

Return to England and Second Indian Voyage

Clive returned to England in 1753, a hero at twenty-eight. He was feted by the Company, painted by Reynolds, and courted by politicians. But the money was insufficient for his ambitions, and in 1755 he sailed again for India — this time as Governor of Fort St. David, with the rank of lieutenant-colonel in the King’s army.

Before he could take up his post, events in Bengal overtook him.

The Black Hole and the Fall of Calcutta

In June 1756, Siraj-ud-Daulah, the young and volatile Nawab of Bengal, descended upon Calcutta. The British settlement, weakly garrisoned and ill-commanded, fell in a matter of hours. The notorious incident of the Black Hole of Calcutta — in which 146 European prisoners were crammed into a tiny dungeon overnight, from which only twenty-three emerged alive — became the casus belli for the Company’s revenge.

Clive was dispatched north with a small force. With Admiral Charles Watson commanding the naval squadron, Clive retook Calcutta in January 1757. The Nawab, shaken, concluded a treaty but immediately began intriguing with the French at Chandernagore. Clive destroyed the French settlement in March 1757, storming its fortifications in a model amphibious operation.

The Battle of Plassey (1757)

Siraj-ud-Daulah now resolved to crush the British once and for all. Clive, with characteristic boldness, had already begun secret negotiations with the Nawab’s disaffected commanders — most importantly Mir Jafar, the commander-in-chief of the Bengal army, whom he promised the throne. The conspiracy was a masterpiece of intelligence and duplicity. Clive knew every weakness in the Nawab’s court; the Nawab knew nothing of Clive’s plans.

On 23 June 1757, the two armies met at Plassey, a mango grove on the banks of the Bhagirathi River. Clive had 900 European soldiers, 2,100 sepoys, and eight field guns — about 3,000 men in total. Siraj-ud-Daulah commanded perhaps 50,000 men, with 53 French-manned cannon. The disparity was absurd.

The battle began with an artillery duel. The French guns were well served, and Clive’s men took heavy casualties from the opening salvo. Clive ordered his troops to shelter behind the high bank of a tank (reservoir) while the cannonade thundered overhead. For hours the two sides exchanged fire, neither willing to advance. Then, as the monsoon rains swept across the field, the Nawab’s army began to shift. Mir Jafar, true to his bargain, held his division immobile. The Nawab, suddenly suspicious of treachery, ordered a general retreat.

Clive seized the moment. He advanced with his whole force, crossing the tank and deploying in the open. The sepoys pressed forward with the bayonet; the European infantry poured volleys into the crowded masses of the Nawab’s army. Within an hour the battle was over. Siraj-ud-Daulah fled on a camel, was captured by Mir Jafar’s men, and was executed shortly after. Clive had won Bengal with fewer than a hundred casualties.

Plassey stands as one of the most consequential battles in world history. It placed the richest province of India under British control — its revenues, its trade, its strategic position — and established the East India Company as a territorial power. The Company’s agents would never again be mere merchants.

The Settlements of Bengal

Clive spent the next three years consolidating the victory. Mir Jafar was installed as Nawab, but the real authority was Clive’s. The Company’s treasury was replenished with the Nawab’s gold; the French were expelled from Bengal; the Northern Circars were occupied by Colonel Forde’s expedition. When the Dutch sent an armament to Bengal in 1759, hoping to restore their own influence, Clive destroyed it at the Battle of Chinsurah — a victory so complete that the Dutch, like the French and the Nawab before them, sued for terms.

In 1760, Clive returned to England. He was thirty-four, the richest man in Britain, with an estate worth £40,000 a year and a reputation as the conqueror of India. He was created Baron Clive of Plassey in the Peerage of Ireland, purchased vast estates in Shropshire, and entered Parliament.

The Diwani (1765)

But the Company’s affairs in Bengal deteriorated catastrophically in his absence. A succession of weak governors, the corruption of the Company’s servants, and the mounting debts of the Nawabs brought Bengal to the brink of anarchy. Clive was recalled in 1764 to rescue the Company from its own incompetence.

He arrived in Bengal in May 1765 to find the civil service rotten with private trade, the army on the verge of mutiny, and the treasury empty. His solution was audacious. He negotiated a treaty with the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, who was then a pensioner of the Nawab of Oudh. By the terms of this arrangement — the grant of the Diwani — the East India Company was appointed the revenue administrator of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. The Nawab of Bengal remained nominally sovereign, but the Company now collected all taxes, administered justice, and controlled the treasury.

The Diwani transformed the nature of British power in India. Overnight, the Company ceased to be a trading corporation and became the de facto ruler of some twenty million people. The revenue from Bengal — £3 million annually at its peak — financed the Company’s wars, its administration, and the fortunes of its servants.

The White Mutiny

Clive’s second administration was a war against corruption and indiscipline. The Company’s officers, accustomed to enriching themselves through private trade, resented his reforms. The most serious crisis came in 1766, when Clive ordered the reduction of the “double batta” — the field allowance paid to officers serving away from their headquarters. Nearly two hundred officers of the Bengal army — virtually the entire officer corps — resigned their commissions in protest.

Clive accepted the resignations. He faced down the mutineers with a display of ice-cold resolve, promoting NCOs to fill the vacancies and issuing orders that any officer who persisted in his resignation would be barred from the service for life. The mutiny collapsed. The ringleaders were dismissed, the rest returned to their duties, and the principle of Company authority over its own military establishment was permanently established.

Parliamentary Inquiries and Death

Clive returned to England for the last time in 1767, his health shattered by years of tropical service. He spent his remaining years defending his reputation. In 1772, a parliamentary inquiry into the affairs of the East India Company — driven by the opposition, by Clive’s political enemies at the India House, and by the genuine evidence of corruption among Company servants — singled him out for attack.

The charges were grave: that Clive had amassed an immense fortune through the oppression of Indians, that he had misappropriated Company funds, that he had used his power for private enrichment. Clive faced the House of Commons on 21 May 1773 and delivered a defence that ranks among the great orations of the eighteenth century.

“I stand astonished at my own moderation,” he told the House, pointing to the conquests he had given Britain without asking for reward. He reminded them that the revenues of Bengal — the richest prize ever won by British arms — flowed into the Company’s treasury, not his own. His jaghir, he proved, had been granted by the Mughal Emperor, not extorted from the Company.

The House voted to acquit him. But the ordeal destroyed him. Wracked by depression, addicted to opium, haunted by the accusations of rapacity that would follow his name for centuries, Robert Clive took his own life on 22 November 1774 at his house in Berkeley Square, London. He was forty-nine years old.

Character and Legacy

Clive was a man of furious contradictions. He was capable of extraordinary vision — his letter to William Pitt the Elder in 1759, proposing that the Crown assume the government of the Indian territories, anticipated the British Raj by a century — yet he was also capable of breathtaking duplicity, as in the conspiracy against Siraj-ud-Daulah. He was physically brave beyond measure, yet emotionally fragile, subject to deep depressions that his contemporaries called “the blue devils.”

His legacy is the foundation of British India. The Diwani of Bengal was the kernel from which the entire British Empire in Asia grew. His enemies — and there were many — accused him of plunder and despotism, and the charge carries truth. Clive enriched himself on a scale that shocked even his own age. But he also laid the administrative and military framework that made the Company’s rule sustainable, and he did so through acts of personal courage and strategic genius that his contemporaries rightly compared to the greatest captains of antiquity.

“I stand astonished at my own moderation.”
