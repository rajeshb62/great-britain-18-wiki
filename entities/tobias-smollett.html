Tobias Smollett

Tobias George Smollett (19 March 1721 – 17 September 1771) was a Scottish novelist, surgeon, historian, and journalist — one of the most versatile and combative literary figures of mid-eighteenth-century Britain. He is remembered today primarily as a novelist, author of The Adventures of Roderick Random (1748), The Adventures of Peregrine Pickle (1751), and The Expedition of Humphry Clinker (1771), but in his own time he was equally well known — and far more lucrative — as the author of A Complete History of England (1757–1758, 4 vols., later expanded to 11 vols.), a sweeping general history that became one of the most widely read works of British history in the later eighteenth century.

Smollett’s History covers the entire span of English history from the Roman invasion to the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748). Volume X, the portion held in this wiki, covers from the middle of Queen Anne’s reign through the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (c. 1708–1748), provided in the thoroughgoing synthesis of political, military, diplomatic, and social narrative that was his trademark. As the first non-naval general history source in this wiki, Smollett’s volume provides the essential political context for the specialised naval histories of John Campbell and the detailed parliamentary reporting of William Belsham.

Life and Career

Early Life and Medical Training (1721–1744)

Smollett was born at Dalquhurn, Dunbartonshire, Scotland, the grandson of Sir James Smollett of Bonhill, a Scottish judge. He was educated at Dumbarton Grammar School and the University of Glasgow, where he studied medicine — a profession that would give him the title “M.D.” he proudly affixed to his histories. His medical training was practical rather than academic: he apprenticed to a Glasgow surgeon, learning anatomy, pharmacy, and the treatment of wounds.

In 1739, at the age of eighteen, Smollett left Scotland for London, carrying with him a tragedy, The Regicide, which he vainly hoped to have produced on the stage. Its rejection began a lifelong mistrust of the London literary establishment. Unable to find work as a playwright, he secured a surgeon’s position on HMS Chichester and sailed for the West Indies in 1741.

Naval Service and Literary Apprenticeship (1741–1748)

Smollett’s service as a surgeon’s mate in the naval expedition against the Spanish possessions in the West Indies — the disastrous Cartagena expedition of 1741 — gave him firsthand experience of the brutality, incompetence, and disease that characterised eighteenth-century warfare. He was present at the siege of Cartagena under Admiral Edward Vernon and General Thomas Wentworth, an operation so mismanaged that thousands died of yellow fever, dysentery, and starvation without ever engaging the enemy effectively. The experience marked him for life and supplied the material for his first novel.

Returning to London, Smollett established a surgical practice but found medicine both insufficiently lucrative and insufficiently stimulating. He turned to writing, producing a translation of Gil Blas (1748) and, more importantly, his first novel, The Adventures of Roderick Random (1748), a picaresque narrative loosely based on his own experiences at sea and in London. The novel was an immediate success and established his reputation as a writer of vigorous, coarse-grained, often brutal realism.

The Novelist (1748–1757)

The decade from 1748 to 1757 was Smollett’s great period as a novelist. He published The Adventures of Peregrine Pickle (1751), his most ambitious novel — a vast, sprawling, Rabelaisian work that moves from Bath to London to France, filled with grotesque characters, scatological humour, and episodes of extraordinary savagery and tenderness. It was followed by The Adventures of Ferdinand Count Fathom (1753), a darker and more experimental novel that attempted to anatomise villainy from the inside, and The Life and Adventures of Sir Launcelot Greaves (1760–1762), a periodical novel that reflects his growing engagement with political satire.

Smollett’s novels are remarkable for their descriptive energy, their command of vernacular dialogue, and their unflinching portrayal of the violence and squalor of eighteenth-century life. They are also marked by a profound misanthropy: Smollett’s heroes — Roderick Random, Peregrine Pickle — are not paragons of virtue but rogues, bullies, and opportunists who survive by their wits in a world that is fundamentally corrupt. The moral landscape of his fiction is one in which kindness is rare, cruelty is routine, and the only sane response is a defensive cynicism tempered by occasional fits of generosity.

A Complete History of England (1757–1758)

The turn to history was partly financial — by the late 1750s Smollett was deep in debt, partly from supporting an extravagant household and partly from the collapse of his speculative scheme to purchase a Jamaican plantation. The Complete History of England was a commercial venture, written to a publisher’s commission and produced at extraordinary speed. The first four volumes, covering from the Roman invasion to the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle, were published in 1757–1758, and Smollett later expanded them to eleven volumes with continuations.

The History was written from the secondary sources available in the mid-eighteenth century — principally the works of Gilbert Burnet, Paul de Rapin-Thoyras, and Laurence Echard — but Smollett brought to it the narrative skill of a novelist and the partisan energy of a political journalist. His account of the Stuart reigns and the Revolution of 1688 is notably more critical of the Whig settlement than Burnet’s; Smollett was a Tory in sympathy, suspicious of the commercial and financial interests that had triumphed after 1688, and more sympathetic to the Jacobite cause than any respectable historian of the period would have been expected to be.

The History was a commercial success — it went through three editions by 1759 and remained a standard work for decades — but it was fiercely attacked by Whig reviewers for its alleged Jacobite sympathies. Smollett defended himself in the preface to later editions, claiming that he had written “without favour or affection” and that his critics were merely partisans who could not bear to see their heroes subjected to impartial scrutiny.

Political Journalism: The Briton (1762–1763)

In 1762, Smollett was recruited by the ministry of the Earl of Bute — the first Scottish prime minister and a fellow Scot — to edit The Briton, a weekly newspaper defending the government against the virulent attacks of John Wilkes’s North Briton and the London radical opposition. The newspaper was a failure as a political instrument — Wilkes’s North Briton was far more effective — but it shows Smollett at the height of his powers as a polemicist, hurling invective against the “Sons of Sedition” who threatened, in his view, the stability of the state.

The collapse of the Bute ministry in 1763 left Smollett exposed to the retaliation of the victorious Whigs. The Briton was discontinued, and Smollett found himself vilified in the press as a hireling Scot and a ministerial puppet. The bitterness of this experience drove him into a deepening melancholy and hastened his final departure from England.

Later Years, Travels, and Death (1763–1771)

Smollett’s health, never robust after the Cartagena fever, deteriorated in the 1760s. He travelled through France and Italy in 1763–1765, seeking a warmer climate and a respite from the political wars. His Travels through France and Italy (1766) is one of the most cantankerous travel books in the language — a sustained complaint about foreign food, foreign religion, foreign plumbing, and the universal dishonesty of foreign innkeepers. Its bad temper is legendary; Laurence Sterne satirised it in A Sentimental Journey (1768), presenting Smollett (thinly disguised as “Smelfungus”) as the type of the traveller who sees nothing but what is wrong.

His final years were spent at Leghorn (Livorno), Italy, where he had moved to escape the English climate and his creditors. It was there, in 1771, that he completed his masterpiece, The Expedition of Humphry Clinker — a novel of letters that is, on its surface, a comic travelogue of a Welsh family’s journey through England and Scotland, and, beneath its surface, a wise, humane, and deeply forgiving meditation on the varieties of human character. It is the least misanthropic of Smollett’s novels and the one that has worn best.

He died at Leghorn on 17 September 1771, at the age of fifty, and was buried in the English cemetery there.

A Complete History of England — Volume X

Volume X of the Complete History (third edition, 1759) covers from the middle of Queen Anne’s reign through the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748). It contains two books:

Book Eighth — Queen Anne (c. 1708–1714):

  • The siege of Lille and the Battle of Oudenarde (1708)
  • The Battle of Malplaquet (1709) — the bloodiest engagement of the war
  • The Sacheverell trial and the fall of the Whig ministry (1710)
  • The secret negotiations for the Peace of Utrecht
  • The death of Queen Anne and the Hanoverian succession

Book Ninth — From the Death of Anne to the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1714–1748):

  • The Jacobite rising of 1715 — the Earl of Mar’s rebellion, Sheriffmuir, Preston
  • The Septennial Act (1716)
  • The South Sea Bubble (1720) and its aftermath
  • The Walpole ministry and the consolidation of Whig power
  • The War of Jenkins’ Ear (1739) — Porto Bello, Cartagena
  • The War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748)
  • The Battle of Dettingen (1743) — George II’s last battle
  • The Jacobite rising of 1745 — Culloden and the suppression
  • The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)

Smollett’s narrative in this volume is the general history that contextualises the specialised naval accounts of John Campbell and the parliamentary reporting of William Belsham. His account of the South Sea Bubble, the Walpole system, and the Jacobite risings is particularly valuable for its integration of political, economic, and military events into a single continuous narrative.

Historiographical Significance

Smollett occupies an awkward position in the historiography of eighteenth-century Britain. He was a working historian who wrote for money, not for posterity, and his History was produced under conditions — speed, commercial pressure, reliance on secondary sources — that would disqualify it in the eyes of modern academic historians. Yet his History was widely read and respected in its own time, and it contributed to shaping the historical consciousness of the generation that lived through the Seven Years’ War, the American Revolution, and the French Revolutionary Wars.

His Tory sympathies — his willingness to see the Stuart case, the Jacobite perspective, and the costs of the Whig settlement — gave his History a critical edge absent from the Whig orthodoxy of Burnet or the later Whig historians. He was among the first to write English history from a British rather than an English perspective, attentive to the distinct experiences of Scotland and Ireland within the composite monarchy. And his narrative skill — the novelist’s eye for character, motive, and dramatic incident — made his History readable in a way that few of his contemporaries’ works were.

Relationships

  • Gilbert Burnet — Smollett used Burnet’s History of His Own Time as a source but took a significantly more critical view of the Glorious Revolution and the Whig settlement
  • John Campbell — fellow Scottish historian; their works (Smollett’s general history and Campbell’s naval history) are complementary sources in this wiki, together providing the political and maritime dimensions of early eighteenth-century British history
  • William Belsham — Smollett’s History ends at 1748, where Belsham’s more detailed Whig narrative picks up (his History of Great Britain covers 1688–1802); the two historians represent different generations and different political sensibilities
  • John Wilkes — Smollett’s antagonist in the newspaper wars of 1762–1763; the clash of The Briton and the North Briton was a defining moment in the development of the eighteenth-century radical press
  • John Stuart, 3rd Earl of Bute — Smollett’s patron during his brief career as a government journalist; the connection damaged Smollett’s literary reputation in Whig circles
  • Laurence Sterne — parodied Smollett’s Travels in A Sentimental Journey, the two novelists representing opposing poles of eighteenth-century sensibility
