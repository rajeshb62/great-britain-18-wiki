Horatio Nelson, 1st Viscount Nelson (1758–1805)

Horatio Nelson was the most brilliant and charismatic naval commander in British history — a man whose intuitive grasp of tactics, ferocious aggression, and almost mystical connection with the men under his command transformed him into a national demigod. His three great victories — the Battle of the Nile, the Battle of Copenhagen, and the Battle of Trafalgar — destroyed the naval power of Revolutionary and Napoleonic France and established British maritime supremacy for a century. His death at the moment of his greatest triumph fixed him forever as Britain’s greatest martial hero.

Early Career

Nelson was born at Burnham Thorpe in Norfolk, the son of a country clergyman. He entered the navy at the age of twelve, joining the Raisonnable under his uncle Captain Maurice Suckling. By twenty he was a lieutenant; by twenty-one, when the American Revolutionary War broke out, he was a post-captain. He commanded the frigate Hinchinbrook in the disastrous San Juan expedition of 1780, where the climate nearly killed him — the first of many illnesses and wounds that would punctuate his career.

During the peace that followed the American war, Nelson lived on half-pay at Burnham Thorpe, and was nearly forgotten by the Admiralty. Only the outbreak of the French Revolutionary Wars in 1793 brought him back to active service.

The Mediterranean and Cape St. Vincent

In 1793, Nelson was given command of the sixty-four-gun Agamemnon and sent to the Mediterranean under Lord Hood. He distinguished himself in the occupation of Toulon and later in the reduction of Corsica, where he was wounded at the siege of Calvi — a splinter of stone striking his right eye, blinding it permanently. The eye itself remained, but its sight was gone.

His great breakthrough came on the 14th of February, 1797, at the Battle of Cape St. Vincent under John Jervis (later Earl St. Vincent). The Spanish fleet of twenty-seven sail of the line outnumbered Jervis’s fifteen. Nelson, commanding the Captain at the rear of the British line, saw that the Spanish were about to escape through a gap in the formation. Without waiting for orders, he wore out of the line and threw his ship across the enemy’s path, engaging the great Spanish flagship Santissima Trinidad and boarding the San Nicolas and the San Josef in a feat of personal heroism celebrated throughout the fleet. Jervis rewarded him with a knighthood and a colonelcy of Marines. Nelson was promoted to rear-admiral.

The Battle of the Nile

In May 1798, St. Vincent detached Nelson with a squadron of three ships of the line and two frigates to intercept the French fleet that had sailed from Toulon under the command of Admiral Brueys. The French expedition, carrying Bonaparte’s invasion army, had vanished into the Mediterranean. Nelson’s chase became one of the most extraordinary hunts in naval history.

From the 9th of May, when he sailed from Gibraltar, to the 4th of June, Nelson cruised in search. He put into Corsica: no intelligence. Into Naples: none. Into Sicily: at last he learned that the French had been seen off Malta. He pressed on — but found they had departed, steering south-east. Concluding they were bound for Egypt, he made directly for Alexandria. There, on his arrival, he had “the mortification to find that not a single ship had made its appearance there.” He retraced his course to Sicily; at Syracuse he completed wood and water and set out again, eastward. At last, off the coast of the Morea, the Turkish governor of Coron told him the French had been seen off Candia a month earlier, steering for Alexandria. Nelson turned back.

On the 1st of August, 1798, the Pharos lighthouse off Alexandria was descried. As the fleet approached, the lookouts on the Alexander and Leander made the signal: ships of war to the eastward. Nelson, with that “promptitude of decision which is the characteristic of a great mind,” formed his plan in an instant. The French were anchored in Aboukir Bay in a line of battle extending nearly north-west and south-east, their heads to the sea. They had placed themselves so close to the shore that they believed the space between their van and the shoals was too narrow for any ship to pass.

Nelson saw that it was not.

He ordered his fleet to prepare to anchor by the stern — a technique that would allow his ships to swing at anchor and bring their broadsides to bear while remaining stationary. The Goliah, leading the British van, passed round the bow of the enemy’s leading ship, Le Guerrier, and anchored inside the French line. Four others followed, and the battle was joined as the sun was setting.

The punishment was annihilating. Within fifteen minutes the van ship of the enemy was dismasted and reduced to a wreck. Nelson himself was struck on the head by a piece of langridge shot; a flap of skin fell over his one good eye, plunging him into total darkness. Believing his wound mortal, he refused to let the surgeon attend to him before the other wounded had been treated. “I will take my turn with my brave fellows,” he said.

The climax came just after nine o’clock, when the French flagship L’Orient, of 120 guns, caught fire. Brueys was already dead — “he had received three wounds, yet would not leave his post; a fourth cut him almost in two.” The flames ran through the ship. “About ten o’clock the ship blew up. This tremendous explosion was followed by a silence not less awful: the firing immediately ceased on both sides; and the first sound which broke the silence, was the dash of her shattered masts and yards, falling into the water from the vast height to which they had been exploded.”

Of thirteen French ships of the line, nine were taken and two burnt. Four frigates were burnt or sunk. The British loss was 895 killed and wounded; the French lost 3,105 prisoners and an estimated 5,225 dead. Only two ships of the line and two frigates escaped. “Victory,” said Nelson, “is not a name strong enough for such a scene; he called it a conquest.

The battle of the Nile was perhaps the most complete naval victory of the age of sail. It stranded Bonaparte’s army in Egypt, shattered French naval power in the Mediterranean, and opened the way for the formation of the Second Coalition. Nelson was created Baron Nelson of the Nile.

The Siege of Acre and the Mediterranean

In 1799, Nelson returned to the theatre to support the Neapolitan kingdom against the French, and came to the aid of Sir Sydney Smith at the siege of Acre, though Smith’s own defence of the town would ultimately turn Bonaparte back from Syria. Nelson’s involvement in the chaotic politics of Naples — where he sanctioned the execution of the captured Republican admirals — remains the most controversial episode of his career, and casts a shadow over his reputation that the hagiographers have never entirely dispelled.

He was created Viscount Nelson in 1801 for his Mediterranean services.

The Battle of Copenhagen

By 1801, the situation in the Baltic had become critical. Tsar Paul I of Russia had revived the Armed Neutrality of the North — a league of Russia, Sweden, Denmark, and Prussia, united in resistance to the British claim to search neutral shipping for enemy contraband. The coalition threatened to cut off the Baltic supplies of timber, hemp, flax, and tar essential to the British navy.

A fleet under Admiral Sir Hyde Parker was dispatched to break the League. Nelson was appointed second-in-command with a separate squadron. Parker was cautious and elderly; Nelson was impatient and aggressive. On the 2nd of April, 1801, Nelson led his squadron of twelve ships of the line against the Danish fleet moored in a crescent off Copenhagen, protected by the formidable Trekroner fortress.

The battle was fought in shoal water and under heavy fire from both ships and shore batteries. After three hours of the most savage fighting, with several of Nelson’s ships grounded and the issue still in doubt, Parker hoisted the signal for retreat — the famous signal number 39, by the then universally understood code.

Nelson, standing on the quarterdeck of his flagship Elephant, raised his telescope to his blind eye. “I have only one eye,” he said to Captain Foley. “I have a right to be blind sometimes.” He then turned to his signal lieutenant: “You know, Foley, I really do not see the signal.” And to his flag-captain: “Leave off action? Now damn me if I do!”

He continued the action. Within an hour, the heavier Danish ships had struck, the Danish fire slackened, and Nelson was able to open negotiations for an armistice, threatening to burn the captured Danish ships if the Danes did not fire on his boats as he took possession. Copenhagen fell; the Armed Neutrality dissolved; Tsar Paul was assassinated within weeks and his successor Alexander reversed the League’s policies. The Baltic was secured.

The Blockade of Toulon and the Pursuit to Trafalgar

In 1803, war with France resumed after the brief Peace of Amiens. Nelson was appointed to command the Mediterranean fleet, hoisting his flag in the Victory. For nearly two years he blockaded the French fleet at Toulon, his ships rarely leaving the coast for more than a few days at a time. It was one of the most gruelling commands in naval history, and Nelson’s health suffered severely.

In the spring of 1805, Admiral Villeneuve escaped from Toulon with the combined French and Spanish fleet, hoping to draw Nelson to the West Indies and then double back to the Channel, where Bonaparte’s invasion flotilla awaited. Nelson pursued across the Atlantic, missed Villeneuve by a few days at the West Indies, and raced back to England. Villeneuve, instead of making for the Channel, put into Cadiz.

Nelson followed him there, and blockaded the port.

The Battle of Trafalgar

On the 21st of October, 1805, Villeneuve came out, his fleet of thirty-three ships of the line — the finest concentration of naval power the two Bourbon kingdoms could assemble — steering south-east from Cadiz. Nelson, with twenty-seven ships of the line, was waiting.

His plan was the most daring of his career. Instead of forming a single line of battle and engaging the enemy line for line — the orthodox method — Nelson divided his fleet into two columns, each to sail directly into the heart of the enemy’s formation. The weather column, under his command, would cut the enemy’s line about its centre; the lee column, under Collingwood, would attack the rear. The object was not to exchange broadsides at a distance but to break the enemy’s formation into a confused melee in which British superiority in gunnery and seamanship would tell decisively.

At noon, as the two fleets closed, Nelson hoisted his famous signal: “England expects that every man will do his duty.”

The Victory led the weather column into the enemy’s centre. She came under concentrated fire from the combined fleet and was raked repeatedly as she approached; but she did not fire a shot until she was within forty yards of Villeneuve’s Bucentaure. Then she unleashed a broadside that raked the French flagship from stem to stern, killing and wounding some two hundred men in a single discharge.

The battle became a chaos of individual engagements. The Victory grappled with the Redoutable, and the two ships lay locked together. From the mizzentop of the Redoutable, a marksman — according to tradition, a sharpshooter named Robert Guillemard — took aim at Nelson, who was walking the quarterdeck in his full dress uniform, the stars of his orders glittering on his breast. The bullet struck him in the left shoulder, passed through his lung, and lodged in his spine.

Nelson fell. “They have done for me at last, Hardy,” he said to his flag-captain. He was carried below to the cockpit, where he lay in agony for three hours, his life ebbing away. The surgeon could do nothing.

Shortly after four o’clock, Hardy came down to report that the battle was won: fourteen or fifteen enemy ships had struck. “I hope none of our ships have struck, Hardy,” Nelson said. “No, my Lord, there is no fear of that.” Nelson smiled. “I am a dead man, Hardy. I am going fast: it will be all over with me soon.” He sent his last message to Lady Hamilton and to the nation, and then repeated, over and over: “Thank God, I have done my duty.”

He died at half-past four. The victory was the most complete in naval history: eighteen enemy ships of the line were captured or destroyed — among them the Santissima Trinidad, the largest ship in the world — and not a single British vessel was lost. British naval supremacy was established for a century.

Legacy

Nelson was the first admiral to break decisively with the rigid line-of-battle tactics that had governed fleet actions for a hundred years. His genius lay in his ability to read a battle as it developed, to trust his subordinates, and to inspire a level of devotion in his men that no other commander of the age could match. He was buried in St. Paul’s Cathedral on the 9th of January, 1806, in a state funeral that dwarfed any previous national tribute.

His signal — “England expects that every man will do his duty” — remains the most famous words ever spoken by a British commander, a phrase that captured the essence of his leadership: the belief that every sailor under his command was as important as the admiral himself, and that together they formed an instrument of national destiny.

Nelson’s column in Trafalgar Square, completed in 1843, stands as the physical embodiment of a cult that has no parallel in British history — the cult of a man who, as Southey wrote, possessed “the most perfect simplicity of character” combined with “the most splendid professional abilities,” and who died, as he had always wished, “in battle and in victory.”
