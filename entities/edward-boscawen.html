Edward Boscawen (1711–1761)

Edward Boscawen — known to the fleet as “Old Dreadnought” — was the most aggressively fearless of Britain’s mid-century admirals. He led from the front, pressed his attacks to the very muzzle of the enemy’s guns, and combined the ferocity of a fighting seaman with the humanitarian instincts of a reforming commander. His victories at Louisbourg and the Battle of Lagos were decisive elements in the annus mirabilis of 1759.

Family and Early Service

Boscawen was born in 1711 into the ancient Cornish family of Boscawen Rose, whose lineage could be traced back to the reign of King John. His family’s standing, however, was less the driver of his career than his own merits — his various promotions were won through professional service, not family influence.

He first distinguished himself in the disastrous Cartagena expedition of 1741, during the War of Jenkins’ Ear. On the night of the 19th of March, Boscawen — then commanding the Shoreham — led a party of seamen against the Spanish battery at Baru. The boats rowed silently toward the shore, landing a mile to leeward of their objective to avoid detection. Boscawen leaped out first, and his men followed. They advanced through a narrow channel between two reefs of rocks and found themselves, to their surprise, within the very muzzle of a five-gun battery of which they had been completely ignorant. The Spanish opened a heavy fire, but Boscawen, distinguished always for presence of mind, rushed forward through the embrasures. His men drove the enemy from their guns before a second discharge could be fired. He subsequently stormed the principal battery, spiked the guns, tore up platforms, destroyed carriages, guard houses, and magazines, and withdrew his men with little loss — only to return days later and carry the position a second time after the Spanish had repaired the defences, this time with the Shoreham, Princess Amelia, and Lichfield anchoring to bring their broadsides to bear.

After the death of Lord Aubrey Beauclerk at the castle of Bocca Chica, Boscawen was given command of the Prince Frederic of seventy guns. He superintended the demolition of the forts of Porto Bello and, in May 1742, returned to England, anchoring at St. Helen’s after a nine-week passage from Jamaica.

Channel Service and Cape Finisterre

Between 1745 and 1747, Boscawen commanded the Dreadnought of sixty guns on Channel cruises, capturing the French frigate Merdea. In 1746, as captain of the Royal Sovereign (guard ship at the Nore), he was charged with the extraordinary duty of fitting out and inspecting the small cruisers hastily assembled for the campaign against the Jacobite rebellion in Scotland.

He was then appointed to the Namur, a seventy-four-gun ship rebuilt from a former ninety-gun vessel. At the Battle of Cape Finisterre on the 3rd of May, 1747 — fought under George Anson and Sir Peter Warren — Boscawen played a vital role. Perceiving that the French held the weather gauge and were firing into the British ships as they struggled to form line, Boscawen crowded sail and pressed forward, deliberately drawing the enemy’s fire onto his own vessel. His Namur was among the first into action, and he harassed and delayed the French long enough for the rest of the British fleet to come up. He was severely wounded in the shoulder by a musket ball, and his conduct earned him the favourable notice of the Admiralty. On the 15th of July, 1747, he was promoted to rear-admiral of the blue.

The East Indies Command

In an appointment almost unprecedented since the reign of Charles II, Boscawen was given command not only of the fleet bound for the East Indies, but also of all the land forces employed on the expedition. The opposition at home seized on this as a political grievance — it had been the subject of abuse in the reigns of Charles II and James II — but no blame attached to Boscawen himself. The expedition sailed in 1748, and though it did not achieve decisive results against the French in Indian waters, it demonstrated the scope of his command.

Louisbourg

With the outbreak of the Seven Years’ War, Boscawen was appointed to command the fleet that would co-operate in the siege of Louisbourg, the great French fortress on Cape Breton Island that commanded the approaches to the St. Lawrence. The fleet sailed for Halifax, where it was joined by General Amherst and the army. On the 7th of June, 1758, it anchored in a bay seven miles west of Louisbourg. Boscawen landed the troops under the cover of the fleet’s guns, and the siege began. His handling of the naval elements was decisive — the Royal Navy sealed the harbour, prevented reinforcement or resupply, and delivered Amherst’s army safely to the shore. When Louisbourg surrendered, Boscawen dispatched part of his fleet with troops aboard under Lord Amherst for further operations, while he himself returned to England. The capture of Louisbourg opened the St. Lawrence to Wolfe’s expedition against Quebec the following year.

The Battle of Lagos

In 1759, Boscawen was ordered to the Mediterranean with a squadron to blockade the French fleet at Toulon. After a failed attempt to force action by attacking the French ships in the outer road — an enterprise in which the Culloden and her companions suffered heavily — Boscawen retired to Gibraltar to refit. He left frigates stationed off Malaga and near Ceuta to watch for the enemy. On the 27th of July, the Ceuta frigate signalled the appearance of fourteen French sail on the Barbary shore. The British fleet, with the same number of ships of the line, immediately put to sea.

On the 28th of August, 1759, Boscawen came up with the French fleet under Monsieur de la Clue off the coast of Portugal, near Lagos Bay. Early in the action, Boscawen’s flagship, the Namur, lost her mizzenmast, compelling him to shift his flag to the Newark. By nightfall, only the Centaur of seventy-four guns had been captured. But the next morning the French were in full flight, four ships visible standing into the shore. The Ocean ran among the breakers and was taken — though de la Clue had been landed — and was set afire. The Temeraire (seventy-four) and the Modeste (sixty-four) were brought off with little damage; the Redoubtable was destroyed. The French Mediterranean fleet was shattered.

Boscawen arrived at Spithead with his prizes on the 1st of September. For Lagos, he had broken the back of the French Mediterranean squadron, ensuring that no naval force could interfere with the expeditions gathering against Quebec and the French West Indies.

Final Years

On the 9th of December, 1759, Boscawen was appointed General of Marines, with a salary of £3,000 per annum. In January 1760, he was ordered to cruise off Quiberon Bay to watch the remnants of the French fleet that had escaped Edward Hawke’s victory. The service brought him no glory — violent weather repeatedly forced him back to port. During these months, he turned his attention to the health of his seamen, taking possession of a small island near the river Vannes which he planted with vegetables to prevent scurvy — a quiet act of humanity that speaks as well of the man as any battle.

His health failing, he retired to his newly finished seat at Hatchlands in Surrey. There he was seized with a bilious fever and died on the 10th of January, 1761. His body was carried into Cornwall and buried in the parish church of St. Michael in Penkevel. A monument designed by Robert Adam and executed by Raysbrak bears the inscription commemorating “Admiral of the blue, general of marines, Lord of the Admiralty, and one of His Majesty’s most honourable privy council” — a record of service that began at the embrasures of a Spanish battery and ended in the quiet planting of vegetables for sick sailors.

In character, Boscawen was the embodiment of the aggressive tactical school: he believed in pressing the enemy without regard for odds, and his presence of mind in action was legendary. Yet he was also a commander who cared for the comfort of his men, who thought it his duty to search neutral ships for contraband of war destined for French ports, and who conducted his affairs, even in war, with a decorum that won him the respect of his enemies.
