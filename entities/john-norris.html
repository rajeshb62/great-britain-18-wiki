Admiral of the Fleet Sir John Norris

Born: c. 1660, probably County Cork, Ireland
Died: 13 July 1749, London
Nickname: None recorded — known as the “Norwegian Admiral” for his Baltic commands

Early Life and Career

Sir John Norris was born of Irish extraction, the son of a gentleman of County Cork. Nothing is known of his childhood, but he entered the Royal Navy young and served as a lieutenant at the Battle of Beachy Head in 1690, that disastrous engagement in which the combined Anglo-Dutch fleet was defeated by the French under Admiral Tourville. The lesson of Beachy Head — that a divided command and a cautious admiral could lose a kingdom — was one Norris never forgot.

His first independent command was the Pelican fire-ship, but he soon graduated to the Sheerness, a 28-gun frigate, in which he saw active service in the Mediterranean under Admiral Russell. He accompanied the fleet that burnt the French ships at La Hougue in 1692, the climactic revenge for Beachy Head.

The Hudson’s Bay Expedition (1696)

Promoted to captain in 1693, Norris distinguished himself in command of the Hampshire during an expedition to recover the Hudson’s Bay forts from the French. The enterprise was part of the wider struggle of the Nine Years’ War, in which the fur-trading posts of the frozen north were as hotly contested as the sugar islands of the Caribbean. Norris’s conduct on the expedition earned him favourable notice and began the reputation for dogged competence that would sustain his career for half a century.

War of the Spanish Succession

During the War of the Spanish Succession, Norris commanded successively the Lyme, the Revenge, and the Torbay, serving in the Mediterranean under Sir George Rooke and Sir Cloudesley Shovel. He was present at the Battle of Vigo Bay in 1702, the spectacular destruction of the Spanish treasure fleet, and at the capture of Gibraltar in 1704 — that bold seizure of the Rock that would change the course of Mediterranean history.

He was raised to flag rank in 1709, becoming a rear-admiral. In 1710 he commanded a squadron in the Mediterranean, protecting British commerce and cooperating with the allied forces fighting in Spain. His diligence and seamanship were acknowledged by his promotion to vice-admiral of the blue in 1711.

The Baltic Commands and the Northern War

Norris’s reputation was made in the Baltic. Between 1715 and 1727, he commanded no fewer than five expeditions to the Baltic Sea during the Great Northern War, the long struggle between Sweden and a coalition of Russia, Denmark, and Poland. King George I, as Elector of Hanover, had a direct interest in the Baltic — the Duchies of Bremen and Verden, acquired from Sweden, were his German patrimony — and Norris was his chosen instrument.

In 1715, Norris sailed with a powerful squadron to protect British commerce from the depredations of Charles XII of Sweden, who had seized British ships trading to the Baltic ports and rejected all treaties of neutrality. Norris’s fleet cruised off the Swedish coast, covering the allied operations against Stralsund and Wismar, and forced Charles’s privateers to abandon their attacks on British shipping.

In 1716, he returned to the Baltic, this time in collaboration with the Russian fleet of Peter the Great. The two admirals — Norris and the Tsar himself — met off Copenhagen, and a combined Anglo-Danish-Russian fleet of formidable strength was assembled. Norris was instructed to cover the transport of troops to the Danish islands, but the enterprise was frustrated by a combination of bad weather and the growing mutual suspicion between the allies. The alliance between King George and the Tsar proved brittle; each suspected the other of territorial designs, and Norris was left to protect British interests as best he could, shuttling between Copenhagen, Reval, and the Russian squadrons with diplomatic tact as well as naval skill.

The later Baltic commands of 1717, 1719, 1721, and 1727 were increasingly concerned with the politics of the Northern peace settlement. Norris’s presence with a British fleet served as the ultimate argument at the negotiating table. The Swedes called him “the English admiral who comes every year to see if we are quiet,” and the nickname “Norwegian Admiral” stuck.

Mediterranean and Lisbon Commands

In 1734, Norris commanded a squadron sent to the Mediterranean during the War of the Polish Succession, tasked with protecting British trade and showing the flag against Spain and France. In 1739, on the outbreak of the War of Jenkins’ Ear, he was appointed Commander-in-Chief in the Channel, flying his flag in the Victory — Nelson’s future flagship — and cruising off the Spanish coast.

In 1741, failing health and advancing age seemed to have ended his active service. He was by now in his eighties, one of the oldest admirals in the history of the Royal Navy, and had served through four reigns — from William III to George II. But the old man was not yet done.

The Channel Command of 1744

In February 1744, a French invasion fleet of unprecedented size assembled at Dunkirk and Brest under the command of Admiral de Roquefeuil, carrying 15,000 troops for a landing in England in support of the Jacobite Stuart claimant. The British government was badly informed and slow to react. Norris was summoned from retirement at the age of eighty-four and given command of the Channel fleet.

It was a desperate moment. The French fleet sailed from Brest and for several days the British had no certain intelligence of its whereabouts. Norris put to sea from Spithead on 6 March 1744 with 17 ships of the line. The weather was appalling — a succession of winter gales that had already dispersed the blockading squadrons. Norris drove his fleet westward into the teeth of the storm, searching for the French.

On 8 March, he found them. The French fleet was off Dungeness, but the wind had shifted, pinning them against a lee shore. Norris, despite the heavy weather that had scattered several of his own ships, bore down with the vessels he had and forced the French commanders to abandon their enterprise. Roquefeuil, his fleet damaged and his nerve shaken, retreated back to Brest.

The “Great French Invasion Scare” of 1744 was over, and Norris had been its decisive answer. He was the oldest man in the Channel that winter, and he had outfought both the French and the elements. It was a fitting climax to sixty years of service.

The Norris Dynasty

Norris was the founder of a naval and military dynasty. His son, Captain Richard Norris, commanded a ship under his father and was later court-martialled after the action off Toulon in 1744 — a case that deeply wounded the old admiral. Another son, Admiral Harry Norris, also served with distinction. A third son, John Norris, was a major general in the army. Sir John’s brother, Admiral Sir William Norris, also served in the Baltic campaigns.

To have three sons in the service, and to be himself the longest-serving admiral of his age, was a remarkable achievement for a man of Irish gentry stock. The Norris dynasty was one of the most enduring of the 18th-century navy, though it never produced another figure of quite his stature.

Death and Character

Sir John Norris died on 13 July 1749, probably in his eighty-ninth year, at his house in London. He was buried in the church of St Mary’s, where a monument was erected to his memory.

Campbell’s biographer describes him as “a man of excellent natural parts, improved by a long course of experience,” who possessed “a steady judgment and undaunted courage.” His career was remarkable for its sheer duration — sixty years at sea or in command — and for the consistency of his performance. He was not a spectacular commander in the mould of Hawke or Anson; he never fought a Trafalgar or a Quiberon Bay. But he was the man the government turned to in a crisis, the old hand who could be trusted to get the fleet to sea and to bring it back again, whether the enemy was French, Spanish, Swedish, or the winter gales of the North Sea.

Legacy

Sir John Norris is now largely forgotten outside specialist naval history, but his career illuminates an important truth about the 18th-century navy: not every great commander achieves greatness by a single, spectacular victory. Norris’s greatness was that of longevity, reliability, and the steadiness that comes from accumulated experience. He served his country for longer than almost any admiral in its history, and when the crisis came in 1744, it was to him — an old man in his ninth decade — that his country turned.

His Baltic expeditions opened the door to British influence in Northern Europe, protecting the Hanoverian connection that was so vital to 18th-century British foreign policy. His Mediterranean commands helped establish the British presence that would culminate in Gibraltar and Minorca. And his Channel command of 1744 may well have saved England from a Jacobite restoration, though he never received full credit for it.

He is linked in the lore of the navy to John Byng — Byng commanded a ship in Norris’s fleet at the relief of Lisbon in 1735, and his own career intersects with Norris’s at many points. The contrast between the two men — Norris, the survivor who died in his bed after sixty years of service, and Byng, shot on his own quarterdeck after a single failure — is one of the most poignant in naval history.
