Charles Edward Stuart

Charles Edward Louis John Casimir Sylvester Maria Stuart (31 December 1720 – 31 January 1788) — known to history as “Bonnie Prince Charlie” or “the Young Pretender” — was the Stuart claimant to the thrones of England, Scotland, and Ireland, and the commander of the Jacobite rising of 1745. His was the last serious attempt to restore the Stuart dynasty to the British throne. The failure of that rising, culminating in the destruction of the Highland Jacobite army at Culloden, sealed the fate both of the Stuart cause and of the Highland clan system that had sustained it.

The Stuart Claim and the ‘45 Rising

Charles was the eldest son of James Francis Edward Stuart (the Old Pretender, son of the deposed James II) and of Maria Clementina Sobieska, granddaughter of the great Polish king John III Sobieski. He was born in Rome, where the exiled Stuart court had been given refuge by the Pope. From childhood he was reared on the conviction that the thrones of his ancestors were his by right, and that the Hanoverian usurper George I and his successors had no legitimate claim to the Crown.

In 1743, with France at war with Britain in the War of the Austrian Succession, Charles’s father authorised him to attempt a restoration. The French government, seeing the Jacobite cause as a useful weapon against the Hanoverian state, promised support. Charles left Rome secretly in January 1744 and joined a French invasion fleet at Dunkirk, but storms scattered the French ships and the invasion was abandoned.

Disregarding his father’s cautions and the scepticism of the European courts, Charles resolved to act alone. “I am determined,” he wrote before his departure, “to be the instrument of my own deliverance and to restore my royal father or perish in the attempt.” On 23 July 1745, with only seven companions and a small store of arms and money, he landed on the island of Eriskay in the Hebrides, at the head of Loch nan Uamh. The local chiefs were appalled by the recklessness of the enterprise — Alexander Macdonald of Sleat refused to join, telling the Prince, “Sir, you are come on a rash and desperate undertaking.” But Charles’s charm, audacity, and his promise that French support was imminent persuaded a sufficient number of Highland chieftains to raise their clans.

The March South: Prestonpans to Derby

On 19 August 1745, Charles raised his father’s standard at Glenfinnan, at the head of Loch Shiel. Within days, a Jacobite army of some 1,200 Highlanders had assembled, and the rising began in earnest. The Prince marched on Edinburgh, entered the capital without resistance on 17 September, and established a Jacobite court at Holyroodhouse. The old palace of the Stuart kings was, for six weeks, the seat of a rival British government.

On 21 September, at Prestonpans, east of Edinburgh, the Jacobite army met the government forces under Sir John Cope. The battle was over in less than fifteen minutes. The Highlanders charged with the broadsword, smashed through the government lines, and routed Cope’s army in a shattering victory. The news electrified Scotland and terrified London. The Prince, now in effective control of most of Scotland north of the Forth, held a council of war to decide the next move.

Against the advice of his Scottish commanders — who favoured consolidating their position in Scotland and waiting for French reinforcements — Charles insisted on an immediate invasion of England. On 8 November, the Jacobite army, now about 5,000 strong, crossed the border into England. They advanced through Carlisle, Manchester, and Derby, meeting no serious opposition. The government under the Duke of Newcastle was in chaos. The French had not landed, the English Jacobites had not risen, and the army of William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland was slowly assembling in the Midlands. Yet London was in panic: on 6 December, the news reached the capital that the Highlanders were at Derby, only 120 miles away.

Then, on 6 December 1745, at the Derby council of war, the Jacobite campaign reached its decisive moment. Charles’s generals — Lord George Murray in particular — insisted that the army must retreat. There were no English recruits, no French landing had materialised, and two government armies were converging to trap the Jacobites in the Midlands. Charles protested, pleaded, and raged, but the council overruled him. The army turned north. It was the turning point of the rising — the moment when the Jacobite cause passed from the brink of triumph into irreversible decline.

The Retreat and Culloden

The retreat from Derby was a masterpiece of military discipline. The Jacobite army marched north through the winter, harried by government forces but never broken. They reached Scotland, won a further victory at Falkirk Muir (17 January 1746), and laid siege to Stirling Castle. But the army was shrinking, the Highland clans were quarrelling among themselves, and the Duke of Cumberland — recalled from the continent to take command of the government forces — was advancing with a professional army stiffened with battle-hardened regulars.

The two armies met on Culloden Moor, east of Inverness, on 16 April 1746. It was a catastrophe for the Jacobites. Charles, exhausted and ill-advised, chose to fight on unsuitable ground — a flat, boggy moor that neutralised the Highland charge. The Jacobite army was outnumbered, underfed, and outgunned. Cumberland’s artillery raked the Highland lines; when the clans finally charged, they were cut down by musket and cannon fire before they could close. The battle was over in less than an hour. Perhaps 1,500 Jacobites lay dead on the moor; the government lost about 50.

Charles fled the field. The rising was finished.

The Escape and the Aftermath

The five months that followed — Charles’s flight through the Western Highlands and the Hebrides, pursued by government troops with a price of £30,000 on his head — became the stuff of romantic legend. He moved from island to island, sheltering in caves and crofts, protected by a network of loyal supporters. The most famous episode of his escape came when he was hidden by Flora MacDonald, who smuggled him across the sea to Skye disguised as an Irish spinning-maid named Betty Burke. The Prince and his protectors narrowly avoided capture on several occasions, and on every occasion the bounty was refused.

In September 1746, five months after Culloden, a French ship picked Charles up at Loch nan Uamh — the very bay where he had landed fourteen months before — and carried him to France. He never set foot on British soil again.

The aftermath of the rising was brutal. Cumberland — who would be called “Butcher Cumberland” by the Highlanders — pursued a policy of systematic pacification. The clan system was destroyed: the estates of Jacobite chiefs were forfeited, the wearing of Highland dress was prohibited, and the traditional authority of the chiefs was broken by the Heritable Jurisdictions Act (1747), which abolished the feudal courts on which the clan system depended. The Highlands were subjected to military occupation, and the government pushed through a programme of road-building, fort-construction, and disarmament designed to ensure that no rising could ever again threaten the Hanoverian state. These measures, sustained over decades, laid the legal and administrative foundations for the Highland Clearances of the later eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Exile and Decline

Charles spent the rest of his life in a long, bitter exile that destroyed him. He was expelled from France under the terms of the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) and moved restlessly through Europe — Avignon, Rome, Florence, Basel — sustained by a dwindling pension from the Spanish and French courts. He drank heavily. His behaviour became erratic and paranoid. He quarrelled with his father, alienated his few remaining supporters, and sank into a pattern of depression and alcoholism that wrecked his health and his character.

In 1772, at the age of fifty-one, he married Princess Louise of Stolberg-Gedern, a German princess forty-two years his junior. The marriage was a disaster: Charles’s drinking and his rages drove Louise into the arms of the Italian poet Vittorio Alfieri, and she left him in 1780. There were no legitimate children — a fact of dynastic significance, since Charles’s younger brother Henry, Cardinal York, was a Catholic priest who could not marry. The Jacobite succession was, in effect, a dead line.

Charles Edward Stuart died in Rome on 31 January 1788, at the age of sixty-seven. He was buried in the Basilica of St. Peter’s in the Vatican — the last royal Stuart to be laid to rest in the city of his birth and his exile. His brother Henry succeeded him as the Jacobite claimant, but the cause was already a ghost.

The Romanticisation of the Jacobite Cause

The memory of Charles Edward Stuart underwent a remarkable transformation in the decades after his death. The failure of the ‘45 removed the Jacobite cause from the sphere of practical politics and transferred it into the realm of sentiment, poetry, and nostalgia. The Bonnie Prince Charlie of popular memory — the handsome young prince who had dared everything and lost everything, who had trusted the loyalty of the Highland clansmen and been betrayed by the forces of history — became a figure of romantic tragedy rather than of political threat.

The ballads of the rising — The Skye Boat Song, Will Ye No Come Back Again?, The Bonnie Banks of Loch Lomond — were composed or popularised in the decades after Culloden, and they fixed the image of the prince as a figure of pathos and heroism. Sir Walter Scott’s novels — particularly Waverley (1814), which tells the story of a young Englishman caught up in the ‘45 — cemented the romanticisation of the Jacobite cause. The tartan-wearing, kilted Highlander of Victorian imagination, loyal to a lost cause and a vanishing way of life, was partly a product of this Jacobite nostalgia.

The reality was more complex. The Jacobite cause was not a movement for Scottish independence but a dynastic struggle for the British throne. The Highland clans who rallied to Prince Charles were motivated as much by clan loyalty, economic grievance, and anti-Hanoverian sentiment as by Stuart legitimism. And the prince himself — the central figure of the romance — was, in his later years, a tragic testament to the corrosive effects of failure: a man who had staked everything on a single throw of the dice and watched his world collapse around him.

“Bonnie Prince Charlie — the Young Pretender — whose name still stirs the heart of every Jacobite, though the cause for which he fought died on Culloden Moor.”
