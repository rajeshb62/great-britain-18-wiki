Charles Townshend

Charles Townshend (29 August 1725 – 4 September 1767) was Chancellor of the Exchequer from August 1766 until his sudden death in September 1767. He is remembered as the author of the Townshend Acts of 1767 — the duties on glass, lead, paper, paint, and tea imported into the American colonies that rekindled the imperial crisis after the repeal of the Stamp Act. Brilliant, erratic, and utterly unreliable, Townshend was known to contemporaries as “Champagne Charley” — a man whose prodigious oratorical gifts were fatally compromised by a volatile and mercurial temper.

Early Life and Rise

Charles Townshend was the second son of Charles Townshend, 3rd Viscount Townshend, and Audrey Harrison. The Townshend family had been prominent in British politics since the Revolution of 1688 — his grandfather, the 2nd Viscount Townshend, had served as Secretary of State under George I and George II. His elder brother, George, inherited the title.

He was educated at Leiden and Cambridge, and entered Parliament in 1747 as member for Great Yarmouth, a seat controlled by the Townshend interest. He quickly established himself as one of the most brilliant debaters in the House of Commons — fluent, witty, incisive, and fearless. His speeches were said to carry the House with an irresistible blend of logic, mockery, and charm.

Townshend’s early offices were minor but indicative of his rising reputation: a Lord of the Admiralty (1754), a Lord of the Treasury (1756), and Secretary at War in 1761 under Bute. He opposed the peace preliminaries with France in 1762, was dismissed, and spent two years in opposition.

The Rockingham Ministry

Townshend served as Paymaster General in the Rockingham ministry (1765–1766). This office — traditionally a source of immense private profit, since the Paymaster held large sums of public money without interest — he conspicuously declined to exploit for personal gain, following the example set by Pitt in the 1740s.

In the great debates on the repeal of the Stamp Act in 1766, Townshend took a characteristically complex position. While nominally supporting the Rockingham government’s policy of repeal coupled with the Declaratory Act, he made speeches that seemed to concede the Americans’ position on taxation — earning Pitt’s applause — while simultaneously embracing the claim of unlimited parliamentary sovereignty. His performances baffled friend and foe alike.

Chancellor of the Exchequer

When Pitt (now Lord Chatham) formed his second ministry in August 1766, Townshend was appointed Chancellor of the Exchequer. It was an appointment driven by necessity rather than choice: Pitt distrusted Townshend but could not exclude a man of his talents and connections from a ministry that desperately needed strength in the Commons.

The Chatham ministry was crippled from the start by the Earl’s catastrophic illness. Chatham suffered a complete breakdown — what contemporaries described as a nervous collapse — in the spring of 1767, withdrawing to his Somerset estate and refusing all business for months. Townshend, nominally subordinate, was left virtually unguided at the Exchequer.

The Townshend Acts

In May 1767, in what Belsham describes as “one of his vain and capricious moods,” Townshend rose in the House of Commons and boasted “that he knew how to draw a revenue from the Colonies without giving them offence.” George Grenville, watching from the opposition benches, instantly and eagerly challenged him to make good his promise. Townshend did not hesitate.

The resulting Townshend Acts imposed duties on glass, lead, paper, painters’ colours, and tea imported from Great Britain into the American colonies. The distinction between internal taxes (like the Stamp Act, now repealed) and external duties (like the traditional regulation of imperial commerce) was crucial to Townshend’s argument: duties at the port of entry were, he claimed, a legitimate exercise of Parliament’s commercial authority, not a direct tax.

Belsham describes the measure with barely concealed outrage:

“By a species of artifice unworthy of a great nation, an attempt was now made to inveigle the Americans into the payment of that revenue which could not be extorted by means more direct and unequivocal.”

The Americans were not fooled. Colonial resistance — pamphlets, boycotts, non-importation agreements — erupted with the same fury that had greeted the Stamp Act. The Townshend duties, Belsham writes, “rekindled those alarms and jealousies which might reasonably be supposed rather allayed than extinguished.” The colonists compared them to “a wedge, designed to make way for others which would be greater and heavier.”

The duties on tea, retained when the other duties were repealed in 1770, became the casus belli of the Boston Tea Party (1773) and the final crisis that led to war.

”Champagne Charley”

Townshend’s nickname reflected his style: effervescent, unpredictable, dangerously intoxicating. Belsham, writing within living memory of the man, paints a devastating portrait:

“From what appears, his ambition, increasing with his increase of honors, was much better sustained by political ability, destitute as that ability was of a clear and correct judgment, than his ability by political rectitude.”

He was known in the House of Commons for sudden changes of position, for speaking brilliantly on one side of a question and then — having confused himself — voting on the other. His speeches dazzled but his decisions baffled. Pitt, who had hoped to control him, found himself unable to do so; the King, by contrast, found Townshend’s freewheeling independence alarming but useful against Chatham’s faction.

Intrigue and Death

By the summer of 1767, Chatham’s illness had created a vacuum at the heart of government. Townshend “renewed his intrigues with redoubled assiduity” and, “highly resenting that his name was wholly omitted in the late projected arrangements, resolved to devote himself entirely to the Court.” The King received his advances eagerly. The consequences might have been “very perplexing and pernicious” — Belsham writes — had not Townshend’s death “prematurely and unexpectedly intervened.”

He died on 4 September 1767, at the age of forty-two. The precise cause is uncertain, but contemporaries noted his habit of drinking heavily. He was succeeded as Chancellor of the Exchequer by Lord North.

Edmund Burke, looking back on Townshend’s brief ascendancy, delivered the most famous epitaph:

“Even then, Sir, before this splendid orb was entirely set, and while the western horizon was in a blaze with his descending glory, on the opposite quarter of the heavens arose another luminary, and for his hour became Lord of the Ascendant.”

His widow, Caroline Townshend, was created Baroness Greenwich in her own right — a mark of the short-lived favour he had enjoyed at court.

Character and Legacy

Townshend’s legacy is the Townshend Acts — a legislative miscalculation of breathtaking proportions. He imposed duties that he claimed would be unobjectionable; they provoked a crisis that led, in the end, to the loss of the American colonies. He did not live to see the consequences of his work.

Belsham’s assessment — that his ambition exceeded his judgment, and his ability exceeded his rectitude — captures the tragedy of a man who had every gift except steadiness. He was the most brilliant orator of his generation and the most irresponsible minister. The empire paid the price.

“The term of this Minister’s public life was too short, and his situation too subordinate, to allow that full and free scope of action which is necessary to the perfect development of a character.” — Belsham
