Vice-Admiral John Byron

Born: 8 November 1723, Newstead Abbey, Nottinghamshire
Died: 10 April 1786, London
Nickname: “Foul-weather Jack”

Early Life and Family

John Byron was born at Newstead Abbey, the ancestral seat of the Byron family, a lineage of Norman origin traceable to the time of William the Conqueror. His father was William Byron, 4th Baron Byron; the title passed to John’s elder brother. The Byrons were a proud and violent family, given to feuds and eccentricities, but John — the younger son — was destined from childhood for the sea.

He entered the navy at the age of thirteen, serving in the Severn under Commodore George Anson, whose name would be forever linked with Byron’s. In 1740, when Byron was just seventeen, he was appointed a midshipman in the Wager, the store-ship accompanying George Anson’s famous circumnavigation.

What followed would shape the rest of his life.

The Wager Shipwreck (1741)

The Wager had been an East Indiaman, hastily converted for naval service, and she was ill-suited to the violent storms of the Southern Ocean. After rounding Cape Horn — a passage of such ferocity that Anson’s squadron was scattered to the winds — the Wager was driven onto the desolate coast of Patagonia in May 1741 and wrecked on the rocks. More than a hundred men perished in the surf; the survivors, numbering about a hundred and forty, found themselves marooned on an uninhabited island that became a frozen, rain-swept prison.

The story of the Wager survivors is one of the great narratives of the sea. The captain, David Cheap, maintained his authority with difficulty as discipline dissolved in the face of starvation and despair. Byron, though only a midshipman, emerges from the accounts as a figure of stoic endurance. When the crew mutinied and most of the men abandoned Captain Cheap, Byron chose to stay with the small, loyal remnant. For months they eked out an existence on seal meat and wild celery; many died of disease.

Eventually, Byron and a handful of companions built a crude boat from the wreckage and made their way northward along the coast of Patagonia and Chile, a journey of terrifying hardship. They crossed the Andes into Santiago, where a Spanish governor treated them with unexpected courtesy, recognising in the ragged English castaways a romantic dignity that transcended the state of war between their nations.

Byron spent nearly three years in captivity and arrival in England; he did not reach home until 1745.

The Narrative of the Wreck

Byron’s account of his sufferings, published later as The Narrative of the Honourable John Byron, Containing an Account of the Great Distresses Suffered by Himself and His Companions, on the Coast of Patagonia, became one of the most popular travel narratives of the 18th century. It is written in a plain, unadorned style, with a vividness that makes the reader feel the salt spray and the cold. The book went through several editions and was still being read a hundred years later.

The adjective “Byronic” is usually associated with his more famous grandson, the poet Lord Byron, but the grandfather’s life had its own dark romance — a shipwreck, a mutiny, a journey across a continent, and a nickname earned from the cruelty of the elements.

Post-Captain and the Seven Years’ War

Promoted to post-captain in 1746, Byron commanded successively the Siren, the America (60 guns), and the Victory — the same ship that Nelson would command half a century later. During the Seven Years’ War, he served under Edward Hawke and Sir John Moore, and was present at the capture of Quebec in 1759 and the destruction of the French fleet at Quiberon Bay. He commanded the Nassau in the operations against Belle Île in 1761, and later served as governor of Newfoundland, a posting that combined naval and administrative duties.

The Circumnavigation of the Dolphin (1764–1766)

In 1764, Byron was given his most celebrated command: HMS Dolphin and HMS Tamar were fitted out for a circumnavigation designed to lay claim to the Falkland Islands (discovered by the French but claimed by Britain) and to explore the South Pacific for unknown lands. The expedition was part of a larger strategic effort to extend British influence in the Pacific before the French could consolidate their presence.

Byron sailed from the Downs in June 1764, called at Madeira and Rio de Janeiro, and then — following a route that would become the classic British circumnavigation — passed through the Straits of Magellan in an astonishingly fast twenty-two days. He planted the flag at the Falklands, establishing Port Egmont, the first British settlement in the South Atlantic.

Crossing the Pacific, Byron discovered several islands, including parts of the Tuamotu Archipelago, though his haste robbed him of the credit he might have earned; many of his “discoveries” were later charted more thoroughly by Cook and Bougainville. He returned to England in May 1766, having completed the circumnavigation in less than two years — the fastest on record. It was a voyage of remarkable speed if only moderate scientific achievement.

”Foul-weather Jack”

The nickname “Foul-weather Jack” was earned not on the Dolphin but over a lifetime at sea. Wherever Byron sailed, the weather seemed to turn against him. From the shipwreck of the Wager in the Southern Ocean to the storms that plagued his West Indies command, Byron’s career was a record of relentless ill-fortune in matters meteorological.

The name was given to him by the sailors, half in affection and half in rueful recognition: when Byron put to sea, the old hands would glance at the sky and mutter, “Foul-weather Jack is out.”

The West Indies Command and the American War

Promoted to vice-admiral in 1778, Byron was given command of the West Indies station at the most critical moment of the American Revolutionary War. France had entered the war as an ally of the American rebels, and a powerful French fleet under the Comte d’Estaing had arrived in the Caribbean, threatening the British sugar islands — the most valuable remaining possessions of the crown after the loss of the American colonies.

Byron’s task was to contain D’Estaing, to protect Gibraltar from the combined Franco-Spanish fleets, and to hold the West Indies for Britain. He was given a powerful squadron — his flagship was the Princess Royal — and sent south with Admiral Howe’s fleet from the Channel.

The campaign in the West Indies was a grim disappointment. Byron pursued D’Estaing across the Atlantic, reaching St Lucia only to find that the French admiral had escaped to Grenada. In July 1779, the two fleets met off Grenada in an engagement that was inconclusive but damaging to British prestige. Byron outnumbered D’Estaing in ships of the line but failed to achieve a decisive victory; the French captured the island of Grenada and the British fleet returned to St Kitts in a state of mutual recrimination.

Byron’s conduct at Grenada was criticised by some of his captains — he was accused of poor tactical judgment and of failing to press the attack when he had the advantage. The situation was not helped by the primitive state of signalling in the navy, the confusion of battle smoke, and D’Estaing’s own considerable skill. But the result was plain: Grenada was lost, and the British fleet had come out second-best.

The Hurricane of 1779

Byron’s ill-fortune with the elements reached its climax in October 1779. A hurricane of extraordinary violence struck the West Indies, catching Byron’s fleet at sea off Martinique. The ships were scattered, dismasted, and driven onto the coasts. Several vessels were wrecked with heavy loss of life.

For Byron, it was the Wager shipwreck all over again — the same towering seas, the same screaming wind, the same helplessness in the face of nature’s fury. He survived, his flagship badly damaged, but the hurricane effectively ended his active command. He returned to England in 1780, his health broken and his reputation tarnished.

Later Years and Death

John Byron spent his last years in retirement in London, producing the narrative of his youthful sufferings that would make him famous after his death. He died on 10 April 1786, at the age of sixty-two, and was buried in the church of St Mary’s, Twickenham.

His grandson, George Gordon, Lord Byron, the poet, was born two years after the admiral’s death. The poet inherited both the title and the temperament — the romantic melancholy that seems to have run in the Byron blood. The shipwreck in Don Juan owes much to the grandfather’s narrative; the poet made his admiration for “Foul-weather Jack” plain in his verse.

Character and Appearance

Byron was a tall, handsome man, with a face that his contemporaries described as “singularly expressive” — a countenance that bore the marks of the suffering he had endured. He was known for his composure under pressure, his kindness to his men, and a certain fatalism about the weather that his long experience had amply justified.

He was not a great commander in the sense of Anson or Hawke. His battle at Grenada was a muddled affair; his tactics lacked the inspiration that marks the truly great naval leader. But he was a brave man, a skilled seaman, and an enduring symbol of the British navy’s hardiness. Any man who could survive the Wager wreck, a forced march across the Andes, two years as a prisoner in Spanish Chile, a circumnavigation of the globe, and a West Indies hurricane was a man of uncommon fibre.

Legacy

John Byron is remembered as the shipwrecked midshipman of Anson’s voyage, as the circumnavigator who first explored the Falklands, and as “Foul-weather Jack,” the unluckiest admiral in British history. His narrative is a classic of sea literature, a direct ancestor of the great 19th-century tradition of maritime adventure writing.

His connections to George Anson (under whom he began his career), Edward Hawke (under whom he served at Quiberon Bay), and John Byng (whose court-martial and execution shadowed the generation of officers who came after him) place him at the centre of the 18th-century navy’s most dramatic stories. And his grandson, Lord Byron, ensured that the family name would echo through literary history long after the admiral’s own deeds had faded from public memory.

In the navies of the 18th century, there were commanders who won great battles and commanders who lost them. John Byron was neither. He was the man who endured — the man the storms could not kill, the man who came back from the dead to tell his tale.
