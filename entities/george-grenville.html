George Grenville

George Grenville (14 October 1712 – 13 November 1770) was Prime Minister of Great Britain from 1763 to 1765. He is principally remembered as the author of the Stamp Act (1765), the ill-fated attempt to tax the American colonies that precipitated the constitutional crisis leading to the American Revolution. His ministry was also marked by the prosecution of John Wilkes, the General Warrants controversy, and the unpopular Cider Tax. Grenville was a capable administrator of narrow vision — “accurately versed in the routine of business,” Belsham records, but “utterly destitute of those commanding talents” required of a first minister.

Early Life and Political Career

Born at Wotton House in Buckinghamshire, Grenville was the second son of Richard Grenville and Hester Temple — making him brother of Earl Temple (the brother-in-law of William Pitt the Elder) and uncle of the future William Pitt the Younger through his sister Hester’s marriage. He was educated at Eton and Christ Church, Oxford, and was called to the bar in 1735.

He entered Parliament in 1741 as member for Buckingham, a seat he held for life. His early career was spent in the orbit of his brother-in-law Pitt and his brother Temple, members of the Patriot Whig opposition. In 1744 he was appointed one of the Lords of the Admiralty, and in 1747 a Lord of the Treasury under Henry Pelham. His abilities — thorough, systematic, indefatigable — made him a valued administrator but not a popular figure.

In 1754 he was appointed Treasurer of the Navy and Privy Councillor. He opposed the ministry of the Duke of Newcastle on the question of the Hessian subsidies in 1755, joining Pitt in a brief period of opposition, but returned to office as Treasurer of the Navy in Pitt-Newcastle coalition of 1757. He resigned in 1761 in protest at Bute’s management of the war.

Prime Minister (1763–1765)

Bute’s resignation in April 1763 brought Grenville to the head of the Treasury. Belsham’s character sketch is the best contemporary portrait:

“Mr. George Grenville, brother to Earl Temple, a man accurately versed in the routine of business, practised in all the punctilios of form, and the minutiae of office; open and affable in his manners; not deficient in probity, although ambitious of distinction. But though his abilities were of a class to entitle him in a subordinate station to respect, he was utterly destitute of those commanding talents, that intuitive sagacity, and intellectual comprehension, which mark the man whom nature seems to have destined to the government of empires.”

Grenville inherited a difficult situation: a vast national debt from the Seven Years’ War, a discontented Parliament, and a King who had not chosen him freely but accepted him as the only available alternative to Pitt. The King’s secret reliance on Bute — Bute’s private interviews with ministers, his continued access to the royal ear — poisoned Grenville’s relationship with the Court.

The Wilkes Affair and General Warrants

The greatest domestic crisis of Grenville’s ministry arose from the prosecution of John Wilkes. In April 1763, The North Briton No. 45 — Wilkes’s weekly newspaper — published a savage attack on the King’s speech from the throne, accusing His Majesty of “a direct falsehood.” The Grenville government, already stung by Wilkes’s earlier attacks, determined to make an example.

The ministry issued a general warrant — a warrant that named no specific person but commanded the arrest of “the authors, printers, and publishers” of the offending paper. Forty-eight people were arrested. Wilkes was committed to the Tower of London, his papers seized.

The case became a national sensation. Lord Chief Justice Pratt ruled the general warrant illegal, and Wilkes was released on the grounds of parliamentary privilege. The mob celebrated. The City of London voted Pratt a gold box. The government’s attempted prosecution collapsed in farce.

The General Warrants controversy permanently established the illegality of such warrants in English law. It made Wilkes the most famous radical in Britain and contributed heavily to the unpopularity of the Grenville ministry.

The Cider Tax

Grenville’s need for revenue — the national debt after the Seven Years’ War was immense — drove him to the Cider Tax of 1763, a duty of four shillings per hogshead on cider, subject to the laws of excise. The excise system, which permitted officials to enter premises to inspect production, was deeply unpopular. The measure revived the passions of Walpole’s Excise Bill crisis of 1733. Pitt mocked Grenville in the House by singing “Gentle Shepherd, tell me where” in response to Grenville’s demand for alternative revenue sources. The nickname “the Gentle Shepherd” stuck to Grenville thereafter.

The Stamp Act

The decisive measure of Grenville’s premiership was the Stamp Act of 1765. Unable to raise sufficient revenue at home, Grenville turned his gaze to America. Walpole, to whom the same project had been proposed decades earlier, had declined: “it was a measure too hazardous for him to venture upon” — he would leave it “to some more daring successor.”

Grenville was that successor. The Stamp Act imposed a tax on all colonial paper — newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents, ship’s papers, licences, playing cards, and dice. The declared purpose was “raising a revenue in America in aid to the mother country.”

The Act passed the Commons in March 1765, supported by comfortable majorities. Belsham records that the opposition was “weak in respect to numbers” but “unusually ardent and animated.” General Conway protested alone against the right; Colonel Barré delivered a devastating reply to Charles Townshend’s claim that the Americans were “children planted by our care, and nourished by our indulgence”:

“They planted by your care! No — your oppressions planted them in America… They nourished by your indulgence! No — they grew by your neglect of them…”

The Act triggered an explosion of colonial resistance — the Stamp Act Congress, non-importation agreements, mob violence against stamp distributors, and the emergence of a coordinated inter-colonial opposition. The crisis would define the next decade.

Dismissal

The King had never been comfortable with Grenville, who treated His Majesty with a lecturing condescension that George III deeply resented. By April 1765, the King was determined to change his ministers. He negotiated secretly with Pitt through the Duke of Cumberland. Grenville, learning of these overtures, demanded — in concert with the Duke of Bedford — the immediate dismissal of all officials connected with Lord Bute: his brother Stuart Mackenzie, the Duke of Northumberland (whose son had married Bute’s daughter), and Henry Fox (Lord Holland).

The King, who had hoped to keep Bute’s influence alive, was furious. He dismissed Grenville in July 1765 and appointed the Marquis of Rockingham in his place.

Final Years

Grenville never held office again. He went into opposition and served as the effective leader of the Grenvillite faction. He opposed the repeal of the Stamp Act in 1766, arguing — correctly from a constitutional standpoint — that repeal would be seen by the Americans as a surrender. He opposed the Townshend Acts in 1767 from the opposite direction — they were not, he argued, a serious attempt to assert British authority.

He died on 13 November 1770, at the age of fifty-eight, and was buried at Wotton. His son, George Nugent-Temple-Grenville, became the first Marquess of Buckingham.

Character and Legacy

Grenville was remembered as an administrator, not a statesman — competent, hardworking, and upright, but lacking the vision and flexibility required for the crises he faced. Belsham’s judgment is severe but not unjust: his abilities entitled him to respect in a subordinate station, but he was not equal to the government of an empire. His Stamp Act, intended as a modest revenue measure, set in motion the events that tore the British Empire apart.

“Little, certainly, did this Minister imagine how fertile would be the invention of his successors in office, or how thoroughly subdued by time and custom the spirit of the people.” — Belsham
