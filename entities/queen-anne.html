Queen Anne

Anne (6 February 1665 – 1 August 1714) was the last Stuart monarch of Great Britain, reigning from 8 March 1702 until her death. Her reign, now remembered as the Augustan Age, witnessed the forging of the United Kingdom of Great Britain, the defeat of Louis XIV’s France in the War of the Spanish Succession, and the final establishment of the Protestant succession that would pass the crown to the House of Hanover upon her death.

Early Life and Accession

Born at St James’s Palace, Anne was the second daughter of the Catholic James II and his first wife, Anne Hyde. Raised as a Protestant under the direction of her uncle Charles II, she was a distant figure throughout the reigns of her father and her brother-in-law William III. Her health was fragile from childhood — she suffered chronically from gout and a painful eye condition — yet she bore seventeen pregnancies, of which only five children survived infancy, and none lived to succeed her.

The death of William III on 8 March 1702 brought Anne to the throne at the age of thirty-seven. Her accession was greeted with universal acclaim. She was, in Burnet’s phrase, “entirely English in her heart” — a welcome contrast to the Dutch William. Her first speech to Parliament promised to follow William’s footsteps in defending the Protestant religion and the liberties of Europe, and she was voted a civil list of £700,000.

The War of the Spanish Succession

The great conflict of Anne’s reign had already begun before her accession. The death of Charles II of Spain in 1700 had left the vast Spanish inheritance contested between the French Bourbon candidate, Philip of Anjou (grandson of Louis XIV), and the Austrian Habsburg claimant, the Archduke Charles. Louis XIV’s acceptance of the Spanish throne for his grandson, followed by his recognition of the exiled James III (the Pretender) as King of England, united the nation behind war.

The military direction of the war fell to John Churchill, 1st Duke of Marlborough, appointed Captain-General of the allied forces in the Low Countries. Marlborough’s genius — combined with Anne’s unwavering personal support and his wife Sarah Churchill’s intimacy with the Queen — produced a sequence of victories without parallel in British military history:

  • Blenheim (1704) — Marlborough’s forced march to the Danube and crushing defeat of the Franco-Bavarian army, saving Vienna and shattering the myth of French invincibility
  • Ramillies (1706) — the destruction of Marshal Villeroi’s army and the conquest of the Spanish Netherlands
  • Oudenarde (1708) — a sweeping victory over the Duke of Burgundy and Marshal Vendôme, followed by the epic siege of Lille
  • Malplaquet (1709) — the bloodiest battle of the war, a pyrrhic victory that cost the allies over 20,000 casualties and began to turn opinion against the war

The naval dimension of the war was equally consequential. Sir George Rooke’s daring capture of Gibraltar in August 1704, followed by the tactical draw at Málaga the same month, established British naval supremacy in the Mediterranean. The capture of Minorca in 1708 under Sir John Leake gave Britain a permanent Mediterranean naval base at Port Mahon.

The Act of Union (1707)

Perhaps the most enduring achievement of Anne’s reign was the Act of Union 1707, which united the Kingdoms of England and Scotland into a single Kingdom of Great Britain. The union was driven by necessity: the Scottish Parliament’s Act of Security (1704) had threatened to choose a different successor from England’s after Anne’s death, and the disastrous Darien Scheme had left Scotland’s finances dependent on access to English trade.

Anne took a personal interest in the negotiations, expressing “a hearty desire to see the Union perfected.” The Treaty of Union was ratified by both Parliaments in 1707. The Scottish Parliament dissolved itself, and Scotland sent forty-five MPs and sixteen representative peers to the new united Parliament at Westminster. Burnet, a Scottish bishop who participated in the negotiations, records the bribery, the arguments, the resistance, and the eventual acquiescence that produced the Union. Anne attended a thanksgiving service at St Paul’s, declaring it “the happiest event of my life.”

Political Turmoil and the Fall of the Whigs

Anne’s reign was marked by fierce party conflict between the Whigs and Tories, and the Queen’s own sympathies shifted over time. She began her reign with a mixed ministry under the moderate Lord Godolphin (Lord Treasurer) and Marlborough (Captain-General), both of whom she trusted. But pressure from the Whig junto — Somers, Halifax, Wharton, Sunderland, and Orford — forced the admission of Whig ministers, including the Earl of Sunderland as Secretary of State in 1706.

The turning point came in 1709–1710. The trial of Dr Henry Sacheverell, a High Church Tory preacher who had denounced the Revolution Settlement and the toleration of Dissenters, ignited a firestorm. The Whigs’ prosecution of Sacheverell turned him into a popular hero. When the Lords convicted him by a narrow margin and imposed a light sentence, London erupted in celebration — the church bells rang, bonfires blazed, and the Whigs had fatally wounded themselves.

The Queen, who had long chafed under Whig dominance and whose affection for Sarah Churchill had soured, moved decisively. She dismissed the Earl of Sunderland in June 1710, dissolved Parliament, and brought in a new Tory ministry under Robert Harley (Earl of Oxford) and Henry St John (Viscount Bolingbroke). Marlborough, though retained as Captain-General through 1710, was dismissed on 31 December 1711 — accused of peculation and political disloyalty.

The Peace of Utrecht (1713)

The new ministry pursued peace. Secret negotiations with France, conducted behind the backs of the allies, produced a preliminary peace in 1711. The Barrier Treaty — by which the Dutch had secured the right to garrison key fortresses in the Spanish Netherlands — was denounced. The British demanded — and got — Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, Hudson’s Bay, and the Asiento (the monopoly on supplying slaves to Spanish America).

The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) ended the War of the Spanish Succession. The allies felt betrayed — the Habsburgs had not secured Spain, the Dutch had lost their Barrier — but Britain had gained a global empire. The peace secured the Protestant succession: Louis XIV recognised Anne as Queen and abandoned his support for the Pretender.

The Succession Crisis and Death

The great question of Anne’s reign — who would succeed her — was never far from the surface. The Act of Settlement (1701) had settled the crown on the Electress Sophia of Hanover and her Protestant descendants. Anne, though personally sympathetic to her half-brother James (the Old Pretender), never wavered in her public commitment to the Protestant succession.

As her health declined through 1713–1714, the Tory ministry under Harley and Bolingbroke was riven by internal division. Bolingbroke intrigued with the Jacobites; Harley hesitated. The Electress Sophia died in June 1714, but her son George, Elector of Hanover, stood ready to claim the throne.

On 30 July 1714, Anne suffered a stroke at Kensington Palace. She lay insensible while the Privy Council — dominated by Whig peers who had been summoned by the Dukes of Argyll and Somerset — moved to secure the Hanoverian succession. On 1 August 1714, Queen Anne died. The Jacobite succession plot collapsed. George, Elector of Hanover, was proclaimed King George I without opposition.

Character and Legacy

Anne was — in the judgment of those who knew her — a woman of limited intellect but sound instincts, obstinate in her loyalties and genuinely devoted to the Church of England. She was the last British monarch to exercise the royal veto, refusing her assent to the Scottish Militia Bill in 1708. She preferred the quiet routines of court life to the pomp of public ceremony and was deeply affected by the loss of her husband, Prince George of Denmark, in 1708.

Her reign transformed Britain. The Union of 1707 created a new political entity on the British Isles. The victories of the War of the Spanish Succession established Britain as a first-rank military and naval power — the rock against which the ambitions of Louis XIV broke. The Treaty of Utrecht gave Britain Gibraltar, Minorca, and a foothold in the Atlantic slave trade. And the peaceful accession of George I in 1714 — despite a decade of party fury and Jacobite intrigue — proved that the Revolution Settlement of 1688–1689 had secured the Protestant succession beyond serious challenge.

She gave her name to an age: Augustan, classical, the age of Marlborough, Swift, Pope, Defoe, and Newton — an age of war and poetry, of imperial expansion and domestic division, in which the foundations of the British Empire were laid.

“I know my own heart to be entirely English.” — Queen Anne’s first speech to Parliament
