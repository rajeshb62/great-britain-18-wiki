South Sea Company

The South Sea Company — The Governor and Company of the Merchants of Great Britain Trading to the South Seas and Other Parts of America, for the Encouragement of Fishing — was created in 1711 as a financial contrivance of Robert Harley’s Tory ministry. Its ostensible purpose was trade with Spanish America; its real function was to solve the pressing problem of the national debt. The Company’s name is forever linked with the South Sea Bubble of 1720 — the greatest financial catastrophe of the eighteenth century, and a disaster whose political and economic repercussions reshaped British public life for a generation.

Yet the Company’s story extends far beyond the Bubble. It continued to exist as a corporate entity for over 140 years after the crash, managing a residual role in the Asiento trade and the national debt until its final dissolution in 1853.

Harley’s Creation: The Origins of the Scheme (1711)

The South Sea Company was born from the financial exhaustion of Queen Anne’s government. The War of the Spanish Succession, now in its tenth year, had driven the national debt to unprecedented heights. The government’s credit was sustained by tallies and exchequer orders that traded at heavy discounts; the Whig-dominated Bank of England had made loans on terms that were increasingly onerous; and the Tory ministry of Robert Harley, which came to power in 1710, was desperate for a solution that would both ease the debt burden and create a Tory rival to the Whig monied interest.

Harley’s scheme was audacious. The government would take the floating debt — the unfunded tallies, army debentures, navy bills, and other instruments that had accumulated during the war, amounting to some £9 million — and convert them into equity of a new corporation. This corporation, the South Sea Company, would receive a monopoly of British trade to the South Seas (the east coast of Spanish America) and, crucially, the right to import African slaves into the Spanish colonies under the Asiento — the contract that the Peace of Utrecht would soon secure from Spain.

The conversion was a masterpiece of financial engineering. The holders of the government’s worthless tallies and debentures — soldiers, sailors, contractors, and merchants — found themselves transformed into shareholders of a company that possessed both a monopoly of potentially lucrative trade and a guaranteed return from the government on the debt it had assumed. The government, for its part, exchanged a mass of short-term, high-interest obligations for a single long-term, low-interest loan. The Company’s capital was set at £9,471,324 — an enormous sum — and the government agreed to pay 6% interest on the perpetual annuity.

The Company was incorporated by Act of Parliament in September 1711, with Harley as its first Governor. It was, from the first, a political creature — a Tory counterweight to the Whig Bank of England and East India Company, designed to secure the loyalty of the monied interest to the Harley ministry.

The Asiento and the Treaty of Utrecht (1713)

The Company’s commercial prospects depended on the outcome of the peace negotiations with France and Spain. The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) gave Britain the Asiento — the exclusive right to supply the Spanish colonies with African slaves — for thirty years, along with the right to send one annual trading ship of 500 tons to the Spanish American fairs.

These were valuable privileges, though not as valuable as the Company’s promoters claimed. The Asiento had been held by the Portuguese, the French, and the Dutch at various times; each had made money from it, but none had become fabulously wealthy. The annual ship — the navío de permiso — was a more promising concession, since it provided a legal cover for the illicit trade that everyone knew would far exceed the permitted tonnage.

The Company’s first trading voyage, the Elizabeth and Ann (1717), was a modest success. But the Company’s directors soon discovered what every European power had learned: that the Spanish colonial monopoly was fiercely defended, that the colonists were unreliable trading partners, and that the costs of maintaining factories and factors in the Spanish Indies were far higher than anticipated. The Company’s commercial operations never generated significant profits; they were, at best, a supplement to the income from the government annuity that was the Company’s real raison d’être.

The Bubble: The Great Conversion Scheme (1719–1720)

The South Sea Bubble was not an accident but a deliberate design — a scheme of breathtaking ambition that captured the imagination of the entire nation and then destroyed it.

In 1719, the Company’s directors, led by the financier John Blunt, proposed to take over the entire national debt — the unfunded portion as well as the redeemable annuities held by the Bank of England and the East India Company — and convert it into South Sea stock. The proposition was presented to Parliament in January 1720 as a competition with the Bank of England; both institutions submitted rival bids, and after a legendary bidding war, the South Sea Company’s offer was accepted.

The terms were staggering. The Company undertook to pay the government £7.5 million for the privilege of converting the debt, and to lend the government £1.25 million at 5%. In return, it would issue new stock to the holders of the national debt — exchanging government annuities for South Sea equity at a conversion rate that the Company’s directors controlled.

The mechanics of the scheme were corrupt from the outset. Belsham, in his History of Great Britain, records that the directors distributed fictitious stock — shares that had not been subscribed but were merely credited to the accounts of ministers, courtiers, and members of Parliament — as bribes to secure the passage of the enabling legislation. Over £574,000 of this “stock” was distributed to “the Treasury, the Secretaries of State, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, the Paymaster of the Forces, the First Lord of the Admiralty, the Master of the Ordnance, and many other persons of consideration.”

The Speculative Frenzy

The South Sea Act received the royal assent on 7 April 1720. What followed was the most extraordinary speculative mania in British history.

The Company opened its first subscription in April, offering stock at £300 per £100 nominal. It was massively oversubscribed. The second subscription, at £400, was even more successful. By the end of May, South Sea stock was trading at £500. In June, it reached £750. On 24 June, it touched £1,060.

The whole nation was seized by what contemporaries called “stock-jobbing madness.” Peers and parsons, merchants and maidservants, widows and aldermen — all rushed to invest. Coffee houses became trading floors; provincial towns opened their own stock exchanges; joint-stock companies were floated for the most improbable purposes — a company for “trading in hair,” another for “a wheel of perpetual motion,” a third for “an undertaking which shall in due time be revealed.” These “bubble companies” — there were nearly 200 of them — fed the frenzy and multiplied the risk.

Walpole, who had opposed the South Sea scheme in Parliament, wrote to a friend that “the madness of the times is such that there is not a man in England who does not think himself worth £50,000.” The London stock market was turning over £2 million per week — more than the total capital of the Bank of England.

The Collapse

The crash came in the late summer of 1720. The Company’s directors — knowing that the stock price was unsustainable — began selling their own holdings in August. The price started to slip: from £1,000 on 31 August to £775 on 2 September to £525 on 9 September. On 24 September, the Company’s own books were closed and South Sea stock stood at £175.

The collapse was catastrophic. Smollett describes the scene: “The ebb of this portentous tide was so violent that it bore down everything in its way; and an infinite number of families was overwhelmed with ruin. Public credit sustained a terrible shock; the nation was thrown into a dangerous ferment; and nothing was heard but the ravings of grief, disappointment, and despair.”

The banking system — still in its infancy — was nearly destroyed. The goldsmith-bankers and the Sword Blade Company, which had financed much of the speculation, went bankrupt. A run on the Bank of England followed; the Bank’s directors, who had imprudently agreed to subscribe £3.5 million into South Sea stock at 400%, were forced to renounce the agreement to save themselves. “Public credit,” writes Smollett, “sustained a terrible shock.”

The Parliamentary Inquiry and the Political Aftermath

When Parliament met in December 1720, the cry for vengeance was universal. George I, who had shortened his stay in Hanover to return to the crisis, urged Parliament to “consider of the most effectual and speedy methods to restore the national credit, and fix it upon a lasting establishment.”

The Commons appointed a secret committee to investigate the Company’s affairs. The revelations were appalling. The directors had distributed fictitious stock to a who’s who of the British political establishment: the Chancellor of the Exchequer John Aislabie had received £70,000; the Postmaster-General James Craggs the Elder had received £50,000; the Earl of Sunderland, the King’s chief minister, was deeply implicated. The directors themselves had sold their own stock before the crash, realising enormous profits while the public was left holding worthless paper.

The parliamentary vengeance was savage. Aislabie was expelled from the House of Commons, committed to the Tower, and had his estates sequestered. The directors of the Company had their estates confiscated to the sum of £2 million. Several members of Parliament involved in the corruption resigned or fled. The Earl of Sunderland — one of the King’s most trusted ministers — was forced to defend himself in the Commons; he survived, but his reputation was ruined and he died the following year.

The Robert Walpole who emerged from the wreckage to become the dominant figure in British politics was a man who had been wise enough to oppose the South Sea scheme from the start. His “Ingraftment” plan of 1721 — by which £9 million of South Sea stock was divided between the Bank of England and the East India Company — restored public credit and established Walpole’s reputation as the saviour of the financial system. He became First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer in April 1721, the beginning of his twenty-one year ascendancy.

The Company After the Bubble (1721–1853)

The South Sea Company did not disappear after the crash. It continued to exist as a corporate shell, managing its residual affairs, collecting its annuities from the government, and conducting a limited trade under the Asiento.

The Asiento itself was a source of persistent difficulty. The Company’s factories at Buenos Aires, Havana, Cartagena, and Panama were chronically unprofitable; the annual ship was a source of endless conflict with Spanish customs officials; and the illicit trade that accompanied it provoked the Spanish into ever more aggressive enforcement measures.

The tension between British smugglers and Spanish guarda costas escalated into a series of incidents — most famously the loss of Captain Jenkins’s ear in 1731 — that culminated in the War of Jenkins’s Ear (1739–1748) and the wider War of the Austrian Succession. The South Sea Company’s Asiento privileges were suspended during the war, resumed after the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), and finally surrendered by the Treaty of Madrid (1750), when the Spanish government paid the Company £100,000 in compensation.

After the loss of the Asiento, the Company had no remaining commercial function. It continued to exist as an investment trust, receiving the government annuity for the debt it had assumed in 1720 and paying dividends to its surviving shareholders. Its stock, which had traded at £1,060 in July 1720, settled at a fraction of par and remained there for the remainder of the century.

The Company’s corporate existence was gradually wound down. Its functions were absorbed by the Bank of England; its debts were gradually redeemed; and in 1853, by Act of Parliament, the South Sea Company was finally dissolved. The last bondholders were repaid; the last entry was made in the Company’s books; and the greatest speculative venture of the eighteenth century passed into history.

Historical Significance

The South Sea Bubble was more than a financial disaster; it was a political, social, and cultural event of the first importance. It destroyed the Tory party’s claim to be the party of sound finance; it discredited the Court Whigs who had been implicated in the scheme; and it cleared the way for the Walpolean Whig supremacy that dominated British politics for two generations.

The scandal produced a wave of literature — pamphlets, satires, cartoons, and sermons — that fixed the image of the “stock-jobber” as a figure of dangerous, anti-social cupidity. The Bubble Act of 1720 (repealed only in 1825) prohibited the formation of joint-stock companies without a royal charter, stifling corporate development in Britain for over a century.

But the most important consequence of the Bubble was the demonstration — at terrible cost — of the principle that the financial system rested on confidence. The national debt, the Bank of England, the entire edifice of public credit that had been constructed since 1694 — all of it presupposed the trust of the investing public. When that trust was destroyed, as it was in 1720, the consequences were catastrophic. The restoration of that trust — slow, painful, and incomplete — was the great achievement of Walpole’s financial management.

“The South Sea affair was not merely a stock-jobbing fraud, but a national calamity, which struck at the root of public credit, and shook the whole fabric of government to its foundation.” — Belsham
