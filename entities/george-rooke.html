Sir George Rooke (1650–1709)

Sir George Rooke (1650–1709) was an English naval commander who served as Admiral of the Fleet under Queen Anne, achieving the two most celebrated naval victories of the early War of the Spanish Succession: the destruction of the Franco-Spanish treasure fleet at the Battle of Vigo Bay (1702) and the capture of Gibraltar (1704). He also fought the largest fleet action of the war at the Battle of Malaga (1704), a tactical draw that confirmed Anglo-Dutch naval supremacy in the Mediterranean. A decorated veteran of the Nine Years’ War and the Baltic expedition of 1700, Rooke was also a Tory figure who became a lightning rod for partisan controversy in his later career.

Early Career and the Nine Years’ War

Rooke was born in 1650, the son of Sir William Rooke, a Kentish baronet, and Anne (née L’Estrange) of Hunstanton, Norfolk. He entered the Royal Navy in his youth and saw active service in the Third Anglo-Dutch War. By 1692, during the Nine Years’ War, he had risen to the rank of rear-admiral and fought at the Battle of Barfleur and the subsequent action at La Hogue (May 1692), where the combined Anglo-Dutch fleet under Edward Russell, Earl of Orford shattered the French invasion threat against England. Rooke commanded a division in the battle and was knighted for his conduct.

Promoted to vice-admiral, Rooke served in the Mediterranean under Russell and later commanded a squadron in the Channel. In 1697, with Russell’s elevation to the peerage and appointment as First Lord of the Admiralty, Sir George Rooke was declared Admiral of the Fleet and took command of the main fleet at Portsmouth. He found the ships “not half manned, and worse victualled” but by the end of June 1697 had assembled forty-four sail of the line. He escorted the Russian Tsar Peter the Great across the Channel in January 1698, a service that left the Tsar with a lasting interest in British naval discipline.

The Baltic Expedition (1700)

In the spring of 1700, King William III dispatched Rooke with a powerful squadron to the Baltic, where he was joined by a Dutch squadron to enforce a peace mediated by England and Sweden between Denmark and the Duke of Holstein-Gottorp. Rooke pushed into the Sound, manoeuvring against the Danish fleet under the guns of Kronborg Castle, and linked up with the Swedish fleet of Count Wachtmeister. The combined Anglo-Swedish-Dutch fleet of fifty-two ships of the line blockaded Copenhagen and covered the landing of King Charles XII of Sweden’s troops near Elsinore, which forced the Danes to sign the preliminaries of the Treaty of Travendal in August 1700.

The War of the Spanish Succession: Cadiz and Vigo Bay

Upon Queen Anne’s accession in March 1702, Rooke was appointed Vice-Admiral of England and given command of a massive combined English and Dutch fleet: thirty English and twenty Dutch ships of the line, with 13,801 troops under the Duke of Ormond. The expedition’s primary objective was the capture of Cadiz, the great Spanish Atlantic port.

The Failure at Cadiz

The fleet appeared off Cadiz on 12 August 1702. The Duke of Ormond argued for an immediate assault on the Isle of Leon, believing the town was in panic and could be taken by a sudden attack. But the sea officers in the council of war overruled him, insisting first on reducing the Fort of St. Catharine and Port St. Mary. The delay proved fatal: the landing at the Bay of Bulls on 15 August met only brief resistance, and the troops took Port St. Mary without a fight, but the army then became distracted by plunder and the looting of churches and private houses. Spanish resistance stiffened, the governor of Cadiz refused to surrender, and the expedition was abandoned in September. The plunder of Port St. Mary caused a scandal in England.

The Victory at Vigo Bay

The disaster at Cadiz was redeemed by one of the most spectacular naval actions of the century. While the fleet was returning home, Rooke learned from Captain Thomas Hardy (who had brought an express from Lisbon) that the Spanish treasure galleons, under the convoy of a French squadron commanded by Monsieur Château-Renault, had put into Vigo harbour on 16 September 1702. Rooke called an immediate council of war on 6 October, and the assembled flag-officers resolved to attack.

The harbour entrance was barely three-quarters of a mile wide, defended by a battery of eight brass and twelve iron guns on the north side, a platform of forty guns on the south, and a stone fort with a breastwork and trench mounting ten guns with 500 men. Across the harbour stretched a formidable boom of ships’ yards and topmasts, fastened with three-inch rope and hawsers, moored at each end to seventy-gun ships. Inside were five French men-of-war of sixty to seventy guns moored broadside to the entrance.

Rooke and his flag-officers removed their flags from the great first- and second-rates to smaller third-rates that could enter the harbour — Rooke moving from the Royal Sovereign to the Somerset. A detachment of fifteen English and ten Dutch men-of-war was ordered in, supported by fireships, frigates, and bomb-vessels. On 23 October 1702, the Duke of Ormond landed 2,500 men six miles south of Vigo, Lord Shannon stormed the stone fort with 500 grenadiers, and the British flag flew from the batteries. Vice-Admiral Hopson in the Torbay then rammed the boom and broke through, the Kent and the rest of the squadron pouring into the harbour. Despite a French fireship that grappled the Torbay (and was extinguished only because it carried a cargo of snuff), the English ships battered the French into submission. Fifteen French men-of-war and seventeen Spanish galleons were burnt, sunk, or taken. The treasure seized — or destroyed in the fighting — dealt a severe blow to the finances of France and Spain. Rooke was hailed a hero, though the plunder scandal from Cadiz dogged the campaign.

The Capture of Gibraltar (1704)

After Vigo, Rooke commanded the Mediterranean fleet in 1703 and early 1704. In the spring of 1704, with a fleet augmented by Sir Cloudesley Shovel’s squadron, Rooke considered several schemes — including a second attack on Cadiz — but found himself constrained by contradictory instructions from the Court of the Archduke Charles (the Austrian claimant to the Spanish throne) and the King of Portugal. Determined not to spend the summer “doing nothing of importance,” Rooke called a council of war in the roadstead of Tetuan on 17 July 1704. After a long debate, it was resolved to make “a sudden and vigorous attempt upon Gibraltar,” for three reasons: the place was poorly garrisoned and might be taken; its possession was of infinite strategic importance; and the capture would give lustre to Queen Anne’s arms.

The fleet arrived in the Bay of Gibraltar on 21 July 1704. The Prince of Hesse-Darmstadt landed 1,800 marines on the isthmus to cut off communications. The governor, summoned, declared he would defend the place to the last. At daybreak on 22 July, Rooke gave the signal for bombardment. The fleet poured fifteen thousand shot into the town in five hours, driving the defenders from their fortifications at the South Mole Head. Captain Hicks and Captain Jumper pushed ashore with their pinnaces and seized the fortifications. A Spanish mine killed two lieutenants and forty men, but Captain Whitaker and his seamen took a redoubt between the mole and the town. Rooke sent in terms, and on 24 July the governor capitulated. The garrison, the Prince of Hesse, and the seamen of the fleet — not a single soldier had been landed for the assault — had taken the Rock. The Spanish later claimed the place had neither garrison nor guns, but Campbell’s account records over one hundred brass pieces mounted on the defences.

The Battle of Malaga (1704)

After garrisoning Gibraltar, the fleet sailed to Tetuan for wood and water. On 9 August they sailed again, sighting the French fleet. For three days the French avoided combat, but on Sunday, 13 August 1704, Rooke brought them to action off Malaga. The French line consisted of fifty-two ships and twenty-four galleys under Count Toulouse, High Admiral of France. The Anglo-Dutch fleet numbered fifty-three ships, but the French had significant advantages: seventeen three-deck ships against just seven, six hundred more guns overall, cleaner hulls fresh from port, the assistance of galleys that could tow disabled ships out of the line and replenish their complements, and abundant ammunition.

Nevertheless, at ten in the morning, the English bore down in order of battle. Rooke opened the engagement with a chase-gun at the French admiral and threw out the signal for battle. The fighting fell heaviest on the Royal Catherine, the St George, and the Shrewsbury. The battle raged for the entire day. Several English ships were towed out of the line having exhausted their powder; the Monk of sixty guns was boarded three times by a seventy-gun French ship and beat her off each time. But by afternoon the French van gave way, and the battle ended with daylight. The French slipped away overnight using their galleys as tugs.

The Dutch — whose ships had formed the rear — declared Malaga “the best fought action recorded in history.” French claims of victory (Louis XIV ordered a Te Deum and struck a medal) were propaganda: the French fleet avoided battle for the remainder of the war, never again contesting the empire of the sea. Malaga, in Campbell’s judgment, was the battle that “decided the empire of the sea,” confirming Britain and the Netherlands as the undisputed maritime powers. Parliament voted its thanks, and Rooke’s name was bracketed with the Duke of Marlborough’s — the two great captains of the annus mirabilis of 1704.

Later Career and Controversy

Despite his triumphs, Rooke came under sustained attack from Whig historians and politicians, notably Gilbert Burnet, Bishop of Salisbury, who in his History of His Own Times accused Rooke of lukewarmness at Cadiz and downplayed the victory at Malaga. The partisan atmosphere in Parliament — Lord Haversham’s blistering speeches, the inquiries into Admiralty mismanagement, the attacks on the Prince of Denmark’s council — gave Rooke such disgust that after 1704 he declined further command. Campbell records that Rooke “desired that the Queen might be easy, and the nation well served, rather than that any opportunities might be given him of adding either to his reputation or estate.”

The Duke of Argyle defended Rooke’s memory in the House of Lords in 1740, calling Burnet “a very credulous weak man” and insisting that “the greatest part of [his account] is a downright lie.” The Tory pamphleteer Mr. Hornby, in his Caveat against the Whigs, wrote: “In 1704, Sir George Rooke, with a crew of cabin-boys, took the almost impregnable fortress of Gibraltar.”

Sir George Rooke died on 24 January 1709, at the age of fifty-eight.

Legacy

Rooke’s legacy rests on three achievements: the destruction of the treasure fleet at Vigo Bay, which damaged Franco-Spanish finances at a critical moment; the capture of Gibraltar, which gave Britain the strategic gateway to the Mediterranean for over three centuries; and the Battle of Malaga, which — though tactically inconclusive — broke French naval morale and left the allies unchallenged at sea for the remainder of the war. His Gibraltar prize, taken without a single soldier setting foot ashore, stands as one of the most astonishing feats of arms in the age of sail.

He is sometimes overshadowed by his contemporaries Cloudesley Shovel, who commanded under him at Malaga and later perished in the Scilly naval disaster of 1707, and Edward Russell, Earl of Orford, the victor of Barfleur. Among his subordinates at Gibraltar and Malaga were Sir John Leake, who would go on to capture Minorca and command in the Mediterranean, and George Byng, Viscount Torrington, the future victor of Cape Passaro.
