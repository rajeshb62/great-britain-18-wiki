George Anson, 1st Baron Anson (1697–1762)

George Anson was the architect of British naval supremacy in the mid-eighteenth century — a circumnavigator who captured the richest prize in naval history, a victor at the Battle of Cape Finisterre who dismantled a French squadron, and a reforming First Lord of the Admiralty who rebuilt the Royal Navy into the instrument that conquered a global empire. His career spanned the transition from a navy that merely held its own to one that swept the oceans of the world. Yet his legacy is shadowed by his role in the execution of Admiral John Byng.

Early Career

Born in 1697 into a Staffordshire family of modest gentry, Anson entered the navy as a volunteer. He was made captain of the Diamond in 1731, commanded the Squirrel on the coast of South Carolina in 1732 (where he developed an early appreciation of American colonial affairs), and in 1737 received command of the Centurion, a sixty-gun ship that would become the most famous vessel in the British navy. A cruise to Guinea between 1737 and 1739 kept him occupied in the routine of the peacetime service, but with the outbreak of the War of Jenkins’ Ear, greater things were in store.

The Circumnavigation, 1740–1744

In 1740, Anson was appointed commodore of a squadron destined for the South Seas, with orders to harass Spanish possessions on the Pacific coast of America and, if possible, to intercept the annual Manila galleon carrying the treasure of the East across the Pacific. His squadron consisted of the Centurion (flagship), the Gloucester, the Severn, the Pearl, the Wager store-ship, and the Trial sloop — six vessels carrying 961 men.

The squadron sailed from Spithead in August 1740. Almost immediately, the enterprise began to unravel. Admiralty procrastination had delayed the fitting-out, and the ships sailed at the worst possible season for rounding Cape Horn. The storms that greeted them off the southern tip of South America were of unimaginable ferocity: mountainous seas, sleet, snow, and winds that howled with a fury that no one aboard had ever experienced. The ships were scattered. The Severn and Pearl turned back. The Wager was wrecked on the desolate coast of Patagonia, her crew left to an unimaginable struggle for survival. The Gloucester suffered so terribly from scurvy that her crew could scarcely work the ship.

By the time Anson reached the island of Juan Fernández in March 1741 — the rendezvous point — the Centurion carried only a fraction of her original crew. The Trial sloop had lost so many men that she required assistance to anchor. Out of 961 men who had left England, only 145 would ever return. Scurvy had claimed the rest — a mortality rate that shocked even the hardened officers of the Royal Navy and that would eventually drive Anson to become a reformer of naval hygiene.

From Juan Fernández, Anson raided Spanish settlements along the coast of Chile and Peru. He took the small town of Paita by storm, captured several prizes, and gathered intelligence on Spanish shipping. But the great object of the expedition — the Manila galleon — eluded him. He sailed across the Pacific to the Ladrones (Marianas), then to Tinian, where a bizarre incident nearly ended his life: the Centurion was driven from her anchorage by a sudden gale, leaving Anson and part of his crew marooned on the island for nearly a month before the ship miraculously returned.

The Capture of the Manila Galleon

Anson finally reached Macao on the coast of China, where he found the Chinese authorities obstructive and suspicious. He gave out that he was bound for Batavia, but his real intention was to return to the Pacific and try once more for the galleon. He calculated that two ships might sail that year — the previous year’s having been prevented from making the passage.

On the 20th of June, 1743, at sunrise, a sail was discovered from the masthead. The whole ship filled with joy. At half-past seven she was visible from the deck, firing a gun and taking in her topsails. The Centurion steered towards her. The Spanish galleon — the Nuestra Señora de Covadonga — came boldly on, her commander confident in the size and strength of his vessel. She was a ship of remarkable dimensions, pierced for forty-four guns but carrying sixty, with a crew of over 500 men.

At noon the Centurion was within a league. The galleon hauled up her foresail and lay to, hoisting Spanish colours and displaying the standard of Castile from her maintop-gallantmast-head. Anson ordered his men to maintain a constant, separate fire — a tactic designed to prevent the Spanish practice of falling flat on deck during each broadside and rising to reload. The two ships engaged at pistol-shot range. In the first half-hour, the Centurion shot ahead and lay across the galleon’s bow, raking her with an incessant fire. The manoeuvre decided the contest: the Spaniards fell into confusion, and after suffering sixty-seven killed and eighty-four wounded (against only two killed and sixteen wounded in the Centurion), the galleon struck her colours.

In that same moment of victory, an officer told Anson that the Centurion was dangerously on fire near the magazine — cartridges had blown up in the engagement, igniting a parcel of oakum in the after hatchway. The galleon fell on board the Centurion at the same instant. Anson, with perfect presence of mind, gave calm orders to extinguish the fire with the least possible alarm. It was done.

The treasure of the Covadonga — bars of silver, chests of coin, and the accumulated wealth of the Manila-Acapulco trade — was transferred into the Centurion. Anson returned to Macao, where a fire in the suburbs of Canton gave him an opportunity to render such effective service to the Chinese that the viceroy admitted him to a formal audience — an unprecedented honour for a foreign commander — and the necessities that had been refused by chicanery were granted in gratitude.

The Centurion sailed from Canton on the 15th of December, 1743, with her treasure aboard. Entering the English Channel, she passed undetected through a French fleet, hidden by a thick fog. On the 15th of June, 1744, she anchored at Spithead. The voyage round the world was complete.

Cape Finisterre and the Peerage

Anson was forty-four years old when he returned. His fame and the fabulous wealth of his prize made him the most popular man in England. He was promoted to rear-admiral of the blue, elected Member of Parliament for Haydon in Yorkshire, and on the 27th of December, 1744, appointed a Lord of the Admiralty. Further promotions followed — rear-admiral of the white in April 1745, vice-admiral in July 1746.

In the spring of 1747, he went to sea in the Prince George of ninety-eight guns with Rear-admiral Sir Peter Warren in the Devonshire and twelve other ships. On the 3rd of May, off Cape Finisterre, they fell in with a French fleet and convoy bound for the East and West Indies — thirty-eight ships in all, including nine men-of-war. Anson observed that the French intended to escape rather than fight, and made the signal to chase and engage without regard to the line of battle. The Centurion was in the squadron, and fortune still attended her. By six o’clock in the evening, six men-of-war and four Indiamen were prizes. The French commander, presenting his sword to Anson, pointed to the Invincible and the Glory, two of the surrendered ships, and said: “Sir, you have conquered the Invincible, and Glory attends you.”

On the 13th of June, 1747, Anson was raised to the peerage as Baron Anson of Soberton in the county of Southampton, choosing the motto Nil desperandum — “Despair nothing” — which perfectly expressed the equanimity with which he had endured so many dangers.

First Lord of the Admiralty

On the 24th of April, 1748, Anson married Elizabeth, eldest daughter of Philip, Lord Hardwicke, then Lord Chancellor of Great Britain. He was appointed Vice-Admiral of Great Britain on the 12th of June, 1749. When the Earl of Sandwich retired from the head of the Admiralty board on the 12th of June, 1751, Anson succeeded him as First Commissioner — a post he would hold, with a brief interruption, for the remainder of his life.

As First Lord, Anson transformed the Royal Navy. He standardised the design of warships, sweeping away the chaotic variety of classes and dimensions that had plagued the service. He issued uniform patterns for officers — the origin of the standard naval uniform that persists in modified form to this day. He revised the Articles of War, tightening discipline and clarifying the duties of commanders. He overhauled the dockyards, expanded the fleet, and established a system of permanent squadrons that could maintain distant blockades. In the early 1750s, when the king travelled to Hanover, it was Anson who convoyed him; in 1752 and 1755, during the king’s absence, he was named one of the Lords Justices of the kingdom.

The Byng Controversy

The shadow upon Anson’s character arises from the trial and execution of Admiral John Byng in 1757. Byng had failed to relieve Minorca, a failure that the government — and the Admiralty headed by Anson — needed to explain to a furious public. Anson was deeply involved in the proceedings. The biographers of the time concede that “a dubious shade has fallen on his character” from this episode. As First Lord, Anson had a natural interest in diverting odium from the Admiralty onto the unfortunate admiral: if Byng had been acquitted, much of the consequences of his conduct would have been ascribed to his superiors. It is therefore argued that Anson was anxious — not necessarily for Byng’s death, but certainly for his conviction. He may have actively prevented the royal mercy from extending to Byng after the court-martial had recommended clemency.

The full extent of Anson’s responsibility remains debated. What is certain is that his name is, to this day, linked with the most controversial execution in British naval history.

The Seven Years’ War and Death

When the Seven Years’ War erupted, Anson was the guiding hand of British naval strategy from Whitehall. He directed the global deployment of the fleet — the blockade of Brest under Hawke, the Mediterranean campaign under Boscawen, the expedition against Louisbourg, the reinforcement of William Pitt the Elder’s grand designs. His administrative reforms had created the navy that won the war.

George Anson died on the 6th of June, 1762. He did not live to see the Peace of Paris in 1763, which confirmed the supremacy of the navy he had built. In person, he was a man of quiet and reserved demeanour, capable of great patience and sustained purpose, but also of political ruthlessness when the interests of the service — or of his own position — demanded it. His motto, Nil desperandum, is the key to his character: a man who never despaired, whether he was weathering the storms off Cape Horn, lying becalmed in the Pacific with his crew dying around him, or navigating the treacherous shoals of the Admiralty boardroom.
