Sir John Leake

Early Life and Family

Sir John Leake was born in 1656 at Rotherhithe, the second son of Richard Leake, a distinguished gunner who served with great bravery in the first Dutch War and the Battle of Solebay (1672), and who was later appointed Master-Gunner of England within the Tower of London. John Leake’s younger brother was Captain Stephen Leake, the noted engineer and artillery officer. The Leake family thus formed something of a naval and military dynasty.

John Leake served as a young man on board the Royal Prince alongside his father, learning the practical arts of seamanship and gunnery from one of the most experienced gunners in the service.

Rise to Command

Leake’s early career was marked by steady advancement through the ranks. He had the good fortune to serve under the patronage of the Earl of Torrington, who recognised his abilities and recommended him for command. By the 1690s, Leake had established himself as a capable and enterprising captain.

His great opportunity came during the War of the Spanish Succession. In 1704, as a rear-admiral, he played a prominent part in the capture of Gibraltar under Sir George Rooke, commanding the vessels that covered the landing of the marines who scaled the fortress. The capture of Gibraltar, achieved on 4 August 1704 after a brief but fierce assault, was one of the most consequential operations of the war, and Leake’s contribution earned him a knighthood.

The Relief of Barcelona (1705–1706)

Leake’s finest hour came in the Mediterranean campaigns in support of the Archduke Charles (Charles III). In the spring of 1705, he commanded a squadron that assisted in the landing of troops before Barcelona. Though the first attempt miscarried — the Prince of Hesse discovered that the Austrian party within the city had been betrayed — the fleet remained on station and Leake’s ships bombarded the French positions.

When the city was recaptured by the allies in 1706, Leake played a crucial role in the relief expedition. He brought reinforcements and supplies that enabled the garrison to hold out against the besieging French army. The relief of Barcelona was a decisive moment in the war for Spain.

The Mediterranean Command

In February 1707, Leake was appointed Vice-Admiral of England and Commander-in-Chief of the Mediterranean fleet, succeeding Sir Cloudesley Shovel after the latter’s return from the Toulon expedition. He hoisted his flag on board the Britannia and sailed for Lisbon, from whence he proceeded into the Mediterranean.

Leake’s first task was to secure the coast of Spain and maintain communication with the allied army. He landed the Imperial troops brought from Italy in time to reinforce the army under Count Staremberg, though not quickly enough to save Tortosa, which fell to the Duke of Orléans. After the loss of Denia — whose garrison were made prisoners of war contrary to the articles of capitulation — Leake’s fleet provided the only reliable line of communication between Catalonia and the allied bases in Italy.

The Reduction of Alicant and Cartagena

After the relief of Barcelona, Leake was employed in the reduction of Carthagena and Alicant. Before Alicant, which was protected by one hundred and sixty guns facing the sea and bravely defended by Brigadier Mahoni, Leake reduced the fortress with only five ships, dismounting many of the guns and driving the enemy from their batteries. This action demonstrated both his tactical skill and his willingness to take calculated risks.

The Conquest of Sardinia (1708)

The most brilliant of Leake’s independent operations came in the summer of 1708. Having conveyed Queen Maria Amalia of Spain to Barcelona with all imaginable respect, he turned his attention to the reduction of Sardinia, which was held for King Philip V.

Leake appeared before Cagliari on 1 August 1708 and summoned the Marquis of Jamaica, the Spanish governor, to surrender. The governor declared his resolution to hold out to the last extremity. Leake ordered the place bombarded all that night; the following morning Major-General Wills landed with 1,800 men and made dispositions for storming the city. The Spanish governor, believing himself at the last extremity, capitulated. The reduction of Sardinia was accomplished in a week, without the loss of a single man. The island’s clergy declared unanimously for Charles III, and the new viceroy was established in peaceful possession of his government. Sardinia proved of immense value: it gave security to allied navigation and enabled Charles III to supply himself with corn and other provisions from the island.

The Conquest of Minorca (1708)

Scarcely had Leake completed the conquest of Sardinia before his assistance was required for another reduction. Sailing from Cagliari on 18 August, he arrived before Port Mahon on the 25th. There he waited for Lieutenant-General Stanhope (later Earl Stanhope and Secretary of State), who arrived on 1 September with the land forces.

A council of war resolved that the ships bound for England should leave behind all marines above middle complement, and that the English and Dutch squadrons should remain at Port Mahon to assist with seamen and marines in the reduction. The siege of Fort St. Philip began with about 3,000 men. Stanhope ordered arrows shot into the place bearing papers that threatened the garrison would be sent to the mines if they did not surrender before the batteries were finished. The garrison — 1,000 Spaniards and 600 French marines under Colonel La Jonquiere — imagined the besiegers numbered at least 10,000, so artfully had they been drawn up. The batteries began to play, demolishing four towers that served as outworks, and Brigadier Wade stormed a redoubt. On the second or third day the garrison beat a parley and capitulated.

The conquest of Minorca gave Great Britain the magnificent harbour of Port Mahon, which would remain a British naval base for much of the eighteenth century and serve as the principal Mediterranean anchorage of the Royal Navy until the loss of Minorca in 1756 — a loss for which John Byng was court-martialled and executed.

Later Career and Retirement

After his Mediterranean triumphs, Leake returned to England, where he was appointed to the highest honours the navy could confer. He served as a Lord of the Admiralty and was in due course promoted to Admiral of the Fleet — the highest rank in the Royal Navy.

With the accession of George I and the change in the political complexion of the government, Leake found himself out of favour. The new Whig ministry settled on him a pension of £600 per annum, which he considered grossly inadequate for a man of his rank and service. As Admiral of the Fleet, he was entitled by custom to half-pay of fifty shillings a day; his junior, Admiral Aylmer, who succeeded him in command, was placed on regular half-pay and paid his arrears from 1710, while Leake himself received far less. Though his friends struggled to persuade him to accept the pension, Leake protested against what he conceived as an act of injustice and partiality, but he did not enter into party opposition. He resolved to devote his remaining years to quiet retirement.

He built a small country seat near Greenwich and also possessed a country house at Beddington, Surrey. In his younger years, he had married a daughter of Captain Richard Hill of Yarmouth, by whom he had one son — whose gross and constant misconduct embittered Leake’s last years. The son entered the navy and was given command of the Tartar frigate at twenty-four; he captured a 24-gun French frigate but proved dissipated and vicious. His disgraceful marriage and early death from a lingering incurable disorder shook Leake’s constitution profoundly.

Death and Character

Sir John Leake died on 1 August 1720, in the sixty-fifth year of his age, after a painful illness involving a mortification in his back where an issue had dried up. He underwent an operation with fortitude and patience, but it proved ineffectual. He was buried in the family vault in the parish church at Stepney.

Of his person, his biographer records that he was of middle stature, well set and strong, a little inclining to corpulency, with a florid complexion, an open countenance, sharp and piercing eyes, and a graceful and manly address. He enjoyed excellent health, hardly knowing what it was to be sick, and though he drank his bottle freely, he was never disguised nor impaired.

Legacy

Leake was one of the great Mediterranean commanders of Queen Anne’s reign, ranking alongside Sir George Rooke and Sir Cloudesley Shovel in the pantheon of early eighteenth-century admirals. His conquests of Sardinia and Minorca in the space of a single month (August 1708) stand as a remarkable achievement in amphibious warfare — accomplished, in the case of Sardinia, without the loss of a single man. Port Mahon, which he secured for Britain, became the Royal Navy’s principal Mediterranean base and remained so for nearly half a century.

George Byng succeeded Leake as the dominant British commander in the Mediterranean, and the traditions of Mediterranean command that Leake established — the ability to cooperate with land forces, to conduct amphibious operations, and to maintain a fleet far from home bases — were carried forward by Edward Hawke and George Anson in the later eighteenth century.
