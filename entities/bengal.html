Bengal

Bengal — the subah (province) of the Mughal Empire that encompassed the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta, the richest and most populous region of the Indian subcontinent. In the second half of the 18th century, Bengal was transformed from the independent-minded province of the Nawabs into the centrepiece of the East India Company’s Indian empire. Its revenues financed British expansion; its trade enriched the Company; its famine of 1770 became the greatest demographic catastrophe of the age; and its governance became the subject of the most famous parliamentary impeachment in British history.

Geography and Economy

Bengal occupied the vast alluvial plain formed by the Ganges and the Brahmaputra — a flat, fertile, water-logged country of rice fields, mango groves, and sluggish rivers, measuring roughly 500 miles north to south and 300 miles east to west. The delta — the Sundarbans — was a labyrinth of tidal creeks and mangrove swamps, the haunt of tigers and pirates. The interior, where the rivers spread out into a network of navigable channels, was the most productive agricultural region in Asia.

The wealth of Bengal was proverbial. Its rice fed the subcontinent; its cotton textiles — the fine muslins of Dacca, the calicoes of Hooghly, the silks of Murshidabad — were the principal manufactured goods of Asian trade. Saltpetre, sugar, indigo, opium, and timber supplemented the export trade. The Mughal treasury depended on Bengal’s revenues; the Nawabs of Bengal were the richest princes in India, their court at Murshidabad a centre of luxury and patronage that rivaled the splendour of Delhi itself.

The principal cities of Bengal bore witness to its prosperity. Murshidabad, the capital of the Nawabs, was a city of palaces, mosques, and gardens, its population perhaps 200,000 souls. Dacca, the former Mughal capital, was the centre of the muslin trade, its looms employing tens of thousands of weavers. Calcutta, the British settlement, had transformed itself from a swampy trading post into the largest European city in India. Patna, far up the Ganges, was the entrepôt of the saltpetre and opium trades. Hooghly, Chinsurah, Chandernagore, Serampore — the factories of the European trading companies lined the banks of the river that gave the province its name.

The Mughal Province

Under the Mughal Empire, Bengal was administered by a subahdar — a provincial governor appointed by the Emperor. The Mughal system was efficient by pre-modern standards: a hierarchy of tax-collectors and magistrates, a standing army, a network of roads and river patrols, and a civil service recruited from the Hindu and Muslim gentry. The land revenue — the foundation of all government in India — was assessed and collected with reasonable regularity.

The 18th century brought the disintegration of Mughal authority. The death of Aurangzeb in 1707 unleashed a succession of rebellions, invasions, and secessions. The Nawabs of Bengal, who had been appointed by the Emperor, became hereditary rulers in practice, paying only nominal allegiance to Delhi. Under Alivardi Khan (1740–1756), Bengal maintained its independence against the Maratha raids that devastated the Deccan, but at the cost of constant military expenditure and the plunder of the treasury.

The English East India Company

The East India Company had been trading in Bengal since the early 17th century. Its principal settlement was Fort William in Calcutta — a modest fortification on the eastern bank of the Hooghly River, which the Company had fortified, garrisoned, and administered as a sovereign entity within the Nawab’s territories. The Company enjoyed extensive trading privileges, granted by successive Mughal emperors, which exempted its goods from transit duties and allowed it to coin its own money.

The relationship between the Company and the Nawabs was one of mutual suspicion. The Company’s wealth, military power, and extraterritorial privileges made it a state within a state; the Nawabs’ arbitrary exactions, confiscations, and threats to revoke the Company’s privileges made the merchants constantly anxious. The balance tipped decisively in June 1756, when the young and volatile Siraj-ud-Daulah, newly ascended to the musnud (throne) of Bengal, descended upon Calcutta and overwhelmed the weak British garrison.

The Black Hole of Calcutta — the infamous dungeon in which 146 European prisoners were confined overnight, from which only twenty-three emerged alive — became the casus belli for the Company’s revenge. Robert Clive, dispatched from Madras with a small force, retook Calcutta in January 1757 and defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah at the Battle of Plassey on 23 June 1757.

The Plassey Settlement

Clive’s victory at Plassey did not, in itself, give the Company sovereignty over Bengal. The Company had no claim to the province; it had merely placed a friendly Nawab — Mir Jafar — on the throne in place of a hostile one. The Nawab remained the nominal ruler, collecting taxes, administering justice, and commanding the army. But the reality was different.

The Company extracted from Mir Jafar an enormous indemnity — perhaps £1.5 million in cash, plus substantial presents to the senior officers. The Company’s private trade was exempted from all duties. The Company’s military forces were retained at the Nawab’s expense. The Nawab’s government became, in effect, a client regime, unable to refuse the Company’s demands without risking deposition.

The corruption and extortion that followed Plassey became notorious. The Company’s servants — who had been clerks and factors on modest salaries — found themselves in possession of almost unlimited opportunities for private enrichment. They engaged in the inland trade, buying and selling goods without paying the duties that the Nawab’s subjects were required to pay. They accepted presents from the Nawab and his ministers. They used their authority to coerce Indian merchants and manufacturers. In the words of the Company’s own investigators, the government of Bengal had become “a system of despotism, corruption, and extortion.”

The Diwani (1765)

Clive returned to Bengal in 1765 as governor, charged with restoring order to a province that had been reduced to anarchy by the Company’s greed. His solution was audacious and transformative.

In August 1765, Clive negotiated a treaty with the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, then a pensioner of the Nawab of Oudh. By the grant of the Diwani, the East India Company was appointed the revenue administrator of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa — the three provinces of the Mughal subah of Bengal. The Nawab of Bengal remained nominally sovereign, exercising criminal jurisdiction and military command. But the Company now collected all taxes, administered the civil courts, and controlled the treasury.

The Diwani was a legal fiction that disguised a revolution. Overnight, the Company ceased to be a trading corporation and became the de facto ruler of twenty million people. The revenue from Bengal — perhaps £3 million per annum — financed the Company’s wars, its administration, its trade, and the private fortunes of its servants. The Nawab became a pensioner, kept in state at Murshidabad while the real business of government was conducted from Calcutta.

Clive’s administration (1765–1767) was a determined effort to regulate the chaos he had created. He forbade the acceptance of presents by Company servants, prohibited private trade in certain commodities, and fixed the salaries of the civil service. He suppressed the “White Mutiny” of the Bengal army’s officers, who had resigned their commissions en masse in protest against the reduction of their field allowances. But the reforms were only partially effective; the temptations of wealth were too great, and Clive’s authority did not long survive his departure.

The Dual System

The system of government established after the Diwani — known as the “dual system” — was the most extraordinary political arrangement of the 18th-century British Empire. The Nawab administered criminal justice and commanded the native army; the Company collected revenue and administered civil justice. The Nawab was maintained in splendour at the Company’s expense; the Company governed through a Deputy-Nawab appointed by the Company.

In practice, the dual system meant that no one was responsible for the welfare of the people. The Nawab had no power; the Company had no duty. The Indian officials who collected the revenue — the diwans — operated under the supervision of British supervisors whose principal qualification was not knowledge of Indian administration but the ability to collect the maximum revenue. The peasantry, the artisans, and the merchants were squeezed between the Company’s demand for profit and the Nawab’s demand for tribute, with no recourse to justice.

The Famine of 1770

The most terrible consequence of the Company’s rule in Bengal was the great famine of 1770 — the Great Famine of Bengal, one of the worst demographic disasters in modern history.

The immediate cause was a failure of the monsoon in 1769, followed by a second failure in 1770. The rice crop withered in the fields; the reserves of grain, normally sufficient to carry the province through a lean year, had already been exhausted by the Company’s revenue demands. The Company’s agents, intent on collecting the maximum land tax, refused to remit the assessments despite the clear evidence of impending catastrophe.

What followed was a calamity of biblical proportions. The price of rice rose tenfold, then twentyfold. The roads were lined with the dead and the dying. Mothers sold their children for a measure of grain. The living, too weak to bury the dead, abandoned the corpses to the jackals and the vultures. The administration — the dual system — collapsed into paralysis.

The death toll is impossible to calculate with precision. Contemporary estimates range from one-third to one-half of the population of Bengal. The best modern scholarship suggests that between ten and fifteen million people died — a mortality that exceeds any European famine of the 18th century and was proportionally comparable to the great Irish famine of the 1840s.

The Company’s response was worse than inadequate. The Court of Directors, writing from London, expressed “deep concern” and blamed the Nawab for the catastrophe. The Company’s own servants, many of whom had profited from the speculation in grain that had accompanied the famine, received no censure. The revenue demand for 1770 was collected almost in full — a fact that Edmund Burke would later use as his most devastating rhetorical weapon against the Company’s administration.

The Administration of Warren Hastings

The dual system was abolished in 1772, when the Company formally assumed direct responsibility for the civil administration of Bengal. The first Governor-General of Bengal, Warren Hastings (1772–1785), was charged with constructing a workable system of British government for the province his predecessors had plundered.

Hastings was one of the most remarkable and controversial figures of the 18th-century British Empire. A servant of the Company since the age of seventeen, he knew India intimately — its languages, its customs, its legal systems, its politics. He was a scholar as well as an administrator, the patron of Sir William Jones’s pioneering work on Hindu and Muslim law.

His reforms were substantial. He reorganized the revenue administration, replacing the hereditary zamindars (tax-farmers) with a system of five-year revenue contracts. He established a supreme court of judicature in Calcutta and regularized the administration of criminal justice. He terminated the pension of the Mughal Emperor and brought the Nawab of Bengal under direct British supervision.

But Hastings’s administration was also marked by the corruption and authoritarianism that had characterized every phase of British rule in Bengal. His wars against the Marathas and the French, his extortion of the Raja of Benares, the execution of Nuncomar — these controversies made him the target of parliamentary attack and ultimately the subject of the most celebrated impeachment in British history.

The Impeachment and the India Acts

The impeachment of Warren Hastings (1787–1795), driven by Edmund Burke with relentless eloquence, was the first great parliamentary reckoning with the morality of empire. Burke charged Hastings with “high crimes and misdemeanours” — the extortion of Indian princes, the oppression of Indian subjects, the corruption of the Company’s administration. The trial, which lasted seven years, ended in Hastings’s acquittal.

But the trial had consequences far beyond Hastings’s personal fate. It focused the attention of Parliament — and the British public — on the condition of Bengal. The result was a series of legislative interventions that transformed the governance of British India.

Pitt’s India Act of 1784 established a Board of Control in London, responsible for the political and military affairs of the Company. The Company’s commercial activities were separated from its territorial administration. The Charter Acts of 1793 and 1813 gradually brought the Company’s government under the supervision of the Crown. The Hastings trial, for all its failure to convict the man, had convicted the system.

Bengal as the Centre of British India

By the end of the 18th century, Bengal was no longer a province of the Mughal Empire — it was the centre of a new empire, the British Indian Empire, that would in time supersede the Mughal itself. Calcutta, the Company’s capital, had grown from a fortified trading post into a metropolitan city — the second city of the British Empire, after London itself. Its architecture — Government House, Fort William, St. John’s Cathedral — announced the permanence of British rule. Its merchants, lawyers, and officials formed the governing class of British India.

The revenues of Bengal financed the expansion of the Company’s dominions — the wars against Tipu Sultan in Mysore, the campaigns against the Marathas, the conquest of the Doab. Bengal’s soldiers — the Bengal Army of the Company — were the largest European-trained force in Asia. Bengal’s governors — Clive, Hastings, Cornwallis, Wellesley — were the architects of the British Raj.

But the Bengal of the 18th century was also a place of tragedy. The famine of 1770 was only the worst of a series of subsistence crises that accompanied the imposition of British rule. The de-industrialization of Bengal — the destruction of its textile industry by the competition of British manufactured goods — was a long, slow process of impoverishment. The transformation of Bengal from the richest province of the Mughal Empire to a dependent colony of the British Crown was the central economic story of the 18th-century British Empire in India.

Legacy

Bengal in the 18th century was the crucible of British imperialism in India. It was the place where the East India Company learned to be a government; where the debates about colonial morality first entered British politics; where the wealth that fuelled the Industrial Revolution was extracted; and where the famine of 1770 revealed the human cost of empire. The memory of Bengal — the Diwani, the famine, the Hastings trial — haunted British political discourse for a century, shaping the argument between imperialism and its critics.
