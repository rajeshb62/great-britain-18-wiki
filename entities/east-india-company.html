East India Company

The East India Company — the Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies — began its life in 1600 as a joint-stock venture of London merchants, armed with a royal charter from Elizabeth I. By the close of the eighteenth century, it had been transformed from a commercial enterprise into the paramount sovereign power of the Indian subcontinent, commanding armies, administering justice, collecting revenues, and governing millions. This metamorphosis — from trade to territory, from counting-house to empire — is the central drama of the Company’s eighteenth-century history, and one of the most consequential transformations in modern British history.

Foundation and Early Years (1600–1708)

The Company was granted its first charter on 31 December 1600, conferring a monopoly of English trade with all lands east of the Cape of Good Hope and west of the Straits of Magellan. For the first half-century of its existence, it competed fitfully with the more powerful and better-capitalised Dutch and Portuguese East India companies, establishing factories at Surat (1612), Madras (1639), Bombay (1668), and Calcutta (1690). Trade consisted chiefly of spices, pepper, indigo, saltpetre, and fine cotton and silk textiles — the famous Indian “chintzes” and “muslins” that revolutionised European fashion.

The Company’s early years were marked by recurrent financial crises, bitter internal disputes between rival factions of merchants, and intermittent warfare with Dutch and French competitors. Nevertheless, by the opening of the eighteenth century, it had consolidated its position on the three great “presidencies” of Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta, each governed by a President and Council exercising a delegated authority that was commercial in form but increasingly political in substance.

The Struggle with France (1744–1763)

The War of the Austrian Succession (1744–1748) and the Seven Years’ War (1756–1763) transformed the Company from a trading corporation into a military power. The French Compagnie des Indes, under the brilliant Joseph François Dupleix, had already begun intervening in the dynastic politics of the Indian princes, using the Company’s military resources to place friendly rulers on the thrones of the Carnatic and the Deccan. The English Company, initially reluctant to follow this dangerous precedent, was forced to respond.

The key figure in this transformation was Robert Clive, a young Company writer (clerk) turned soldier, whose defence of Arcot (1751) against a vastly superior Franco-Indian force established his reputation as a military commander of genius. Clive’s victories in the Carnatic shattered French ambitions in southern India, but the decisive confrontation was yet to come — in Bengal.

The Black Hole and Plassey

In June 1756, the young Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daulah, alarmed by the Company’s fortifications in Calcutta, attacked and captured the settlement. The notorious “Black Hole of Calcutta” — in which, according to the Company’s account, 123 British prisoners were confined overnight in a small cell, of whom only 23 survived — became a permanent fixture of British imperial mythology.

Clive was dispatched from Madras with a small force to recover Calcutta and punish the Nawab. He retook the city in January 1757, then entered into a conspiracy with disaffected elements at the Bengali court — particularly the Hindu banker Jagat Seth and the Nawab’s own commander-in-chief, Mir Jafar. At the Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757), Clive’s force of approximately 3,000 men (of whom only 900 were Europeans) defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah’s army of perhaps 50,000. The battle was less a military engagement than a coup d’état: Mir Jafar’s troops, who formed the bulk of the Nawab’s army, took no part in the fighting. Siraj-ud-Daulah was captured and executed; Mir Jafar was installed as Nawab in his place.

Plassey is one of the hinge-points of world history. It gave the Company effective control of Bengal, the wealthiest province of the Mughal Empire, and established a pattern of indirect rule through puppet nawabs that would be repeated across India. Clive extracted enormous personal wealth from his victory — he received lands yielding £27,000 per annum and a “jagir” (estate) worth £30,000 more — but his greater achievement was to place the Company on a footing of territorial power from which it would never retreat.

The Diwani and the Dual System (1765–1772)

After Plassey, the Company’s position in Bengal deteriorated through a combination of incompetent administration, commercial greed, and fiscal chaos. The puppet nawabs proved unable to satisfy the Company’s insatiable demands for money. The Company’s servants, freed from any effective restraint, engaged in private trade, extortion, and bribery on a colossal scale. The Bengal famine of 1770, in which perhaps one-third of the province’s population perished, was directly attributable to the Company’s mismanagement — its servants had cornered the rice market, exporting grain at enormous profit while the people starved.

In 1765, Clive returned to Bengal for his second and final governorship. His great achievement was to secure from the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II the grant of the Diwani — the right to collect and administer the revenues of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. This placed the Company in an extraordinary constitutional position: it was simultaneously the tax-collector of the Mughal Empire (through the Diwani) and a subject of the British Crown (through its charter). The revenues of Bengal, amounting to approximately £3 million per annum, transformed the Company’s finances and made it the richest corporation in the world.

Clive’s “dual system” — under which the Company collected the revenues through its appointed Deputy Subahdar while the Nawab nominally retained the administration of justice — was a transparent fiction that satisfied no one. Clive himself acknowledged its dangers, warning that without parliamentary regulation the Company would destroy itself and India with it.

The Nabob Controversy and Parliamentary Intervention

The vast wealth that Clive and his fellow servants brought back to Britain — the “nabobs” as they were called, a corruption of nawab — aroused intense resentment and suspicion. These men, many of whom had been obscure clerks a decade earlier, now bought country estates, purchased rotten boroughs, and sat in Parliament, where they were accused of corrupting British politics with Indian gold.

Edmund Burke, in his Thoughts on the Present Discontents (1770), pointed to the nabobs as a prime example of the new forces undermining the constitution. The Crown’s influence — that “power of the Crown, almost dead and rotten as Prerogative” which had “grown up anew, with much more strength, and far less odium, under the name of Influence” — found in the nabobs a new and dangerous source of corruption. Men who had made their fortunes abroad by means that could not bear parliamentary scrutiny were naturally eager to buy their way into the legislature, where they could protect themselves from investigation.

The scandal of the Company’s affairs could not be contained. In 1772, a House of Commons select committee began investigating the Company’s administration in Bengal. The reports that emerged were devastating: they described a government of systematic corruption, commercial monopoly, and territorial plunder, unconstrained by any law but the greed of its servants.

The Regulating Act (1773)

Lord North’s Regulating Act of 1773 was the first attempt by Parliament to control the Company. It established a Governor-General of Bengal with authority over the Presidencies of Madras and Bombay, created a Supreme Court of Judicature in Calcutta, and prohibited Company servants from engaging in private trade or accepting presents. Warren Hastings was appointed the first Governor-General.

The Act was a half-measure that satisfied no one. It left the Company’s commercial monopoly intact but subjected its political authority to a degree of parliamentary oversight that proved unworkable. The Governor-General’s powers were ill-defined and bitterly contested by his own Council; the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction clashed with the Company’s own courts; and the prohibition on presents was evaded as soon as it was enacted.

The Hastings Years (1773–1785)

Warren Hastings’s twelve-year administration was the most controversial in the Company’s history. A brilliant administrator and a genuine scholar of Indian languages and culture, Hastings was also a man of ruthless pragmatism who believed that the Company’s position in India could be maintained only through aggressive expansion and the systematic exploitation of Indian resources.

His wars — against the Maratha Confederacy (1775–1782), against Mysore under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan (1780–1784), against the Rohilla chieftains of northern India — extended the Company’s territorial dominion to the frontiers of the Punjab and the Deccan. These wars were financed through forced loans, tribute from allied princes, and the systematic plunder of conquered territories. Hastings’s enemies in London — particularly Edmund Burke and Philip Francis — accused him of exercising a “despotism without law” and of reducing the Indian population to a condition of “slavery and misery” worse than any they had suffered under their native rulers.

The high point of the conflict between Hastings and his opponents came with Fox’s India Bill of 1783. Charles James Fox’s bill proposed to transfer the Company’s political authority to seven parliamentary commissioners, who would govern India in the name of the Crown. The Company’s directors, shareholders, and many of the Indian interest in London fought the bill with desperate energy. George III made it known that he considered any MP who voted for the bill an enemy; the Lords rejected it, and Fox’s coalition government fell.

Pitt’s India Act (1784)

William Pitt the Younger’s India Act of 1784 was a more careful and constitutionally durable settlement. It created a Board of Control — six privy counsellors appointed by the Crown — to supervise the Company’s political and military affairs, while leaving its commercial operations to the directors. The Governor-General was given ultimate authority over the subordinate presidencies. A system of “double government” was established: the Company’s Court of Directors retained the patronage and the commercial functions, but all political decisions required the approval of the Board of Control.

The Act did not resolve the fundamental tension between commercial interest and sovereign responsibility, but it created a workable framework within which the British government could control the Company without formally taking over its administration. This system lasted — with modifications — until the Company’s final dissolution after the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

The Impeachment of Hastings (1787–1795)

Burke — who, as Belsham records, had become possessed of a “zeal for the reform of abuses in India” that bordered on obsession — launched a campaign in the House of Commons to bring Hastings to account. In 1786, the House voted to impeach Hastings on charges of high crimes and misdemeanours, covering his conduct in the Rohilla War, the treatment of the Begums of Oude, the extortion of presents from Indian princes, and the corrupt administration of justice.

The impeachment trial before the House of Lords opened in February 1788 and dragged on for seven years. Burke’s opening speech — four days of sustained oratory — was one of the greatest performances in the history of English eloquence. He painted Hastings as a “rapacious, lawless, and inhuman tyrant” who had destroyed the ancient constitution of India and reduced a civilised people to barbarism.

Hastings was ultimately acquitted on every charge (1795). The trial exhausted both men: Burke died two years later; Hastings survived, pensioned, but with his reputation permanently damaged. The trial had served a larger purpose: it established, as a matter of public principle, that the Company’s government in India was accountable to Parliament and that the rights of Indian subjects were a matter of legitimate parliamentary concern.

The Company in the Later Eighteenth Century

After Pitt’s India Act and the Hastings trial, the Company entered a period of relative stability. The wars against Tipu Sultan of Mysore continued, culminating in the storming of Seringapatam in 1799. The Maratha Wars (1803–1805, 1817–1818) completed the Company’s domination of the subcontinent. By the time of the Napoleonic Wars, the Company was the dominant power in India, controlling territory larger than any European state.

But the Company’s commercial monopoly was gradually dismantled. The Charter Act of 1813 ended its monopoly of trade with India (though it retained the China trade for another twenty years). The Charter Act of 1833 ended its commercial functions entirely; the Company became a purely administrative body, governing India in the name of the Crown until the Rebellion of 1857 led to its final abolition.

Character and Historical Significance

The East India Company was a paradox — a commercial corporation that became an imperial power without ever intending to do so, a creature of English law that governed a subcontinent whose laws it scarcely understood, an instrument of private profit that was forced, reluctantly and belatedly, to assume the responsibilities of sovereignty. Its eighteenth-century history is the history of a transformation that no one planned and no one could control.

The Company’s wars were financed by Indian revenues, fought by Indian soldiers (sepoys) under European officers, and justified by a rhetoric of commercial freedom that masked a practice of systematic extraction. Its critics — Burke above all — saw in it a new form of despotism more dangerous than the old because it was irresponsible: the Company’s directors in London were too far away to govern, and its servants in India were too unaccountable to be restrained.

“The East India Company is a delegation of the whole power and sovereignty of this kingdom sent into the East. It is a State in the disguise of a Merchant.” — Edmund Burke
