Warren Hastings

Warren Hastings (6 December 1732 – 22 August 1818) was the first Governor-General of Bengal (1773–1785) and the central administrator of the East India Company’s Indian possessions during a critical period of imperial consolidation. His thirteen-year administration laid the foundations of British rule in India — establishing systems of revenue collection, judicial administration, and diplomatic relations that would endure for generations. But his methods were controversial, his wars were expensive, and his enemies at home — led by Edmund Burke — succeeded in bringing him to trial on charges of extortion, corruption, and cruelty. The impeachment of Warren Hastings, stretching from 1787 to 1795, was the longest and most spectacular political trial of the eighteenth century. Hastings was eventually acquitted, but the trial established a new standard of accountability for imperial governance.

Early Career in India

Hastings was born in Churchill, Oxfordshire, the son of an impoverished clergyman who died when Warren was eight. He was educated at Westminster School — where he formed a lifelong attachment to classical learning — and at the age of seventeen sailed for India as a writer (a junior clerk) in the service of the East India Company.

He arrived in Calcutta in 1750, at a moment when the Company was still a merchant corporation with trading factories rather than a territorial power. The transformation of the Company from trader to sovereign was the work of Robert Clive; Hastings was, in many ways, Clive’s successor as the architect of British India. He rose through the Company’s ranks with remarkable speed. He was posted to the trading station at Cossimbazar in 1752 and, in 1756, was taken prisoner by the Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daula, during the Black Hole episode — though he was not among those confined in the infamous prison. He was appointed Resident at the court of the Nawab of Bengal in 1758, at the age of twenty-five, and became a member of the Calcutta Council in 1761. His early reports from Bengal — detailed, analytical, and pragmatic — marked him as the Company’s ablest political officer.

In 1764, Hastings returned to England, where he spent several years attempting to secure his position and his fortune. He found the Company’s directors suspicious of Indian experience and reluctant to entrust authority to those who had served on the spot. He remained in England until 1769, when, his finances exhausted, he secured appointment as second in Council at Madras. He was recalled to Bengal in 1772 as Governor of Bengal, charged by the Company with restoring order to an administration that had fallen into chaos and corruption in the years since Clive’s departure.

Governor of Bengal and the Reforms

Hastings arrived in Bengal at a moment of profound crisis. The Company’s servants had enriched themselves shamelessly during the decade of dual government after 1765 — the system by which the Company exercised the substance of power while leaving the Nawab with the shadow of authority. The Company’s treasury was empty, its credit exhausted, and its reputation ruined. Hastings was instructed to reform the administration at every level: the collection of revenue, the administration of justice, and the conduct of the Company’s servants.

His first great reform was the reorganisation of revenue collection. He abolished the dual system and established a Board of Revenue in Calcutta to supervise the collection of the land tax directly through Company officials. He introduced five-year settlements (the temporary settlement of 1772) under which the revenue demand was fixed for a limited period, giving the cultivators some security against arbitrary increases. The system was not yet the Permanent Settlement that would be introduced by Lord Cornwallis in 1793, and Hastings’s own views on revenue were less doctrinaire than Cornwallis’s: he believed that the Company’s right was to the surplus above what the cultivator needed for subsistence, and he was willing to negotiate with the great zemindars rather than impose a uniform system. But his reforms brought the revenue under Company control and regularised a system that had been riven with private profiteering.

His judicial reforms were equally ambitious. He established a system of courts — district diwani (civil) courts and faujdari (criminal) courts, with a Sadr Diwani Adalat and a Sadr Nizamat Adalat at Calcutta as courts of appeal. The courts were to apply Hindu law to Hindus and Muslim law to Muslims, with the Company’s regulations as the overarching framework. Hastings had a genuine respect for Indian civilisation and Indian legal traditions, and he engaged a group of pandits to compile a digest of Hindu law — the Code of Gentoo Laws — which was translated into English and published in 1776. This was no mere antiquarian exercise: it was an attempt to establish a legal order that Indians would recognise as their own, while subjecting that order to the ultimate authority of the Company’s courts.

The Supreme Council and the Conflicts

The Regulating Act of 1773 — passed by Lord North’s ministry in response to the Company’s financial crisis and the revelations of corruption — restructured the government of Bengal. Hastings was appointed Governor-General of Bengal, with authority over the presidencies of Madras and Bombay. But the Act also created a Supreme Council of four members — Philip Francis, Sir John Clavering, and Colonel George Monson — and a Supreme Court of Judicature at Calcutta, headed by Sir Elijah Impey. The intention was to check the arbitrary power of the Governor-General.

The result was a five-year war of attrition within the Council. Philip Francis — brilliant, ambitious, and convinced that Hastings was a tyrant — led a three-man majority on the Council that systematically opposed Hastings’s policies. Francis believed that the Company’s Indian possessions should be governed on the model of a Whig commonwealth: the zemindars (landlords) as a sort of Indian gentry, the ryots (cultivators) as English yeomen, and the Company as a benevolent sovereign guaranteeing property rights. Hastings, by contrast, saw India as it was: a patchwork of rival states, unstable alliances, and traditions of authority that bore no resemblance to English constitutionalism. In the clash between these two men — the orientalist pragmatist and the Whig idealist — lay the central ideological tension of British India.

The Council’s hostility forced Hastings into desperate expedients. The most notorious was the case of Nandakumar (Nuncomar), a wealthy Bengali Brahmin who had accused Hastings of accepting bribes. The Supreme Council — controlled by Francis — took up the accusation, but before it could be pursued, Nandakumar was himself arrested and tried for forgery before the Supreme Court under Sir Elijah Impey. He was convicted and hanged on 5 August 1775. Hastings’s enemies — then and since — charged that the trial was a judicial murder, orchestrated by Hastings to silence his accuser. The hanging of Nandakumar remained the darkest shadow over Hastings’s administration, and it was the foundation of the most serious charges in Burke’s impeachment.

Wars and Diplomacy

Hastings governed an empire that was perpetually at war. The Company’s possessions were surrounded by powers that had been weakened but not destroyed: the Maratha Confederacy, the Sultanate of Mysore under Hyder Ali, the Nizam of Hyderabad, and the great Mughal successor states. Hastings believed — with good reason — that an aggressive defence of the Company’s interests was the only way to survive.

The Rohilla War (1774) was his first major intervention. The Rohillas were Afghan settlers who had established a state in the territory between Oudh and the Maratha dominions. The Nawab of Oudh, Shuja-ud-Daula, coveted their territory; Hastings agreed to lend him Company troops in exchange for a payment of forty lakhs of rupees. The Rohillas were defeated, their country annexed to Oudh, and the Company collected its fee. The campaign was denounced by Hastings’s enemies as a war of aggression fought for money — a mercenary transaction that brought the Company’s honour into disrepute.

In 1781, Hastings faced a more formidable enemy. The Raja of Benares, Chait Singh — a vassal of the Company under the Vizier of Oudh — had fallen behind in his tribute. Hastings travelled to Benares to demand payment and, when Chait Singh resisted, ordered his arrest. The arrest provoked an insurrection: Chait Singh’s troops attacked the British party, Hastings barely escaped with his life, and a punitive military campaign was required to restore order. The Benares affair became another of Burke’s principal charges: Hastings, it was alleged, had provoked the insurrection by his greed and his brutality.

The same year saw the outbreak of war with Hyder Ali of Mysore, the most dangerous enemy the Company had faced since Clive’s day. Hyder Ali invaded the Carnatic in 1780, burning villages and advancing on Madras. Hastings responded with extraordinary vigour: he despatched a force under Sir Eyre Coote from Bengal to the Carnatic, arranged a naval diversion, and simultaneously negotiated with the Marathas for a separate peace to free his hands. The war was long and costly, but by 1784 the Company had secured its position. Hastings’s handling of the Mysore crisis — his ability to coordinate military and diplomatic operations across three thousand miles — was arguably his finest achievement as Governor-General.

The Impeachment

Hastings resigned the Governor-Generalship in 1785 and returned to England, expecting to be rewarded for his services. Instead, he walked into a political storm. The Whig opposition — led by Edmund Burke, Charles James Fox, and Richard Brinsley Sheridan — had been gathering evidence of the Company’s abuses in India, and they saw in Hastings the embodiment of everything that was wrong with British rule in the East.

Burke’s campaign against Hastings had been building since the early 1780s. He had studied the Indian evidence with extraordinary thoroughness, and he had come to believe that Hastings was not merely a corrupt administrator but a despot who had destroyed the ancient constitution of India. The charges were presented to the House of Commons in 1786, and on 10 May 1787, Hastings was formally impeached at the bar of the House of Lords.

The trial lasted 145 days over eight years — the longest in British history. Hastings appeared before the Lords in Westminster Hall, the same chamber that had witnessed the trial of Charles I and the coronation of the Hanoverian kings. The prosecution was conducted by the greatest orators of the age. Burke’s opening speech — delivered over four days — was a tremendous rhetorical performance, a moral indictment of imperialism that still resonates. He described Hastings as “a captain-general of iniquity,” a man who had “destroyed the whole framework of the Indian constitution” and “oppressed the weak with a rod of iron.” Sheridan’s speech on the Begums of Oudh was said by several witnesses to have been the finest oration ever delivered in the English Parliament; half the House was in tears.

Hastings conducted his own defence with dignity and skill. He was not a great orator, but he was a patient and methodical witness who could explain the complexities of Indian administration to a British audience. He argued that his actions had been necessary — that the Company’s survival in India had required the very measures that Burke denounced as crimes, and that the moral standards Whiggish gentlemen applied to English politics had no application to the desperate realities of Indian warfare.

The trial was terminated by Hastings’s acquittal on 23 April 1795. The Lords voted on each of the seventeen articles separately; on every count the verdict was not guilty. The cost had been ruinous — Hastings claimed to have spent £70,000 on his defence, a sum that left him in genteel poverty for the rest of his life.

Cultural Patronage and the Asiatic Society

Beyond politics, warfare, and impeachment, Warren Hastings left a more benign legacy in the realm of learning. He was one of the great patrons of Oriental scholarship in the eighteenth century — a man who believed, against the prevailing current of his age, that the civilisation of India was a subject worthy of serious study, and that the British would govern India better if they understood it.

He encouraged the study of Persian, Arabic, and Sanskrit by Company officials. He supported the compilation of the Code of Gentoo Laws, the translation of the Bhagavad Gita (the first English translation of the great Sanskrit epic was published under his patronage), and the foundation of the Madrasa at Calcutta for the study of Muslim law. In 1784, he gave his support to Sir William Jones in the founding of the Asiatic Society of Bengal — the first learned society in Asia devoted to the study of Oriental languages, history, and culture. Hastings’s vision of India was not — as Clive’s had been — simply a source of revenue and military power; it was a civilisation with its own laws, its own literature, and its own claim on the attention of educated Europeans.

Yet there was a tension at the heart of Hastings’s Orientalism. The same man who patronised Sanskrit scholarship also authorised the sale of the Begums of Oudh’s treasure; the same man who translated the Gita also sent Company troops to suppress the Rohillas for money. Hastings’s love of Indian civilisation was genuine, but it coexisted with a ruthlessness in the pursuit of Company interests that made him, in Burke’s phrase, “the most finished tyrant in history.”

Later Years and Legacy

After his acquittal, Hastings retired to Daylesford in Gloucestershire — an estate he had bought in 1788 with the intention of re-establishing the family seat that his ancestors had lost two centuries before. He spent his remaining years in obscurity, managing his estate and writing occasional memoranda on Indian affairs. He was granted an annuity by the East India Company, and in 1814, at the age of eighty-one, was summoned to give evidence before the House of Commons on Indian affairs — a moment of belated recognition from the institution he had served.

He died on 22 August 1818, at the age of eighty-five, and was buried in the church at Daylesford. His monument — designed by his admirers in the East India Company — stands in Westminster Abbey, opposite that of Robert Clive.

The legacy of Warren Hastings is deeply contested. To his admirers — and they were many, particularly among those who served in India — he was the founder of British India, the administrator who saved the Company from destruction, systematised its revenues, and established the principles of justice and order on which the Raj was built. To his critics, he was the embodiment of imperial arrogance — a man who had enriched himself and his masters by the systematic exploitation of the Indian people.

The trial itself was, in many ways, Hastings’s most enduring achievement. For the first time in British history, the conduct of an imperial governor was subjected to the full scrutiny of Parliament and the public. Burke’s prosecution established the principle that the British Empire was not merely a system of power and profit but a moral enterprise, answerable to the same standards of justice that governed the constitution at home. That Hastings was acquitted mattered less than the fact that the trial had taken place at all. The impeachment was the first great debate on the ethics of empire — a debate that would continue, in different forms, for the next two centuries.

“I saved India for England — and England impeached me for it.” — Attributed to Warren Hastings
