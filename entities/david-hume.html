David Hume

David Hume (7 May [NS] 1711 – 25 August 1776) was a Scottish philosopher, historian, and economist, the most formidable intellect of the British Enlightenment and the foundational thinker of classical economics. His Political Discourses (1752) laid the groundwork for the quantity theory of money, the price-specie-flow mechanism, and the critique of mercantilism a full generation before Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations. His History of England (1754–1762) — the first truly philosophical history of the nation — became the standard work for half a century. His epistemology and moral philosophy, set out in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), made him the most radical and systematic sceptic in the English-speaking world.

No figure of the eighteenth century better exemplifies the Scottish Enlightenment’s faith that the study of human nature — in its reason, its passions, its commerce, and its politics — could be reduced to principles as regular as those that govern the physical world.

Early Life and Intellectual Formation

Born in Edinburgh to a minor gentry family of the Borders, Hume was intended for the law but abandoned it for philosophy. “I found an unsurmountable aversion to every thing but the pursuits of philosophy and general learning,” he later wrote. He pursued that calling with an intensity and self-discipline that destroyed his health but produced, by the age of twenty-six, the manuscript of A Treatise of Human Nature — three books on the understanding, the passions, and morals that attempted nothing less than to do for the human sciences what Newton had done for the natural.

The Treatise “fell dead-born from the press,” as Hume himself put it — its radical scepticism about causation, personal identity, and the grounds of belief was too subtle and too unsettling for the public taste. It would take two decades of recasting his arguments in essay form before Hume achieved the recognition his genius deserved. But the Treatise — especially its account of human understanding and its demonstration that reason is and ought to be “the slave of the passions” — became the subterranean foundation of all his later work.

The Political Discourses (1752) and the Foundations of Classical Economics

Hume’s economic essays, collected in the Political Discourses of 1752, were his breakthrough work — the first systematic application of philosophical method to economic questions and the texts that made him a European reputation. Published eight years before the accession of George III, they engaged directly with the commercial questions that would dominate the second half of the century: the nature of money, the determination of interest, the balance of trade, the consequences of public debt, and the psychology of commercial society.

Of Money

Hume’s essay Of Money is the first classic statement of the quantity theory. Money, he insisted, is “not one of the subjects of commerce, but only the instrument which men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another” — a radical departure from the mercantilist conviction that specie was wealth itself. The absolute quantity of money in a kingdom was irrelevant to its real prosperity; prices adjusted proportionally. The only real advantage of abundant bullion, Hume drily observed, was in fighting foreign wars.

More important than the quantity theory itself was the mechanism by which Hume connected domestic money to international trade: the price-specie-flow mechanism. A nation that accumulated gold through a favourable balance of trade would find its prices rising, its exports becoming uncompetitive, and its specie flowing outward again until equilibrium was restored. The argument was not merely theoretical but polemical: it cut the ground from under every mercantilist policy designed to hoard precious metals by showing that such hoarding was self-defeating.

Hume expressed deep scepticism about paper credit and banks, which artificially inflated the money supply. His ideal — the 100% reserve bank that “locked up all the money it received” — was never adopted, but the questions he raised about the relationship between monetary expansion and price stability remain at the centre of monetary economics.

Of Interest

In Of Interest, Hume took direct aim at John Locke’s influential theory that the rate of interest was determined by the quantity of money — the more abundant the specie, the lower the interest. Hume argued instead that interest was governed by the rate of profit on mercantile capital. Low interest was a symptom of commercial flourishing, not monetary abundance. The three determinants of interest were: the demand for borrowing (driven by the profit to be made from borrowed capital), the supply of loanable funds (driven by the growth of commercial wealth), and the security of the lender (driven by the legal system). All three improved with commercial development — a diagnosis that harmonised perfectly with Joseph Massie’s independent work on the same subject from two years earlier.

Of the Balance of Trade

The most celebrated essay in the collection, Of the Balance of Trade, is a devastating critique of the mercantilist panic that a nation’s gold could be permanently drained away by an unfavourable trade balance. Hume’s four great arguments have never been bettered: (1) the automatic mechanism of specie-flow makes any persistent drain impossible; (2) no nation can permanently retain all the world’s gold; (3) the prosperity of trading partners is an opportunity, not a threat; (4) policies enacted against the natural course of commerce hurt the nation they are meant to protect. The essay is a masterpiece of economic reasoning expressed in prose of enduring elegance — the philosophical essayist at the height of his powers.

Of the Jealousy of Trade

A companion piece that extends the argument to the political level. The prosperity of a neighbouring nation is not a zero-sum loss but a stimulus to industry and demand everywhere. The essay is a plea for free trade before the term existed, and it reflects Hume’s deepest conviction about human nature: that the passions, including the commercial passions, contain their own method of correction if only legislators are wise enough to let them operate.

Of Public Credit

Hume’s most prescient essay and the one that speaks most directly to the financial history of the later eighteenth century. He argued that national debts, if they reached a certain magnitude, must inevitably end in national bankruptcy. The system of funding — borrowing in perpetuity rather than taxing directly — was a modern invention that “mortgaged the public revenues” and gave a dangerous concentration of power to the monied interest. His warning — that either the nation would destroy public credit or public credit would destroy the nation — was quoted by every subsequent generation of critics of the national debt, from the pamphleteers of the American Revolution to the post-Napoleonic debates on fiscal retrenchment. It is the essay that connects him most directly to the fiscal controversies documented in the East India Company and Bank of England materials of this wiki.

The History of England

After the success of the Political Discourses, Hume turned to the project that would make him financially independent: a complete History of England from the invasion of Julius Caesar to the Glorious Revolution. Published in six volumes between 1754 and 1762, the History was the first major English historical work to be written from a philosophical standpoint — to trace not merely events but the development of the constitution, the growth of commerce, and the progress of manners and government.

The History made him famous and, for the first time, comfortable. It went through many editions and remained the standard English history until Macaulay’s. Its theme — the gradual emergence of ordered liberty from the clash of contending forces — reflected Hume’s mature political philosophy: sceptical of both Whig and Tory dogmas, suspicious of abstract rights, convinced that the English constitution worked not because it was rational but because it had grown.

Hume and Adam Smith

The friendship between Hume and Adam Smith is one of the great intellectual partnerships of the eighteenth century. They met in Edinburgh in the 1740s or early 1750s, and from that meeting developed a mutual regard that survived the attacks of the orthodox (who suspected both of infidelity) and the test of time. When Smith published The Theory of Moral Sentiments in 1759, Hume wrote a generous public notice. When Hume lay dying of a painful intestinal cancer in 1776, Smith visited him in Edinburgh and found him reading Lucian and joking about the fables of immortality.

Smith cared for Hume’s posthumous reputation as if it were his own. His famous account of Hume’s death — published in the face of furious clerical opposition — described “the entire philosophical composure of a man who, approaching his end with perfect equanimity, had nothing to reproach himself with but his benevolence, his integrity, and his innocent and even exemplary conduct.” The portrait drew a storm of abuse against Smith — but it also ensured that Hume’s serene death entered the legend of the Scottish Enlightenment.

The intellectual debt of the Wealth of Nations to the Political Discourses is deep and acknowledged. Smith took from Hume the core of his monetary theory, his critique of mercantilism, and his conviction that commerce and liberty advanced together. The price-specie-flow mechanism, the attack on the balance-of-trade fallacy, the analysis of public credit — all were Hume’s before they were Smith’s. The younger man’s genius was to systematise what Hume had suggested, to build from the essayist’s brilliant intuitions the architecture of a complete system of political economy.

Legacy

Hume died in Edinburgh on 25 August 1776, in the same year that the Wealth of Nations was published and the American Revolutionary War began — two events that would unfold the consequences of his thinking across the Atlantic world. His economic essays became the foundation of classical monetary theory. His History of England shaped the historical consciousness of a generation. His philosophical scepticism — his assault on causation, on the self, on the rational foundations of belief — remains a living challenge to philosophy after nearly three centuries.

But it is as an economist that Hume matters most to this wiki. The Political Discourses are the intellectual lens through which the commercial politics of eighteenth-century Britain — the debates over the national debt, the balance of trade, the colonies, and public credit — can be understood at their deepest level. Hume gave the age its economic vocabulary and its analytic framework. Everything that followed was commentary.

“It is not by augmenting the mass of money, which is always a matter of indifference, but by increasing the quantity of commodities and the labour of industry, that any nation can increase its real power and wealth.”
