Robert Walpole, 1st Earl of Orford

Robert Walpole (1676–1745) was the dominant British politician of the early eighteenth century, widely regarded as the first de facto Prime Minister of Great Britain. His tenure at the head of government spanned over twenty years (1721–1742), a period of sustained Whig supremacy punctuated by the aftershocks of the South Sea Company collapse, the consolidation of the Hanoverian dynasty, and a careful foreign policy that kept Britain out of major continental wars.

Early Career and Rise

Born at Houghton Hall in Norfolk on 26 August 1676, Walpole entered Parliament in 1701 as a Whig member for Castle Rising. He quickly distinguished himself as a skilled debater and capable administrator. Under Queen Anne he served as Secretary at War (1708–1710) and Treasurer of the Navy, earning a reputation for financial competence. The shift in political fortunes that brought the Tories to power in 1710 saw Walpole expelled from the House and briefly imprisoned in the Tower of London on charges of corruption — an experience that forged his lifelong wariness of political zealotry.

The accession of George I in 1714 swept the Whigs back into power. Walpole became Paymaster General of the Forces and, by 1715, First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer. His management of the Commons during the Jacobite Rising of 1715 proved indispensable to the new dynasty.

The South Sea Crisis and Ascendancy to Power

The financial catastrophe of the South Sea Company in 1720 was Walpole’s making. While deeply entangled in the speculative frenzy — he had profited handsomely from stock purchases and sales in 1719–1720 — he positioned himself adroitly during the crash. When the bubble burst in September 1720, stock plummeting from £1,000 to £150, Walpole proposed a scheme to restore public credit by incorporating the South Sea stock into the Bank of England and the East India Company. His plan, though only partially adopted, stabilised a nation teetering on the brink of fiscal collapse.

The scandal destroyed the ministry of the Earl of Sunderland and the influence of the South Sea directors. Walpole emerged as the indispensable man. By April 1721 he was First Lord of the Treasury, Chancellor of the Exchequer, and effectively the chief minister of the Crown — a position he would not relinquish for two decades.

The Long Ministry (1721–1742)

Walpole’s supremacy was founded on a three-pillared system: the confidence of the Crown, the management of the House of Commons through patronage, and a foreign policy of peace that suited a tax-weary nation. He cultivated the favour of both George I and George II, the latter through the critical support of Queen Caroline. His control of patronage was legendary — he distributed places, pensions, and sinecures with systematic precision, building what his opponents decried as a “system of corruption” but what he defended as the necessary oil of government.

Domestic Policy

Walpole’s financial administration was marked by prudence. He established the Sinking Fund in 1716 (later plundered for current expenditure), reduced the national debt, and pursued a policy of low land taxes that endeared him to the landed gentry. His most controversial domestic measure was the Excise Bill of 1733, which proposed extending the excise system to tobacco and wine. A ferocious popular outcry — orchestrated by his Tory and dissident Whig opponents, notably William Pitt the Elder and Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke — forced him to withdraw the bill. It was the most serious defeat of his domestic career.

He fended off repeated attempts by the opposition to reduce the standing army, limit placement in Parliament, and investigate his administration. The Septennial Act, which extended the maximum duration of Parliaments from three to seven years, was a key weapon in his arsenal, reducing electoral uncertainty and the cost of management.

Foreign Policy

Walpole’s guiding principle abroad was peace — a policy that won him the gratitude of the commercial classes but the contempt of those who saw Britain’s interests served by a more aggressive posture. He secured the Treaty of Hanover (1725), managed the diplomatic isolation of Spain, and kept Britain out of the War of the Polish Succession. This pacific policy was increasingly strained in the late 1730s by Spanish depredations on British shipping, epitomised by the infamous Jenkins’s Ear incident of 1731.

The Fall

The clamour for war with Spain became irresistible. Walpole, against his better judgment, yielded to public pressure and declared war against Spain in October 1739 — the War of Jenkins’s Ear. “They are ringing the bells now,” he remarked bitterly, “but they will soon be wringing their hands.” The war went badly from the outset: Admiral Edward Vernon’s capture of Porto Bello in November 1739 was the sole bright spot in a catalogue of mismanaged campaigns.

Weakened by the war, Walpole faced a consolidated opposition led by Pulteney, Sandys, and the rising star William Pitt. In February 1742, following a poor showing in a by-election at Chippenham and a narrow defeat on the Chippenham petition, he resigned as First Lord and accepted a peerage as Earl of Orford.

Character and Legacy

Walpole was a man of coarse manners but sharp intellect, a pragmatist who despised ideology. His famous aphorism — “Every man has his price” — encapsulates his cynical yet effective approach to political management. He was an able financier, a master of parliamentary procedure, and the architect of the Whig oligarchy that would dominate Britain for generations.

His son, Horace Walpole, became one of the great literary figures of the age. His younger son, also Sir Robert Walpole, continued the family political tradition.

Although reviled by contemporaries as the “screen-master general” and the “corrupt knight,” Walpole’s historical reputation has been reassessed. He gave Britain two decades of stability, consolidated the Hanoverian succession, and established the office of Prime Minister as a working reality of the British constitution — even if the title was not yet recognised in law.

“The balance of power” — Walpole’s foreign policy watchword — became the guiding principle of eighteenth-century British statecraft.
