Malachy Postlethwayt

Malachy Postlethwayt (c. 1707 – 1767) was the leading English commercial writer of the mid-eighteenth century, the author of The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce (1751–1755) — the first comprehensive English work of its kind and a monument of eighteenth-century commercial knowledge — and Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved (1757), the most ambitious single treatise on British commercial policy published during the Seven Years’ War. He was the principal English mediator of the French tradition of commercial administration, the most systematic mercantilist thinker of his generation, and a source cited by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations.

Postlethwayt’s dictionary, in its four editions, stands to the commerce of the eighteenth century roughly as Diderot’s Encyclopédie stands to its philosophy: a vast, sprawling, practical, and fiercely partisan attempt to bring all the knowledge of a subject — trade, manufacture, navigation, finance, law — between two folio covers. It was the book to which merchants, magistrates, and ministers turned when they needed to know the law of bills of exchange, the customs duties on tobacco, the regulations of the East India Company, or the principles of marine insurance.

Life and Formation

Remarkably little is known of Postlethwayt’s personal history. He was born around 1707, probably in London, into a family of modest means. Unlike the university-trained economists of the Scottish Enlightenment — his near-contemporaries David Hume and Adam Smith — Postlethwayt learned his trade in the counting-house and the custom-house. He seems to have worked as a clerk or accountant in a commercial office, perhaps in the customs service itself, and his writings are suffused with the practical, ground-level knowledge of a man who has handled bills of lading, calculated exchange rates, and read the endless regulations of the excise. His Latin was imperfect but his knowledge of ledgers was formidable.

The decisive event of his intellectual life was the discovery of Jacques Savary des Brûlons’s Dictionnaire universel de commerce (1723–1730), the great French compendium of commercial knowledge commissioned from Savary by the French Bureau of Commerce. No English equivalent existed. English merchants relied on scattered pamphlets, oral tradition, and the notoriously opaque statutes of trade and navigation. Postlethwayt resolved to remedy the deficiency — a project that consumed the better part of his life and produced the work by which he is remembered.

He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1763 — a recognition of his contributions to commercial knowledge — and died four years later, in 1767, before he could see the fourth edition of his dictionary updated to reflect the Treaty of Paris.

The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce (1751–1755; 4th ed. 1774)

The Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce was published in instalments between 1751 and 1755, in two large folio volumes covering the alphabet from A to Z. It was modelled on Savary but substantially expanded and redirected toward English conditions. Where Savary wrote for the French official — the intendant, the customs officer, the commercial judge — Postlethwayt wrote for the English merchant, the magistrate, the Member of Parliament, and the would-be political arithmetician. The fourth edition, published seven years after Postlethwayt’s death by a consortium of twenty-two London booksellers, included extensive post-war updates reflecting the territorial changes of the Treaty of Paris (1763).

A Monument of Commercial Knowledge

The dictionary is far more than an alphabetical reference. It opens with an extended “General Idea of the Contents” — approximately 150 numbered sections that function as an independent treatise on the scope of commercial knowledge — followed by a philosophical introduction on the relationship between commerce, civilisation, and national character, and two Preliminary Discourses addressed to Parliament on the commercial grievances of the nation.

The dictionary’s range is staggering. The entry on the Bank of England includes a complete double-entry ledger for a model banking business, showing every account a banker would keep — South Sea stock at 115, India stock at 187, a bottomry loan to the captain of an Indiaman at 40%, a privateer adventure returning six times its investment. The entry on marine insurance reproduces a complete specimen policy — the perils clause, the sue-and-labour clause, the Lombard Street standard clause, the convoy warranty — and reports over twenty legal cases from the Admiralty courts. The CUSTOM-HOUSE entry, which runs across multiple letters, is a complete administrative manual for the English customs service, reproducing actual forms from the Southampton custom-house of 1730. BILLS OF EXCHANGE covers the law and practice of five jurisdictions. The entry on the East India Company reproduces the whole statutory framework from William III to George II.

The dictionary also provides the most detailed available picture of the institutional structure of London commerce. The COMPANIES entry catalogues the twelve great livery companies of the City of London with their incorporation dates and founding monarchs — the Mercers (1393), the Grocers (1345), the Drapers, the Fishmongers, the Goldsmiths, the Skinners (1327), the Merchant Taylors (1501), and the rest — and traces the historical development of the great chartered trading companies from the Merchant Adventurers to the East India Company. Postlethwayt notes with remarkable candour the historical shift from exclusive companies to free trade: “experience hath shewn, that the trade of the nation has advanced, in proportion as monopolies have been laid aside.”

Philosophical Foundations: Commerce as the Soul of Empire

The introduction to the dictionary is a sustained philosophical essay — surprising in a work of practical reference — on the moral and civilisational foundations of commerce. “The exercise of trade and commerce,” Postlethwayt writes, “is the soul of the whole British empire.” He traces the fall of great empires — Assyria, Persia, Rome, the Ottomans — to idleness and luxury, and argues that commerce alone civilises: “What would our gentry, our nobility, or our sovereign be better than those of Barbary, was it not for our superiority of traffic and navigation?”

The most revealing passage is his appeal to the English nobility and gentry to abandon the rentier mentality and devote themselves to practical and experimental philosophy. Drawing on Bishop Spratt’s History of the Royal Society, he urges that their country seats, “being removed from the tumult of cities, give them the best opportunity and freedom of observation.” When a general turn for useful inventions takes root among the landed class, he argues, the improvement of the nation’s manufactures and agriculture will follow. It is a distinctively English argument — one that the French intendants, with their centralised bureaucracy, would not have needed to make, and one that reflects Postlethwayt’s deep frustration with the neglect of the English regulatory tradition.

The Dictionary and Smith

Adam Smith cited Postlethwayt’s dictionary in The Wealth of Nations — a testament to its standing as the standard English reference on commercial matters. Smith, of course, rejected the mercantilist framework that the dictionary systematically elaborated. The Wealth of Nations is, among other things, a demolition of the intellectual system that Postlethwayt represented. But Smith read Postlethwayt carefully and used his data. The dictionary provided the empirical foundation — the customs procedures, the navigation acts, the banking regulations, the insurance practices — that the classical economists would reinterpret through the lens of their own theory.

Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved (1757)

If the dictionary is Postlethwayt’s encyclopaedia, Britain’s Commercial Interest Explained and Improved is his manifesto — a collection of nineteen political-economic dissertations published in the third year of the Seven Years’ War, when Britain was at its lowest point. The book opens in near-despair: “the nation is, at present, reduced to a very melancholy state,” destitute of “all maritime allies.” The diplomatic revolution of 1756 had left Britain isolated in Europe; the loss of Minorca, the execution of Admiral Byng, and Braddock’s defeat on the Monongahela had revealed a military and administrative system in crisis.

The Central Diagnosis: Under-Cultivation

Postlethwayt’s argument in the 1757 treatise is structural and, in a surprising sense, supply-side. Britain’s competitive decline, he argues, stems from the under-cultivation of land. He calculates that England contains approximately 62,000 square miles of productive land, of which only about 31,000 square miles — roughly half — are under cultivation. The solution is not more regulation or more protection (the orthodox mercantilist prescription) but more food and raw materials: bring waste lands into cultivation → lower grain prices → lower labour costs → cheaper manufactures → restored export competitiveness.

The calculation is primitive by modern standards — Postlethwayt’s area estimates derive from Sir William Petty’s political arithmetic — but the direction of the argument is remarkable. Postlethwayt, the arch-mercantilist, had arrived at an essentially anti-mercantilist conclusion: that the real constraint on British trade was not foreign competition but domestic under-investment in productive capacity.

The Case for an Imperial System

The book is at its most original in the second half — the dissertations on Ireland and the American colonies that together occupy nearly three hundred pages. Postlethwayt argues for a legislative union between Great Britain and Ireland (forty-four years before the Act of Union of 1801) on thoroughly economic grounds: it would prevent the smuggling of Irish wool to France, integrate Irish agriculture with British markets, remove the destructive trade restrictions that harmed both kingdoms, and follow the successful model of 1707. The argument is a sustained critique of English policy in Ireland, which Postlethwayt describes in remarkably blunt terms: “England has scarce ever exercised her power in commercial affairs but she has suffered for it.”

In the American dissertations, Postlethwayt analyses the colonies by their form of government — crown, proprietary, and charter — and warns that the colonies have developed in ways that undermine imperial unity. He urges a “happy general union” of “all his Majesty’s dominions, no less constitutional than commercial.” The argument is among the earliest systematic cases for what would later be called imperial federation. The timing — 1757 — is poignant: within twenty years, the British American colonies would be in open rebellion.

Critique of the Slave Trade

Postlethwayt’s treatment of the African trade is among the most revealing passages in his work. Unlike many writers who defended the slave trade as a commercial necessity, Postlethwayt criticised it on economic grounds. In the introduction to the dictionary, he argues that the African peoples “could be gradually nurtured in all the arts of humanity” through trade rather than slavery, and that the failure to develop the African interior trade — the “inland trade” in commodities rather than human beings — was one of Britain’s great missed opportunities. “What avails the insignificant advantage we have received by driving the French out of the river Senegal,” he asks, “in comparison to the great and important designs that have been proposed by the author, to enlarge and extend the whole of that commerce in times of peace?”

The argument is not humanitarian — Postlethwayt was a mercantilist, not an abolitionist — but it is genuinely economic. He believed that Africa could produce valuable commodities (gold, ivory, gum, dyewoods) in quantities that would more than compensate for the loss of the slave trade, and that the British commercial interest lay in developing these trades rather than in supplying enslaved labour to the American plantations. This position, unusual in the mid-eighteenth century but not unique, anticipates the economic arguments against the slave trade that would gain ground in the 1780s and 1790s.

Mercantilist System and Its Limits

Postlethwayt was the most systematic English mercantilist of his generation. His work is saturated with the assumptions of the old colonial system: that trade was a zero-sum competition among nations, that the state had a duty to regulate commerce, that the colonies existed for the benefit of the mother country, that the balance of trade was the measure of national prosperity. The dictionary is, among other things, a comprehensive handbook of the mercantile system — a record of the regulations, prohibitions, duties, drawbacks, bounties, and navigation acts that constituted the regulatory framework of the eighteenth-century British empire.

Yet Postlethwayt was not a dogmatic mercantilist. His arguments for the cultivation of waste land, his criticism of the slave trade, his endorsement of free trade within the empire, and his sympathy for the liberalisation of Irish commerce all strain against the boundaries of the system he nominally defended. The tension is productive: Postlethwayt’s work shows mercantilism not as a static doctrine but as a living, internally contested tradition, capable of generating reformist arguments even while defending the fundamental structure of the old colonial order.

Legacy and Significance

Postlethwayt died in 1767, just as the world he had described was beginning to come apart. The dictionary’s fourth edition (1774) appeared on the eve of the American Revolutionary War — the very event that Postlethwayt’s argument for commercial union had been designed to prevent. His framework of imperial integration was overtaken by the independence of the American colonies, the development of classical economics, and the industrial transformation of the British economy.

His influence on Smith, though largely negative — Smith cited the dictionary’s facts while rejecting its theory — was genuine. The Wealth of Nations would not have been possible without the accumulated commercial knowledge that Postlethwayt had systematised. Smith’s critique of the mercantile system gained its force partly from the completeness with which Postlethwayt had set forth the system that Smith was attacking.

Postlethwayt’s true legacy is the dictionary itself — the great folio volumes that preserved the practical knowledge of eighteenth-century commerce for posterity. For the historian of the period, the Universal Dictionary of Trade and Commerce is indispensable: it is the book that tells you how much a guinea was worth, how a bill of exchange was drawn, what the customs duties on tobacco were, how the Bank of Amsterdam operated, and what a merchant in 1750 would have known about the trade of China, the regulations of the French silk industry, and the premium for insuring a ship on a voyage to St Petersburg. In the great ledger of eighteenth-century intellectual life, Postlethwayt is the bookkeeper — and his account of Britain’s commercial civilisation remains open, waiting for readers who know how to read a balance sheet.
