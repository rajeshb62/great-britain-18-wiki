Joseph Massie

Joseph Massie (died 1784) was an English economic pamphleteer and bibliographer, the most penetrating writer on the theory of interest in the period between John Locke and Adam Smith. His only surviving theoretical work, An Essay on the Governing Causes of the Natural Rate of Interest (1750), established the principle that the rate of interest is governed by the rate of commercial profit — a theory that David Hume independently confirmed two years later and that Smith built into the foundation of the Wealth of Nations. Massie also compiled the most important bibliography of pre-Smithian economic literature, the Alphabetical and Chronological Index of Commercial Books and Pamphlets (1764), which catalogued 2,377 items and remains the essential guide to the intellectual landscape of eighteenth-century British economic writing.

Massie is the great shadow of the pre-Smithian tradition: widely read in his own day by those who could find his pamphlets, cited by contemporaries who absorbed his arguments, but neglected by posterity until the German historical economist Wilhelm Roscher and the American editor Jacob H. Hollander restored him to his rightful place in the history of economic thought.

Life and Career

Remarkably little is known of Massie’s life — an obscurity that is itself a measure of the distance between the eighteenth-century world of Grub Street pamphleteering and the institutionalised intellectual world that succeeded it. The standard reference works disagree on the date and place of his birth. He was active as a writer between 1750 and 1765, producing what must have been dozens of pamphlets on the commercial questions of the day. By 1760 he had completed the research for the Index of Commercial Books and Pamphlets, the bibliography that is his most enduring tangible legacy.

In 1760, in the midst of the Seven Years’ War, Massie approached the Treasury with a proposal of stunning ambition: to write a “commercial history of Great Britain” and a treatise on the “elements of commerce illustrated by Applications” — a complete account of British economic development with systematic policy recommendations. The proposal was declined. Britain’s commercial history and the elements of commerce would be written instead by Adam Smith, sixteen years later, building on foundations that Massie had helped to lay.

Massie seems to have been connected, at least indirectly, to the intellectual circle that gathered around David Hume in Edinburgh and London in the 1750s. The two men shared a deep interest in the theory of interest, a scepticism about the quantity-theory-of-money explanations that had dominated the Locke-Petty tradition, and a commitment to reasoning from empirical evidence rather than a priori maxims. Whether they corresponded or met is uncertain — but their arguments converge so closely that it is difficult to believe they were unaware of one another.

An Essay on the Natural Rate of Interest (1750)

Massie’s pamphlet is a landmark in the history of interest theory. The problem he addressed was fundamental: what determines the price of borrowed money? The orthodox answer, derived from Sir William Petty and most rigorously from Locke, was that interest was governed by the quantity of money — scarce money meant high interest, abundant money meant low interest. The legal rate of interest, which had fallen steadily in England (from 10% under Henry VIII to 5% under Anne), was taken as confirming evidence: as the nation had grown wealthier and money more plentiful, interest had naturally fallen.

The Critique of Locke

Massie subjected Locke’s theory to a systematic empirical refutation that remains a model of applied economic reasoning. He compiled a table of English wheat prices and interest rates across five historical periods, using the price of wheat as a proxy for the value of money — and hence for the quantity of money relative to trade. If Locke’s theory were correct, periods when money was dear (wheat cheap) should have had high interest, and periods when money was abundant (wheat dear) should have had low interest.

The data flatly contradicted the prediction. In 1646–1665, when wheat was at £2 17s 5.5d per quarter (indicating abundant money), interest should have been about 2% on Locke’s theory — but it was actually 7%. In the 1666–1705 period, when money was becoming scarcer relative to trade (wheat falling), interest should have risen — but it actually fell. Only the earliest period (1444–1460) was consistent with Locke’s rule. Massie drove the point home with a reductio ad absurdum: if the rule were followed back to earlier periods, interest would have been 14%, 28%, 56%, even 112% — a rate “no one can imagine any Man ever paid.”

Massie’s second critique was even more fundamental. Locke had argued that debts governed interest — that the more borrowers competed for scarce money, the higher the rate would go. Massie pointed out that the enormous increase in both private and public debts since 1600 should, on this logic, have made interest far higher — yet it had fallen by half. The government’s borrowing rate fluctuated wildly with political events (the 1745 rebellion raised it), not with the quantity of debt. What looked like interest on risky loans was partly a premium of risk — an insurance against default — that had nothing to do with the “natural” rate.

The Positive Theory: Interest as a Share of Profit

Massie’s own theory was simple and powerful: the natural rate of interest is governed by the profits of trade. A man borrows £100 only because the use of that £100 in his trade can produce a profit exceeding the interest he must pay. The lender and the borrower divide the profit between them — “the one Half to the Lender, as his Reward for giving the other a greater Opportunity of employing his Money, and the other Half to the Borrower, as an Equivalent for the Time he spends and the Risque he runs in employing it.” The equitable division, Massie thought, was roughly equal: one half to the lender as interest, one half to the borrower as the reward for his labour and risk.

This was not merely a theory of individual bargaining but a general law governing the whole economy. The rate of profit itself was determined by the proportion of the number of traders to the quantity of trade — a rudimentary but rigorous statement of the theory of competition. Where many traders compete for the same volume of trade, profits are low; where few traders serve a large market, profits are high. And profits in turn determine interest: Holland, with the most traders per capita, had the lowest interest (3%); Great Britain (4%) and France (5–6%) followed in descending order of commercial density; Turkey, with the fewest traders, had the highest interest (10–12%).

The Ultimate Causes: Government and Property

Massie pushed the analysis one level deeper. What determined the number of traders and the quantity of trade? His answer was institutional: government and the security of property. “The first and greatest Motive to trade is Necessity” — the inability of any man to supply all his own wants. But beyond this subsistence motive, the quantity of trade depends on whether men have a “Prospect of enjoying hereafter the Fruits of their present Industry.” Free governments that protect property rights — Holland and Britain — produce more traders, more trade, lower profits, and lower interest. Despotic governments — Turkey, Spain — produce fewer traders, less investment, higher profits, and higher interest.

The argument is a remarkable anticipation of the institutional economics of the twentieth century. Massie was not merely theorising about interest; he was offering a theory of economic development in which the quality of political institutions is the decisive variable. The connection between free government, commercial prosperity, and low interest was not a coincidence but a causal chain.

On Public Credit

Massie’s treatment of government borrowing, though a digression from his main argument, deserves separate notice. He argued that the government’s borrowing rate was irrelevant to the natural rate of interest — “All Reasoning about natural Interest from the Rate which the Government pays for Money, is, and unavoidably must be fallacious” — because government borrowing is “founded in Necessity” (which has no rational limit) while natural interest is “founded in Profit” (which does). In peacetime, the government could borrow below the natural rate because its credit stood higher than any private borrower’s. But his most striking claim — and the one that connects him to the fiscal debates of the later century — was that paying off the national debt would raise interest rates, because the portion owed to foreigners (Dutch, Swiss investors) would have to be paid in coin or bullion, draining money from domestic trade.

Whether or not the argument is sound — it depends on the monetary assumptions of the specie-flow model — it shows Massie thinking at the same level of sophistication as Hume and Smith, and on the same set of questions.

The Index of Commercial Books and Pamphlets (1764)

Massie’s other major achievement — and the one that gives him a permanent place in the intellectual history of the eighteenth century — was his Alphabetical and Chronological Index of Commercial Books and Pamphlets, a bibliography of 2,377 items covering English economic literature from the Reformation to the 1760s. The Index is a work of extraordinary learning and organisation, the product of years of collecting and categorising.

The Index is not merely a list of books; it is a map of the intellectual terrain that the Wealth of Nations would later conquer. It reveals the extraordinary richness and diversity of the pre-Smithian tradition: the debates on the balance of trade, the national debt, the East India trade, the Irish wool question, the usury laws, the colonies, the banking system. Every pamphlet that Smith read (or might have read) is there. Every argument that he synthesised, refuted, or transcended is catalogued. The Index shows that Smith was not the lonely genius of classical mythology but the systematiser of a tradition that had been developing for two centuries, and that some of the most important arguments of the Wealth of Nations — the theory of interest, the critique of the balance of trade, the analysis of the division of labour — had been prefigured in the pamphlet literature that Massie knew better than anyone.

Relationship to Hume and Smith

Massie’s relationship to the great Scottish economists is the central question about his place in the history of ideas. The chronological priority is clear: his essay on interest was published in 1750; Hume’s Political Discourses appeared in 1752; Smith’s Wealth of Nations in 1776. The intellectual affinity is also clear: all three argued that interest is governed by profit, not by the quantity of money.

Roscher, the great German historical economist of the nineteenth century, wrote that Massie had refuted Locke’s theory of interest “in a way which two years later Hume repeated with greater simplicity but no more force.” The judgement is generous to Massie and perhaps a little grudging to Hume — Hume’s essay is more elegantly written and more widely circulated — but it captures the essential fact of priority. Massie was there first, and his argument stands on its own merits.

Smith’s debt to Massie is harder to trace directly — Smith cited a wide range of earlier writers but only occasionally mentioned pamphlet writers by name. But the structure of Smith’s argument on interest in Book II of the Wealth of Nations — the relationship between profit and interest, the role of competition in determining both, the connection between commercial development and falling rates — is recognisably the structure that Massie had built twenty-six years earlier. Whether Smith had read Massie’s pamphlet or absorbed its arguments through the medium of the Edinburgh-London intellectual conversation, the continuity is unmistakable.

Legacy

Massie died in 1784, his great project for a “commercial history of Great Britain” unfulfilled, his pamphlets scattered and mostly lost, his name forgotten by all but the most dedicated antiquarians of economic literature. The Essay on the Natural Rate of Interest was out of print by 1758, within eight years of its publication. The Index of Commercial Books and Pamphlets survived in manuscript and was consulted by a few scholars, but its author was not one of them.

His rediscovery began in the nineteenth century with Roscher, who recognised the originality of Massie’s contribution to interest theory. In 1912, Jacob H. Hollander — the great Johns Hopkins economist and collector of early economic texts — reprinted the Essay with a learned introduction, making it available for the first time in over 150 years. Modern historians of economic thought have assigned Massie a secure if minor place in the canon — the man who, two years before Hume and a generation before Smith, solved the problem of interest that had defeated Locke and Petty.

But Massie deserves more than a footnote. His theory of interest as a share of profit, his empirical methodology, and — above all — his insistence that economic phenomena must be traced back to their institutional determinants (the quality of government, the security of property) give him a claim to be considered one of the founding minds of the science of political economy. He wrote in the pamphlet form that the eighteenth century used for its most serious intellectual work, and he wrote better than most. His essay belongs alongside Hume’s Political Discourses as a foundational text of the classical tradition.
