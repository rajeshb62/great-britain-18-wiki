Prince William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland

Prince William Augustus, Duke of Cumberland (15 April 1721 – 31 October 1765), was the younger son of George II and the most controversial British general of the eighteenth century. He was the victor of Culloden, which crushed the Jacobite Rising of 1745 and earned him his enduring nickname — “Butcher Cumberland.” His career encompassed the last pitched battle on British soil, two major defeats in the War of the Austrian Succession at the hands of Marshal Saxe, and the humiliation of the Convention of Klosterzeven. He was a commander of undoubted energy and courage, yet his legacy is stained by the savagery of the Highland pacification that followed Culloden.

Early Life and Military Education

Born at Leicester House in London, William Augustus was the third son and favourite child of George II and Queen Caroline. His elder brother Frederick, Prince of Wales, was estranged from the King, and the young Duke — as his father’s favourite — was groomed for a military career from childhood. At the age of four he was made a Knight of the Garter; at twelve he was appointed colonel of a regiment of foot guards. The speed of his advancement was dizzying even by the standards of royal preferment, but Cumberland was no mere ceremonial soldier. He was drilled in fortification at Hanover, studied under the Prussian military theorist General von Hülsen, and devoured the campaigns of Marlborough and Prince Eugene. He had a genuine passion for the profession of arms, and his father, who had led troops at Oudenarde and would soon lead them again at Dettingen, encouraged it.

The War of the Austrian Succession

Dettingen (1743)

Cumberland’s first taste of battle came at the Battle of Dettingen on 27 June 1743, where he served as a major-general at the age of twenty-two, fighting at his father’s side. The Allied army — a composite force of British, Hanoverian, and Austrian troops — was caught in a defile between the River Main and the wooded hills of the Spessart by the French army under Marshal Noailles. George II, sword drawn, led from the front; Cumberland commanded the right wing and was wounded in the leg by a musket ball, but remained in the saddle until the French were driven back across the river. The victory was celebrated throughout Britain — Handel composed the Dettingen Te Deum in its honour — and Cumberland returned home a hero.

Fontenoy (1745)

His next major command was a catastrophe. In April 1745, Cumberland was given command of the Allied army in Flanders — 50,000 British, Austrian, Hanoverian, and Dutch troops — with orders to relieve the French siege of Tournai. On 11 May, he assaulted the French position at Fontenoy, a fortified line anchored on the village of Antoing and the wood of Barry, defended by Marshal Saxe in his finest defensive battle.

The battle is remembered for one of the most extraordinary episodes in military history. The British infantry, advancing in perfect order across the open ground, marched to within fifty yards of the French line and halted. Their officers raised their hats. The French officers, in a gesture of mutual recognition, returned the salute. The British commander — Lord Charles Hay — stepped forward and invited the French Guards to fire first. “We are the English Guards,” he said, “and we hope you will stand till we come up to you.”

The French volley that followed was destructive, but the British reply was devastating. The disciplined volleys of the redcoats shattered the French centre, and the British columns pushed deep into the French position. For a moment, victory was within Cumberland’s grasp. But Saxe had held his cavalry in reserve, and the Dutch and Austrian auxiliaries on Cumberland’s flanks failed to support the British advance. The French cavalry — the household gendarmerie and the carabiniers — charged into the flanks of the penetrating columns. The British infantry, isolated and outnumbered, were driven back with terrible loss: 7,000 men killed or wounded, a third of Cumberland’s army.

Fontenoy was the defining defeat of Cumberland’s early career. He had fought bravely — two horses were shot under him — but his tactical handling of the battle, particularly his reliance on a frontal attack against prepared defences, was roundly criticised. The French went on to conquer the Austrian Netherlands; Tournai fell; and the war in Flanders was effectively lost.

The Jacobite Rising of 1745

By the autumn of 1745, Cumberland was needed at home. The Jacobite rising under Charles Edward Stuart — Bonnie Prince Charlie — had swept through Scotland, captured Edinburgh, and routed General Sir John Cope at Prestonpans. The Jacobite army crossed into England in November, reaching Derby by the first week of December. The government in London panicked; George II prepared to flee; the Bank of England suffered a run.

Cumberland was recalled from Flanders to take command of the forces in England. He assembled an army of 12,000 men — the largest British field army ever deployed on home soil — and prepared to confront the invaders. But the Jacobites, fatally hesitant, turned back at Derby on 6 December. Cumberland pursued, skirmishing with the rearguard at Clifton Moor, but the main Jacobite force escaped back into Scotland, bloodied but intact.

In January 1746, Cumberland was given supreme command in Scotland. He moved north through the snow and ice of a bitter winter, his army supplied by sea, his line of communication secured by the Royal Navy. On the way, his second-in-command, General Hawley, was defeated at Falkirk Muir on 17 January — a humiliating reverse that delayed Cumberland’s advance and forced him to reorganise the army’s command structure.

Culloden (16 April 1746)

The two armies met on Culloden Moor, east of Inverness, on the morning of 16 April 1746. Cumberland had about 9,000 men — regulars, dragoons, and artillery — deployed across the bleak, marshy moorland. The Jacobites had perhaps 5,000 exhausted, starving Highlanders, who had spent the night in a failed attempt to surprise Cumberland’s camp at Nairn.

Cumberland’s tactical dispositions were a deliberate response to Prestonpans and Falkirk. He had trained his troops to meet the Highland charge — the terrible rush of claymore-wielding clansmen that had broken Cope’s and Hawley’s armies. His men were ordered to hold their fire until the Highlanders were within thirty yards, and then to aim not at the front rank but at the men behind — to disrupt the momentum of the charge by killing the men who pressed the front ranks forward. His left flank was anchored on the park wall of Culloden House, protected by Wolfe’s regiment; his right was secured by a battalion of dragoons in reserve.

The battle was over in less than an hour. The Jacobite charge, launched across boggy ground under a storm of grapeshot and musket fire, was broken before it reached the British line. The few Highlanders who got through were cut down by the bayonets of Cumberland’s second line. The clans broke, and Cumberland unleashed his dragoons. The pursuit was merciless. Prisoners were shot on the field or hunted down in the heather. By midday, the Jacobite army had ceased to exist.

The Pacification of the Highlands

What followed defined Cumberland’s historical reputation. For the next three months, he conducted a systematic programme of suppression in the Highlands, aimed at ensuring that no Jacobite rebellion could ever rise again.

The orders he issued were brutal and precise. Every clansman who had borne arms — or was suspected of having borne arms — was to be executed on capture. The families of rebels were to be evicted. Cattle were to be driven off. Crops were to be burned. The Gaelic language, the tartan, the bagpipes — the entire fabric of Highland culture — were proscribed by law.

The severity was deliberate. Cumberland’s instructions, issued from Fort Augustus on 23 May 1746, called for the “punishment of all persons who have been in any way concerned in the rebellion.” The army swept through the glens, burning and hanging. The exact death toll is uncertain, but it ran into thousands — killed in battle, executed in the aftermath, or dead from starvation and exposure as their homes and crops were destroyed. The prisoners were dealt with harshly: 120 Jacobite officers were executed at Carlisle, York, and Kennington Common; hundreds more were transported to the colonies.

The nickname “Butcher Cumberland” was born in these months. It was coined by the Jacobite propagandists — the name stuck, and it has never been shaken. Even those who acknowledged the political necessity of the suppression were uneasy at its ferocity. The Duke of Newcastle, the Secretary of State, wrote to Cumberland praising his “vigour and prudence” while delicately suggesting moderation. Cumberland ignored the suggestion.

The Convention of Klosterzeven (1757)

Cumberland’s final military command came in the early phase of the Seven Years’ War. In 1757, he was appointed to command the Army of Observation in Hanover — German troops in British pay, charged with defending the Electorate of Hanover from the French. The campaign was a disaster from the start.

The French army under Marshal d’Estrées advanced in overwhelming strength. Cumberland fell back across the Weser, fighting a rearguard action at Hastenbeck on 26 July 1757 in which the British-Hanoverian forces, though outnumbered, gave a creditable account of themselves. But Cumberland’s nerve, or his judgment, failed him. He retreated northward, the French pressing on his heels, and by early September he was cornered at the mouth of the Elbe, his army disintegrating, his supply lines cut.

On 8 September 1757, without consulting London, Cumberland signed the Convention of Klosterzeven with the French commander, the Duc de Richelieu. The terms were humiliating: the Army of Observation was to be disbanded; Hanover was to be neutralised and occupied by the French; and Cumberland’s troops were to go into cantonments across the Elbe. The Convention effectively surrendered the King’s beloved Electorate without a fight.

When the news reached London, the reaction was furious. William Pitt the Elder — who had just taken control of the war — denounced the Convention as a betrayal. George II, mortified and enraged, repudiated his son’s agreement. Cumberland was recalled to England, stripped of his military commands, and retired to private life. He never held a military command again.

Final Years

Cumberland spent his remaining years in embittered retirement. He was still a figure of public importance — he served for a time as Ranger of Windsor Great Park and engaged in political manoeuvring against Pitt’s government — but his military career was finished. George II’s death in 1760 ended whatever residual influence he might have claimed through his father’s favour.

He died suddenly on 31 October 1765 at his house in Upper Grosvenor Street, London, of a suspected heart attack. He was forty-four years old. His body was buried in the Hanoverian vault in Westminster Abbey, close to the father whose battle he had won and whose Electorate he had lost.

Character and Legacy

Cumberland was a man of paradoxes. He was a devoted and loving son to George II, who wept when his military career ended. He was an able administrator who reformed the British army’s logistics and training — the tactical system that Wolfe used at Quebec was, in significant part, the system Cumberland had built. He was physically brave — wounded at Dettingen, two horses shot under him at Fontenoy — and he was a genuinely competent general who had the misfortune to meet Saxe at his best and the Jacobites at their most desperate.

Yet his legacy is the blood of Culloden and the cleared glens. The “Butcher” label was born of Jacobite propaganda, but it was earned. The systematic pacification of the Highlands — the destruction of the clan system, the suppression of Highland culture, the executions and transportations — was a deliberate policy of state terror, and Cumberland was its instrument. Even as the British army perfected the methods that would conquer an empire, the memory of Culloden stood as a warning — and a stain.

His monument in Westminster Abbey commemorates a son of the king who saved the kingdom. The name on the lips of Scottish mothers was less kind.

“Damn the rebels — they now fly before my army as chaff before the wind.”
