George Brydges Rodney, 1st Baron Rodney (1718–1792)

George Brydges Rodney was the most flamboyant and controversial British admiral of the American Revolutionary War — a commander of extraordinary dash whose victory at the Battle of the Saintes saved the British West Indies and introduced the tactic of breaking the line to fleet action, yet whose career was marred by accusations of greed, favouritism, and a fixation on prize money that distracted him from the greater strategic game.

Early Career and the Seven Years’ War

Rodney entered the navy as a boy and was commissioned lieutenant in 1739. By 1742 he had risen to post-captain, commanding the Sheerness and later the Eagle. His first great service came during the Seven Years’ War: in 1759 he bombarded Le Havre, destroying the flat-bottomed invasion boats the French had assembled for a descent on England. In 1762 he captured Martinique from the French, a conquest of the richest sugar island in the Caribbean, and was rewarded with the governorship of Greenwich Hospital.

Between wars, Rodney’s financial indiscipline caught up with him. He lived extravagantly, ran up debts, and fled England in 1774 to live in Paris to escape his creditors. He remained there until the outbreak of the American Revolutionary War, when his debt was compounded by his inability to pay and his absence from English politics had cost him his Greenwich post. Yet the Admiralty, desperate for experienced commanders, recalled him.

The Relief of Gibraltar and the Moonlight Battle

At the beginning of 1780, the two great objects of British naval strategy were the relief of Gibraltar (besieged by the Spanish since 1779) and the defence of the West Indies. Rodney was appointed to the chief command in the latter theatre, but his orders required him to relieve Gibraltar on the way. He sailed from Plymouth on the 29th of December, 1779, with twenty-two ships of the line.

On the 8th of January, 1780, he fell in with a Spanish convoy of twenty-two sail carrying naval stores from San Sebastián to Cadiz; he captured the greater part, including vast quantities of wheat and flour destined for the Spanish fleet. Then, on the night of the 16th of January, off Cape St. Vincent, Rodney encountered a Spanish squadron of eleven ships of the line under Don Juan Langara. In what became known as the Moonlight Battle — fought through the darkness of a winter night — he overwhelmed the Spaniards, capturing four ships including Langara’s own Phoenix of eighty guns. The engagement did not end until two in the morning, when the Monarca, the headmost of the enemy’s fleet, struck to Rodney’s Sandwich. Two more Spanish ships had struck but escaped in the darkness. The victory sent a shock through the courts of Europe: a night action at sea, fought in a winter gale, was almost unheard of.

Rodney then proceeded to Gibraltar, where he landed the captured provisions and stores, relieving the garrison after more than a year of siege. He left a detachment under Admiral Digby to escort the prizes home and continued on to the West Indies.

The West Indies and the Frustration with de Guichen

Rodney arrived at St. Lucia on the 27th of March, 1780, to find the situation less dire than he had feared. The French fleet under the Count de Guichen lay in Fort Royal Bay, Martinique. Rodney sailed on the 2nd of April to provoke an engagement; de Guichen declined. On the 15th, the French slipped out under cover of darkness with twenty-three sail of the line. Rodney pursued and brought them to action on the 17th off Martinique.

The battle was brilliantly conceived but poorly executed by his subordinates. Rodney, in the Sandwich, engaged the French centre alone for an hour and a half, but the van and rear of the British fleet were too far distant to support him. He had anticipated a decisive victory; he obtained only a partial success. De Guichen escaped to Guadeloupe, and Rodney, frustrated by the failure of his captains to support him, returned to St. Lucia. The campaign of 1780 yielded no decisive result.

The Capture of St. Eustatius

In February 1781, with the rupture with Holland, Rodney captured the Dutch island of St. Eustatius, the great entrepôt of the Caribbean — a depot through which the Americans, French, and Dutch had traded throughout the war. The prize was enormous: warehouses crammed with merchandise, a convoy of thirty merchant ships in the harbour, and vast sums of cash. The total was estimated at £3–4 million.

But the capture became a scandal. Rodney lingered on St. Eustatius for weeks, personally superintending the sale of the captured property and settling the claims of British merchants, while the strategic situation in the West Indies deteriorated. He detached Sir Samuel Hood with a squadron to watch the French at Martinique, but Hood was too weak to intercept the French fleet under de Grasse when it sailed for the Chesapeake in the summer of 1781 — a failure that contributed directly to the catastrophe at Yorktown. Rodney was accused of sacrificing the war for the sake of prize money. The island was recaptured by the French in November 1781.

The Battle of the Saintes

Rodney’s redemption came on the 12th of April, 1782. By this time, de Grasse had sailed from Martinique with thirty-four sail of the line, intending to join the Spanish fleet and capture Jamaica — the richest remaining British possession in the Caribbean. Rodney, with thirty-six sail, intercepted him off the Îles des Saintes, a cluster of rocks between Guadeloupe and Dominica.

The two fleets engaged at seven in the morning. For hours the battle hung in the balance. Then, at half-past eleven, with the wind shifting to the south-east, Rodney seized the moment. In the Formidable, he bore down and broke through the French line — a manoeuvre that scattered de Grasse’s fleet beyond hope of recovery. The French admiral’s flagship, the Ville de Paris of 110 guns, was surrounded and forced to strike. Four other French ships of the line were taken. De Grasse himself was brought aboard the Formidable, a prisoner.

The victory at the Battle of the Saintes saved Jamaica, restored British naval prestige, and secured for Rodney a barony — he was created Baron Rodney of Rodney Stoke, Somerset. Yet the battle also triggered a long controversy. Several years afterward, John Clerk of Eldin, a Scottish gentleman of scientific pursuits, published a work on naval tactics claiming that the manoeuvre of breaking the line was his own invention, and that he had communicated it to Rodney through a common friend before the battle. The Admiralty investigated and declined to reward Clerk, noting that the tactic had been employed in earlier engagements — but the debate over who deserved credit for the Saintes has never entirely subsided.

Later Life and Legacy

Rodney returned to England in 1783 to a hero’s welcome. He was voted a pension of £2,000 per year by Parliament. But his health was broken, and his financial affairs remained disordered. He withdrew from active service and lived quietly on his estate in Somerset until his death on the 24th of May, 1792.

Rodney was, by all accounts, a man of extraordinary personal courage and tactical genius — the first admiral to deliberately break an enemy’s line in a major fleet action. His victory at the Saintes ranks with Edward Hawke’s triumph at Quiberon Bay and George Anson’s at Finisterre as one of the decisive fleet actions of the 18th century. Yet his legacy is shadowed by the St. Eustatius affair, the lingering suspicion that he valued wealth over duty, and the sense that his greatest victory owed as much to a shift of wind as to design. He was buried in the family vault at Rodney Stoke.
