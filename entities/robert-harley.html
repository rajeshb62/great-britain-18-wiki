Robert Harley, 1st Earl of Oxford

Robert Harley, 1st Earl of Oxford and Mortimer (5 December 1661 – 21 May 1724), was a British Tory statesman who dominated the government of Queen Anne as Lord Treasurer from 1711 to 1714. He was the architect of the peace policy that ended the War of the Spanish Succession with the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), and a master of parliamentary management whose cautious, temporising style contrasted sharply with the brilliance of his rival Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke. His later involvement with the South Sea Company — the speculative catastrophe in which he was deeply entangled — completed a career that swung from the heights of power to the depths of imprisonment and public disgrace.

Early Life and Education

Born in London, Harley was the eldest son of Sir Edward Harley, a Parliamentarian who had fought for the Parliamentary cause in the Civil War but sided with the moderate Presbyterians against the Cromwellian regime. The family was a noted centre of Puritan Nonconformity and scholarship. Robert was educated at a private school in Shilton, Oxfordshire, and at Christ Church, Oxford, where he developed an interest in law, history, and antiquarian studies that would make him one of the great manuscript collectors of the age.

Speaker of the House of Commons (1701–1705)

Harley entered Parliament in 1689 as a member for Tregony, later representing Radnor. He was a Country Whig by inclination — suspicious of standing armies, placement, and court corruption — but his instincts were managerial rather than ideological. He spoke rarely but worked tirelessly behind the scenes, building networks of patronage and correspondence that would make him the most formidable parliamentary manager of his generation.

In February 1701, Harley was elected Speaker of the House of Commons, a position he held for four years. As Speaker, he cultivated an air of judicious impartiality while quietly building a following of MPs loyal to him personally rather than to the Whig junto or the Court. His great innovation was the creation of a “middle party” — a parliamentary faction drawn from moderate Tories and Country Whigs that held the balance of power between the party extremes. He corresponded ceaselessly, collected intelligence from every corner of the kingdom, and knew the temper of the House better than any man alive.

He was also Secretary of State for the Northern Department from 1704 to 1708, serving alongside the Duke of Marlborough and Lord Godolphin during the great victories of Blenheim, Ramillies, and Oudenarde. But his relations with the Whig junto deteriorated as the war dragged on. The Whigs demanded more offices, more influence, and a more aggressive prosecution of the war. Harley, who had always been a peace man at heart, grew increasingly estranged.

In February 1708, Harley was outmanoeuvred by the Whigs. A scandal involving a clerk in his office named Gregg — who had been selling secrets to the French — was used to drive him from office. Godolphin and Marlborough refused to serve with him; the Queen, reluctantly, dismissed him. He went into opposition, nursing his grievance and planning his return.

The Harley Ministry (1710–1714)

The Sacheverell trial of 1710 destroyed the Whig ministry and handed Harley his revenge. When the Queen dismissed Lord Sunderland in June 1710 and dissolved Parliament, it was Harley she turned to — the man who had always been her favourite minister. He was appointed Chancellor of the Exchequer in August 1710 and was the effective head of the new Tory government.

Harley’s first task was to manage the general election of 1710, which returned a huge Tory majority. His second was to negotiate peace with France — a task he undertook with characteristic secrecy and caution. He established a secret backchannel to France through the Abbé Gaultier, a French priest living in London, and through the merchant Matthew Prior. The preliminaries of peace were signed in September 1711, but the negotiations had to be conducted in hiding from Britain’s allies — the Dutch, the Austrians, and the Emperor — who were not yet ready to abandon the war.

The creation of twelve new Tory peers in December 1711 — the largest single creation of peers in a generation — was necessary to force the peace through the Whig-dominated House of Lords. Harley was created Earl of Oxford and Mortimer in May 1711 and appointed Lord High Treasurer, placing him at the summit of the administration.

His greatest legislative achievement was the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), which ended eleven years of war and gave Britain Gibraltar, Minorca, Newfoundland, Hudson’s Bay, and the Asiento. But the peace was deeply unpopular with the allies, and the manner of its making — the secret diplomacy, the accusations of bribery — left a stain on Harley’s reputation that never washed off.

The Breach with Bolingbroke

The Harley-Bolingbroke ministry was, from the start, an alliance of opposites. Harley was cautious, secretive, and managerial — the great “trimmer” who sought to keep the Tory party together by balancing Jacobites against Hanoverians, High Church against moderate, country against court. Bolingbroke was brilliant, impatient, and overreaching — the demagogue who wanted to lead the Tory party into a new era of High Church, royalist, and potentially Jacobite politics.

Their rivalry became an open feud in 1713–1714. Bolingbroke outmanoeuvred Harley at court, winning the Queen’s confidence for the peace negotiations and undermining Harley’s position with the Tory backbenchers. Harley, for his part, resisted Bolingbroke’s Jacobite intrigues and tried to keep the ministry on a course that would secure the Hanoverian succession. The paralysis of the ministry in the final year of Queen Anne’s reign — the “sick ministry” that stumbled from crisis to crisis — was directly attributable to the personal hatred between the two men.

Harley’s caution was not timidity but calculation. He understood that the Jacobite game was a lost cause — that the Protestant succession was secure, that France was exhausted, and that the Tory party would destroy itself if it tied its fortunes to the Pretender. He was right. When Anne died on 1 August 1714, Harley’s careful management collapsed. The Whigs seized the Privy Council; George I was proclaimed without opposition; the Tories were proscribed.

Impeachment and Imprisonment

The accession of George I marked the ruin of Harley’s political career. The new King distrusted him as a Tory and a peacemaker. Whig revenge was swift and merciless. In 1715, a secret committee of the House of Commons voted to impeach Harley, Bolingbroke, and the Duke of Ormond for high treason — the charge being that they had betrayed the allies by making a separate peace, that they had corresponded with the Pretender, and that they had subverted the Protestant succession.

Bolingbroke fled to France. Harley stayed and faced his accusers. He was committed to the Tower of London, where he remained for two years. His imprisonment was not harsh — he was allowed books, papers, and visitors — but the political and financial strain was immense. The parliamentary inquiry into his conduct dragged on, the evidence was inconclusive, and the Whigs began to lose enthusiasm for a prosecution that threatened to expose their own wartime profiteering.

In July 1717, after a change in the political climate, the charges against Harley were dropped. He was released from the Tower but never again held office. His health was broken, his reputation tarnished, and his finances in ruins.

The South Sea Company and Final Years

Harley’s involvement with the South Sea Company was the final act of his public life. He had been one of the original architects of the Company in 1711 — it was his scheme, devised during his ministry, to fund the national debt by converting government tallies into South Sea stock, thereby creating a permanent fund of creditors with a stake in the government’s credit. The scheme had been brilliant in conception: it gave the government access to cheap capital, gave the Company a monopoly on trade to the South Seas, and gave the investing public a stake in the national finances.

But by 1720, the South Sea system had metastasised into a speculative frenzy of unprecedented proportions. Harley, now in retirement, was drawn back into the Company’s orbit. He accepted a position as a director of the South Sea Company — a decision that would prove catastrophic. When the bubble burst in September 1720, shares plummeting from £1,000 to £150 in a matter of weeks, the parliamentary inquiry into the Company’s affairs turned its attention to the directors. Harley was investigated, his papers seized, and he was found to have been deeply involved in the corrupt distribution of fictitious stock to members of the government and Parliament.

He was expelled from the House of Commons (where he sat as MP for Radnor) and his estate was sequestered. The South Sea disaster completed the destruction of his reputation. Smollett records that the public, already disposed to hate the Tories who had made the peace, now reviled Harley as a “stock-jobbing knave” who had plundered the nation twice — once through the Utrecht settlement and once through the South Sea scheme.

He died on 21 May 1724, aged sixty-two, at his house in Albemarle Street, London — a ruined man. His vast collection of manuscripts — the Harleian Library — was later acquired by the British Museum and remains one of the great collections of English historical documents.

Character and Assessment

Harley was the most skilful parliamentary manager of his age, a man who understood the mechanics of power better than anyone except perhaps Robert Walpole. He was not a great orator — his speeches were dry, legalistic, and laboured — but he was a master of the quiet arts of politics: correspondence, patronage, intelligence-gathering, and the slow construction of a dependable majority.

Smollett, who wrote within living memory of Harley’s career, described him as “a man of moderate abilities, but of great industry, penetration, and address.” He was cautious to the point of paralysis — “a procrastinator,” in the judgment of more recent historians, “who mistook delay for strategy.” His great achievement was the Peace of Utrecht; his great failure was his inability to manage Bolingbroke or to hold the Tory party together long enough to secure a moderate succession.

The Harleian Library — his collection of over 7,000 manuscripts, including the Harleian Charters, the Harleian Apocalypse, and the Codex Harleianus — stands as the enduring monument to his intellect and his love of learning. In this, as in so much else, he was the opposite of Bolingbroke: the scholar-politician who collected books instead of writing them, who built systems instead of inspiring devotion, and who ended his life not in exile but in disgrace, his name forever linked to the South Sea calamity.

“He was a man of great industry, and of a clear judgment; but his temper was so unequal, and his conduct so irresolute, that he often lost the fruits of his labour.” — Tobias Smollett
