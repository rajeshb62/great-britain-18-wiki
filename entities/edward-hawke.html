Edward Hawke, 1st Baron Hawke (1705–1781)

Edward Hawke rose from modest beginnings to become the most feared British admiral of his generation — a man whose relentless blockade of Brest and climactic victory at the Battle of Quiberon Bay shattered French naval power and ended the threat of invasion in the Seven Years’ War. His father, a barrister of no great means, once told the young Hawke that the best he might hope for was to rise to the rank of captain — a prediction the son far surpassed, reaching the very pinnacle of his profession.

Early Career and Toulon

Hawke entered the navy in his youth and received his first command, the sloop Flamborough, in 1734. He subsequently commanded the Wolf and, in 1743, the Berwick of seventy guns. His opportunity for distinction came on the 11th of February, 1744, off Toulon, in an action as politically consequential as it was tactically muddled. The British fleet under Thomas Mathews and Richard Lestock engaged a combined Franco-Spanish force that had emerged from the port. The battle fell into confusion — Lestock held back, Mathews charged ahead unsupported, and the fleet’s discipline collapsed. Amid this fiasco, Hawke in the Berwick fought with singular determination and captured the Spanish seventy-gun ship Poder — the only prize taken in the entire engagement. The feat did not go unnoticed: King George II took a personal interest in Hawke’s career, a patronage that would prove decisive.

Cape Finisterre

By 1747 Hawke commanded the Devonshire and led a squadron of fourteen ships on a mission to intercept French commerce in the Bay of Biscay. On the 14th of October, he fell in with a French convoy bound for the West Indies, escorted by nine men-of-war under Admiral de l’Étenduère. Hawke gave the signal to chase, disregarding the formal line of battle. The Devonshire pressed ahead, her guns breaking loose from their tackles in the fury of the action. Three of the six French prizes were taken by Hawke alone, and the victory was announced in a London Gazette Extraordinary on the 27th of October. His dispatch called for a court-martial on the captured French officers (a matter of form) and noted that the Tonnant, his most formidable adversary, had escaped with the Intrepid. For this victory, Hawke was knighted.

The Blockade of Brest and Quiberon Bay

With the accession of William Pitt the Elder to government, British strategy turned to the aggressive containment of France. Hawke was appointed to command the Western Squadron, charged with the relentless blockade of Brest — a service that demanded extraordinary fortitude. Hawke’s mind possessed a remarkable constancy; he was bold without restless irritability, and the firmness of his character enabled him to endure the grim, cheerless duty of the blockade with as much serenity as he enjoyed the heat of battle.

In November 1759, a violent tempest drove Hawke from his station and forced him to shelter in Torbay. The French commander, Monsieur Conflans, seeing the coast clear, put to sea with twenty-one ships of the line on the 14th of November. Hawke, with the same wind, sailed from England in pursuit. On the 16th he was off Ushant, where English transports returning from Quiberon Bay informed him that the French fleet had been seen standing to the south-east.

On the morning of the 20th of November, the Maidstone frigate signalled the enemy. Lord Howe, in the Magnanime, confirmed they were French. Hawke told his officers he would not trouble himself with forming line — he would attack in the old way and make “downright work” with them. He ordered seven ships to chase.

What followed was the most dramatic naval action of the century. A winter gale darkened the heavens; the sea rolled in tremendous waves that dashed themselves into foam on treacherous shoals unknown to British pilots. Into this maelstrom Hawke drove his fleet. Conflans, who had initially seemed willing to offer battle, lost his nerve and stood towards the shore, hoping to lure the English onto the rocks. The Royal George, bearing Hawke’s flag, approached the Soleil Royal with Conflans aboard. When her pilot warned of the breakers and the imminent danger of the shoals, Hawke replied with the most famous words of his career: “You have done your duty in pointing out the danger, but lay me alongside of the Soleil Royal.”

The Superbe, a French seventy-gun ship, interposed to protect her admiral and received the full broadside intended for Conflans. The smoke cleared to reveal only her masts above the water — she had sunk in an instant. The Formidable fought with heroic determination, her commander Admiral de Verger pouring broadside after broadside into the British as they sailed past, but she struck her colours at four o’clock, every officer killed. The Héros and Thésée sank like the Superbe. Only darkness saved the remnants of the French fleet: seven ships of the line threw their guns overboard and fled into the river Vilaine; a shattered handful escaped to other ports. Of twenty-one French ships, only three survived as effective fighting units.

The victory ended all talk of French invasion. Voltaire himself acknowledged that there were natural circumstances giving an inherent superiority to the English marine over that of France.

First Lord of the Admiralty

After the peace of 1763, Hawke retired to private life — a man of unusual intellectual tastes, who found in reading the same calm satisfaction that others found in society. On the 5th of November, 1765, he was promoted to Vice-Admiral of Great Britain, and on the 2nd of December, 1766, appointed First Lord of the Admiralty. His administration oversaw the fitting out of James Cook’s voyages of discovery, though the design and merit of those expeditions belonged properly to the king. He resigned the post on the 9th of January, 1771, for reasons that remain unclear — a man never regarded as a party man, motivated by private duty rather than faction.

Peerage and Death

On the 20th of May, 1776, George III advanced him to the peerage as Baron Hawke of Towton, in the County of York. He thereafter took no part in public life. His wife had died during his absence in the Mediterranean (where he had gone to supersede the unfortunate John Byng), leaving him four children; only his son Martin Bladen, the second Baron, survived him. Hawke died on the 17th of October, 1781.

In person, Hawke was above the ordinary stature of his countrymen, with a frame of uniform compactness that suggested the complete subordination of the body to the mind. His expression was more respectable than agreeable — a tincture of severity in his character repressed familiarity, though his disposition was neither haughty nor passionate. His forehead was tall and somewhat square, indicating quickness of apprehension and firmness of resolution. The severity showed most in his eyes; the rest of his features promised frank inclinations and a disposition to jocularity, perhaps tinged with satire.

His legacy rests on two pillars: the blockade of Brest, an innovation in naval warfare that kept the French fleet penned and impotent while British expeditions ranged the globe, and the victory at Quiberon Bay, the most complete fleet action of the age, fought in conditions that would have driven any lesser commander to safety.
