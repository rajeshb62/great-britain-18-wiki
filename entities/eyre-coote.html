Sir Eyre Coote (1726–1783)

Sir Eyre Coote (1726–1783) was the most capable British Army officer to serve in India in the generation after Robert Clive — a commander whose reputation for tactical skill, personal courage, and relentless energy earned him the contemporary judgement as the “ablest British officer in India.” His two decisive battles — Wandiwash (1760), which shattered French power in the Carnatic, and Porto Novo (1781), where he broke the juggernaut of Hyder Ali’s invading army — saved the East India Company from destruction at two critical junctures. He served as Commander-in-Chief in India from 1779 until his death.

The Carnatic Wars and Wandiwash

Coote arrived in India in 1754 as a captain in the Company’s service. He first attracted notice in the campaigns of the Carnatic, serving alongside Clive in the operations that followed the defence of Arcot. But his moment arrived when the Seven Years’ War in Europe extended to India, and the French under the Comte de Lally mounted a desperate bid to reclaim the Company’s possessions on the Coromandel Coast.

Lally, a fiery Irish-French commander of considerable ability, had captured Madras in 1758—though he failed to take Fort St. George—and now commanded the field. The British appointed Coote, by then a colonel, to oppose him. The two armies met on the plains of Wandiwash on the morning of 22 January 1760.

The battle was the decisive engagement of the Third Carnatic War. Coote had about 1,900 European infantry, 2,100 sepoys, and twenty-six field guns against Lally’s roughly equal force of French regulars, Swiss mercenaries, and Indian troops. The French cavalry, superior in both numbers and quality, forced Coote to form his infantry in a defensive square, a formation from which he directed his artillery with devastating effect. The critical moment came when Coote, detecting a gap in the French line caused by Lally’s mishandling of his reserves, launched a concentrated assault on the French centre. The French regulars—the Compagnies Franches de la Marine and the Swiss regiments—were broken by the British volleys and the bayonet charge that followed. Lally’s sepoy battalions, seeing the European infantry routed, abandoned the field.

The victory was total. Lally retreated to Pondicherry, where he would hold out for a year before surrendering. Wandiwash destroyed French military power in India. Pondicherry itself fell on 16 January 1761, razed to the ground by the Company’s engineers. Coote was rewarded with a knighthood and the rank of lieutenant-colonel in the King’s army. He returned to England covered in glory, the man who had finished what Clive had begun.

Return to India and the War against Hyder Ali

Coote served in England and Ireland for nearly two decades—as a Member of Parliament and sometime governor of Fort William—but his finest hour was yet to come. In 1779, with the East India Company facing the most serious crisis in its history, he was appointed Commander-in-Chief in India and returned to the subcontinent.

The crisis was Hyder Ali, the Sultan of Mysore. Hyder had spent two decades building the most formidable military machine in Indian history—a hybrid army armed with French muskets and drilled by French officers, supported by excellent cavalry and a sophisticated rocket corps. In July 1780, he burst into the Carnatic with an army of 90,000 men, burning villages, slaughtering resistance, and advancing within sight of Madras. On 10 September 1780, he overwhelmed and annihilated a detached British column under Colonel Baillie near Conjeveram—nearly 3,000 men cut to pieces or taken prisoner. Arcot fell; the Company’s entire position in southern India teetered on the edge of extinction.

“The Government at Madras,” wrote Belsham, “scarcely believed itself to be in safety, when Sir Eyre Coote arrived to take the command of the Company’s forces on the coast of Coromandel.”

Porto Novo (1781)

Coote found a broken army and a terrified presidency. The troops were demoralised, the commissariat destroyed, the treasury empty. He spent the winter of 1780–1781 rebuilding the army’s spirit, drilling raw recruits, and assembling the supplies needed for a campaign. By June 1781, he was ready to march.

Hyder Ali, whose intelligence was excellent, knew Coote was coming and chose the ground. On the 1st of July 1781, the two armies met at Porto Novo (modern Parangipettai), on the coast sixty miles south of Madras. Hyder had selected a position of immense strength: his army of perhaps 70,000 men—including 10,000 regular infantry trained and armed by the French—was drawn up in a crescent between the sea and a chain of salt-water lagoons, the British approach channeled into a narrow corridor of firm ground.

Coote had barely 8,000 men. He formed them into a single line of battle, extended to prevent the overwhelming Mysorean cavalry from turning his flanks, and advanced steadily under the guns of the British frigates standing close inshore. The battle became a grinding infantry fire-fight in the stifling heat. Three times the Mysorean regulars attempted to break the British line; three times they were driven back by volley-fire and the bayonet. Coote himself, despite his fifty-five years and fragile health—he suffered constantly from the effects of the Indian climate—was everywhere on the field, exposing himself to enemy fire with a reckless disregard that inspired his men.

By late afternoon, Hyder had had enough. He drew off, leaving the field to Coote. The victory was not annihilating in its immediate casualties—Hyder lost perhaps 5,000 men—but it was strategically decisive. It saved Madras, broke the spell of Hyder’s invincibility, and began the long process of rolling the Mysorean army back into its own territories. Belsham records that Hyder was, in the engagements that followed, “in repeated engagements foiled and defeated by this fortunate and gallant veteran.”

Coote followed Porto Novo with two further victories at Pollilur (27 August 1781) and Sholinghur (18 September 1781), each fought against the same indomitable but ultimately outmatched enemy. The three battles together—the only occasions in the entire war where a British army met Hyder in open battle and defeated him—confirmed Coote’s reputation.

Death and Legacy

Coote returned to Bengal exhausted and ill. The climate had destroyed his health; he suffered from bouts of fever and the effects of an old wound. He died on 28 April 1783, at the age of fifty-seven, while still serving as Commander-in-Chief.

The East India Company erected a monument to him in Westminster Abbey. Fort Saint George preserves his memory; a statue of him stands in the Madras Presidency still. His strategic achievement is often overshadowed by Clive and by the later victories of Wellesley, but it was Coote, more than any other soldier in the generation between Clive and the Marquess Wellesley, who held the British empire in India together in its hour of greatest peril. The judgement of his contemporaries—that he was the ablest British officer in India—has never been seriously challenged.

Key Battles

  • Battle of Wandiwash (1760) — defeated the Comte de Lally, destroyed French power in India
  • Porto Novo (1781) — broke Hyder Ali’s invasion of the Carnatic, saved Madras
  • Pollilur (1781) — second victory over Hyder Ali in the same campaign
  • Sholinghur (1781) — third victory, concluding the 1781 campaign
