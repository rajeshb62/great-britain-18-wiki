Bank of England

The Bank of England — the Governor and Company of the Bank of England — was founded in 1694 as a wartime expedient: a private corporation formed to lend money to the government in exchange for the right to issue banknotes and manage the national debt. Within a century, it had become the indispensable institution of British public finance — the pivot around which the entire system of government borrowing, commercial credit, and fiscal-military power revolved. Its creation marked the beginning of the “Financial Revolution” that made eighteenth-century Britain the most formidable fiscal-military state in Europe.

Foundation: The Financial Revolution (1694)

The Bank was the brainchild of William Paterson, a Scottish merchant, and Charles Montagu, 1st Earl of Halifax, the Whig Chancellor of the Exchequer. The occasion was the desperate financial straits of William III’s government, which after six years of war against Louis XIV had exhausted all the expedients of traditional taxation. The government’s credit was exhausted; the goldsmith-bankers who had previously advanced money could raise no more; and the army and navy were mutinous for want of pay.

The scheme was simple in conception but revolutionary in its implications. Subscribers would lend the government £1,200,000 at 8% interest, secured by permanent taxes on shipping, beer, and other commodities. In return, they would be incorporated as “The Governor and Company of the Bank of England,” with the right to accept deposits, issue notes, and deal in bills of exchange and gold. The charter was granted by Act of Parliament on 25 April 1694; the books were opened at Mercer’s Hall in June; and within twelve days the entire subscription was filled.

The Bank’s notes — initially handwritten receipts for deposits, but soon printed in standard denominations — circulated as a form of paper money more reliable than the tallies and exchequer orders that had previously served as the government’s currency. The Bank was also authorised to deal in gold and silver bullion and to discount commercial bills, functions that made it the centre of London’s nascent money market.

Burnet, the Whig historian, records the controversy that surrounded the Bank’s foundation. The Tories denounced it as a Whig project to entrench a moneyed interest that would corrupt the landed constitution. The goldsmith-bankers — whose business of issuing private notes was directly threatened — fought the scheme with every weapon at their disposal. But the government’s need was too urgent, and the Bank’s subscription too successful, for the opposition to prevail.

The Bank and the South Sea Crisis (1720)

The Bank’s greatest test in its early decades came with the South Sea Company crisis of 1720. The South Sea scheme — a massive conversion of the national debt into equity of the South Sea Company — depended on the Bank’s cooperation. When the South Sea Bubble burst in September 1720, shares falling from over £1,000 to under £150 in a matter of weeks, the Bank was drawn into the calamity.

Smollett describes the scene in vivid terms. The directors of the Bank, “overwhelmed with consternation,” were pressed by the ministry to support the South Sea Company’s credit. They agreed reluctantly to subscribe £3,500,000 into the South Sea stock at 400% — an arrangement that plunged the Bank into the general ruin as the stock continued to fall. The run on the Bank that followed was so severe that “the money was paid away faster than it could be received from the subscription.” The Bank was forced to renounce its agreement with the South Sea Company, escaping disaster but earning the lasting enmity of the South Sea directors and their allies.

Robert Walpole’s resolution of the crisis — the famous “Ingraftment” scheme of 1721, by which £9 million of South Sea stock was transferred to the Bank of England and the East India Company — restored public credit and demonstrated the Bank’s indispensability. The Bank’s role in the rescue was a landmark in its constitutional evolution: it had been called upon not merely as a lender to the government but as the guarantor of the entire system of public credit.

The Sinking Fund and the Reduction of Interest (1717–1750)

One of the most significant developments in the Bank’s eighteenth-century history was its involvement in the management of the Sinking Fund, established in 1717 by Robert Walpole and James Stanhope — though its intellectual origins lay with the economist Richard Price and, earlier, with the schemes of Robert Harley.

The Sinking Fund was created by applying the surplus revenues of the “general fund” — the aggregated taxes that had been pledged to service the national debt — to the systematic reduction of the principal. As Smollett records, the Bank’s charter renewal of 1717 was the occasion for a comprehensive reorganisation of the public debt. The Bank accepted a reduction in the interest rate on its loan from 6% to 5%, and undertook to circulate the remaining exchequer bills at 3%. The savings generated by these reductions were directed into the Sinking Fund.

The fund was a mixed success in practice. Walpole himself raided it repeatedly for current expenditure during his long ministry. But the principle — that a portion of the national revenue should be permanently dedicated to debt reduction — became a fixture of British fiscal orthodoxy, and the Bank’s role as the manager of the fund made it the permanent overseer of the nation’s balance sheet.

The Bank and War Finance

The eighteenth century was a period of almost continuous warfare — the War of the Spanish Succession, the War of the Austrian Succession, the Seven Years’ War, the American War of Independence, and the wars of the French Revolution. Each war drove the national debt higher, and each war increased the Bank’s importance to the state.

The Bank’s principal function in wartime was to manage the government’s borrowing. When Parliament voted supply, the Treasury issued exchequer bills — short-term interest-bearing instruments — which the Bank circulated at a discount, providing the government with ready cash while the long-term loans (the perpetual annuities known as “consols”) were being subscribed. The Bank’s management of the exchequer-bill system was the lubricant that kept the fiscal-military machine running.

The national debt grew from approximately £1 million at the Bank’s foundation to over £240 million by the end of the American War in 1783 — an increase unprecedented in European history. The Bank’s ability to market this debt to an ever-widening circle of investors — domestic and foreign, institutional and individual — was the foundation of Britain’s ability to outspend France in every eighteenth-century conflict. The French, who relied on tax farming and occasional forced loans, envied the British system but could not replicate it; they had no institution comparable to the Bank of England, and no public credit comparable to Britain’s.

The Restriction of Cash Payments (1797)

The Bank’s most dramatic crisis of the century came in 1797, during the wars of the French Revolution. A combination of military reverses, the threat of French invasion, and a run on provincial banks triggered a panic that threatened to drain the Bank’s gold reserves. On 26 February 1797, the government ordered the Bank to suspend specie payments — that is, to stop paying gold in exchange for its notes.

The Restriction — as it was called — was intended as a temporary measure of three weeks. It lasted for twenty-four years, until 1821. During this period, Bank of England notes became inconvertible paper currency — the first experiment in fiat money conducted by a major European state. The Bank’s notes, irredeemable in gold, were made legal tender for all payments, and the entire British monetary system rested on confidence in the Bank’s promises.

The Restriction was a profound constitutional change. The Bank, which had been founded on the principle that its notes were always exchangeable for gold, now became the issuer of a purely nominal currency. The experiment worked — inflation remained moderate, and the war was financed — but it marked the beginning of the modern era of managed paper money and ended forever the Bank’s character as a purely commercial institution.

The Bank and the Government: A Constitutional Relationship

The Bank’s relationship with the government was a source of perennial constitutional controversy. It was a private corporation — owned by its shareholders, managed by a Governor and Court of Directors elected from the merchant community of London. Yet it performed functions that were essentially sovereign: managing the national debt, issuing the national currency, and acting as the government’s banker.

The Whig governments of the eighteenth century generally maintained a close and harmonious relationship with the Bank. The Tories, by contrast, were suspicious of the “monied interest” that the Bank represented; Harley’s South Sea scheme was, in part, an attempt to create a Tory rival to the Whig-dominated Bank. But by mid-century, the Bank had transcended party. It was no longer a Whig institution but a national one — the indispensable mechanism through which the British state financed its wars and administered its revenues.

Walpole, who as First Lord of the Treasury worked more closely with the Bank than any previous minister, understood this dependence better than anyone. His long ascendancy was built on the confidence of the City of London, and his fiscal management — the reduction of the national debt, the stabilisation of the currency, the regular service of the interest — was the foundation of that confidence.

Character and Historical Significance

The Bank of England was the first institution of its kind in the world — a privately owned central bank performing public functions. Its eighteenth-century history established the model that would be followed, with variations, by every other central bank in Europe and America: the Bank of France (1800), the Federal Reserve System (1913), and the modern successors of the Bank of England itself.

Its foundation marked the transition from a fiscal system based on the Crown’s prerogative and the goldsmith-bankers’ credit to a system based on parliamentary security and permanent public debt. This transition — the “Financial Revolution” — was as important as the political Revolution of 1688 that preceded it. Without the Bank, Britain could not have financed its wars against Louis XIV, the Bourbon powers, or revolutionary France. Without the Bank, the national debt — that vast edifice of paper that sustained the eighteenth-century fiscal-military state — would have been inconceivable.

The Bank was, from the first, a creature of Parliament, not of the Crown. Its charter was granted by Act of Parliament; its capital was lent to Parliament; its notes rested on parliamentary security. This constitutional foundation — the subordination of public credit to parliamentary control — was the distinctive achievement of the British Financial Revolution and the secret of the Bank’s enduring success.

“The Bank of England is, in its origin, the child of war and revolution — a product of that necessity which knows no law. But it became, in the course of a century, the most stable institution in the most stable government in Europe.” — adapted from contemporary commentary
